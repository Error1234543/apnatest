JEE MainHindiFormula Sheet
Oxidation Number (ON) Calculation (by Sum Rule)

Symbols: Sum of ON of all atoms in a neutral molecule = 0; sum of ON = net ionic charge in an ion

Unit: Dimensionless (assigned value)

When to use: Used to identify which species is oxidized or reduced in any redox reaction

Conditions: Assumes standard rules (O = −2, H = +1 except hydrides, F = −1 always, alkali metals = +1, alkaline earth = +2) applied before special cases

Common mistake: Students often assign O = −2 in peroxides (O₂²⁻, each O = −1) or superoxides (O₂⁻, each O = −½); also mistake S in thiosulfate where central S ≠ terminal S

n‑Factor of a Redox Species

Symbols: n-factor = total change in oxidation number per formula unit of the substance

Unit: Dimensionless

When to use: Essential for equivalent concept in redox titrations and stoichiometry using normality

Conditions: Must be calculated from the actual balanced redox equation; same species may have different n‑factor in different reactions

Common mistake: Using the n‑factor from a different reaction or assuming KMnO₄ always has n‑factor = 5 (in acidic medium n=5, in neutral/weak alkaline n=3, in strongly alkaline n=1)

Equivalent Concept in Redox: Eq(oxidant) = Eq(reductant)

Symbols: Eq = Normality × Volume(L) = Mass/Molar Mass × n‑factor = Moles × n‑factor

Unit: Equivalents (dimensionless count)

When to use: Quick solution of redox titration problems without writing full balanced equation

Conditions: Valid only when reactants are pure and reaction goes to completion; breaks down if side reactions occur

Common mistake: Applying equivalent concept blindly to mixed oxidant/reductant problems without confirming stoichiometric completeness

Relation Between ΔG and E_cell

Symbols: ΔG = −nFE_cell, where n = moles of electrons transferred, F = Faraday constant (96500 C mol⁻¹), E_cell = cell EMF

Unit: ΔG in J mol⁻¹ (or kJ mol⁻¹); F = 96500 C mol⁻¹ (≈ 96485 C mol⁻¹ exact)

When to use: Determines spontaneity of cell reaction; links thermodynamics with electrochemistry

Conditions: n must match the stoichiometric electron transfer in the balanced cell reaction as written; standard form uses ΔG° = −nFE°_cell

Common mistake: Using wrong n (not cancelling electrons properly) or confusing E°_cell with E_cell at non‑standard conditions

Standard Cell Potential

Symbols: E°_cell = E°_cathode − E°_anode = E°_reduction(right) − E°_reduction(left)

Unit: Volt (V)

When to use: Predicts whether a redox cell reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions (E°_cell > 0 → spontaneous)

Conditions: All species at 1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, 298 K; values are intensive — do NOT multiply by stoichiometric coefficients

Common mistake: Multiplying standard reduction potential by the coefficient when balancing the half‑reaction (E° is independent of stoichiometric factor)

Nernst Equation (General Form)

Symbols: E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q, where R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T = temperature (K), n = electrons transferred, Q = reaction quotient

Unit: E and E° in V; RT/F at 298 K ≈ 0.0257 V

When to use: Calculates cell potential at non‑standard concentrations/pressures

Conditions: Q must be written from the balanced cell reaction; solids and pure liquids have activity = 1

Common mistake: Writing Q backwards (products/reactants instead of reactants/products in the ln term) or forgetting to use activities (concentrations) not amounts

Nernst Equation at 298 K (log₁₀ form)

Symbols: E = E° − (0.0591/n) log₁₀ Q

Unit: E in V; 0.0591 V = (2.303RT/F) at 298 K

When to use: Most common form used in JEE Main calculations; rapid evaluation of concentration dependence

Conditions: Only valid at 298 K; for other temperatures substitute 0.0591 → (2.303RT/F)

Common mistake: Using 0.0591 at temperatures significantly different from 298 K; dropping the n in the denominator

Nernst Equation for a Single Electrode (Mⁿ⁺|M)

Symbols: E = E° − (0.0591/n) log₁₀(1/[Mⁿ⁺]) = E° + (0.0591/n) log₁₀[Mⁿ⁺]

Unit: E in V

When to use: Calculating reduction potential of a metal electrode at any ion concentration

Conditions: Applicable to simple metal–metal ion electrodes; pure solid metal activity = 1

Common mistake: Sign error — writing E = E° − (0.0591/n) log[Mⁿ⁺] instead of plus; confusing reduction potential with oxidation potential

Concentration Cell EMF

Symbols: For M|Mⁿ⁺(C₁) || Mⁿ⁺(C₂)|M: E_cell = (0.0591/n) log₁₀(C₂/C₁) when C₂ > C₁ (dilute → concentrated works as cathode)

Unit: V

When to use: Finding EMF when both half‑cells involve identical electrodes but different ion concentrations

Conditions: E°_cell = 0; spontaneous direction is always dilute → concentrated (ions flow to equalize)

Common mistake: Swapping C₁ and C₂ leading to negative EMF; forgetting that E°_cell = 0 for concentration cells

Equilibrium Constant from E°_cell

Symbols: At equilibrium, E_cell = 0 ⇒ E°_cell = (0.0591/n) log₁₀ K ⇒ log K = (nE°_cell)/0.0591

Unit: K is dimensionless

When to use: Calculating the equilibrium constant of a redox reaction from standard cell potential

Conditions: Valid at 298 K; K here is the thermodynamic equilibrium constant for the overall cell reaction

Common mistake: Using wrong n value or taking antilog incorrectly; confusing K_c with K_p

Ksp from Solubility Cell Data

Symbols: For Ag|Ag⁺(sat. AgCl)||Ag⁺(0.1 M)|Ag: E_cell = (0.0591/1) log(0.1/[Ag⁺]_sat); then K_sp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻] = [Ag⁺]²_sat

Unit: K_sp in mol² L⁻²

When to use: Determining solubility product of sparingly soluble salts using a concentration cell with a saturated solution

Conditions: Temperature = 298 K assumed; activity coefficients ignored (ideal dilute solution)

Common mistake: Forgetting that [Ag⁺] from saturated AgCl equals √K_sp and not multiplying by stoichiometric coefficient

Resistance and Resistivity (Conductance Basics)

Symbols: R = ρ(l/A), where ρ = resistivity (Ω cm), l = length (cm), A = area (cm²); G = 1/R (Siemens or S)

Unit: R in Ω; ρ in Ω cm; G in S (or Ω⁻¹)

When to use: Foundation for understanding how solution conductance depends on cell geometry and solution properties

Conditions: Applicable to uniform cross‑section electrolytic cells; A and l are cell dimensions

Common mistake: Confusing resistivity (ρ, property of material) with resistance (R, depends on geometry); wrong unit conversion between cm and m

Cell Constant

Symbols: G = l/A (cm⁻¹); also G = R × κ, where κ is conductivity

Unit: cm⁻¹

When to use: Relates measured resistance of an electrolytic cell to the conductivity of the solution

Conditions: G* is a fixed geometric property of the conductivity cell; determined using a solution of known κ

Common mistake: Treating cell constant as variable; confusing G* with conductance G

Conductivity (κ) and Equivalent/ Molar Conductivity

Symbols: κ = G × (l/A) = G × G*; Λ_m = κ/C (molar conductivity in S cm² mol⁻¹, C in mol cm⁻³); Λ_eq = κ × 1000/N (N = normality in eq L⁻¹)

Unit: κ in S cm⁻¹; Λ_m in S cm² mol⁻¹; Λ_eq in S cm² eq⁻¹

When to use: Quantifying how well an electrolyte solution conducts electricity; central to Kohlrausch's law and dilution studies

Conditions: Λ_m = κ/C requires C in mol cm⁻³; if C is in mol L⁻¹, use Λ_m = (κ × 1000)/C

Common mistake: Forgetting the ×1000 factor when C is in mol L⁻¹; mixing up Λ_m and Λ_eq (Λ_m = Λ_eq × n‑factor)

Kohlrausch's Law of Independent Migration of Ions

Symbols: Λ°_m( electrolyte) = ν₊ λ°₊ + ν₋ λ°₋, where λ°₊, λ°₋ are limiting ionic conductivities and ν₊, ν₋ are ion counts per formula unit

Unit: Λ°_m in S cm² mol⁻¹

When to use: Calculating Λ°_m of weak/strong electrolytes from individual ionic contributions; finding solubility products; determining degree of dissociation α = Λ_m/Λ°_m

Conditions: Valid at infinite dilution where inter‑ionic interactions vanish; λ° values are tabulated

Common mistake: Not multiplying λ° by the stoichiometric number of ions (e.g., for CaCl₂, Λ° = λ°_Ca²⁺ + 2λ°_Cl⁻); confusing Λ_m with Λ°_m in degree of dissociation calculation

Degree of Dissociation from Conductivity

Symbols: α = Λ_m / Λ°_m (for weak electrolytes); then K_a = Cα²/(1−α) ≈ Cα² (if α << 1)

Unit: α is dimensionless; K_a in mol L⁻¹

When to use: Finding dissociation constant of weak acids/bases using conductometric data

Conditions: α = Λ_m/Λ°_m holds strictly for weak electrolytes; not directly applicable to strong electrolytes without Debye–Hückel corrections

Common mistake: Using α = Λ_m/Λ°_m for strong electrolytes (where ion‑pairing invalidates it); forgetting that C must be in mol L⁻¹ for K_a expression

Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis

Symbols: W = Z × Q = (E/F) × I × t, where W = mass deposited/evolved (g), Z = electrochemical equivalent, E = equivalent mass (M/n‑factor), I = current (A), t = time (s), F = 96500 C

Unit: W in g; I in A; t in s; E in g eq⁻¹

When to use: Calculating mass of substance liberated at an electrode for a given charge

Conditions: 100% current efficiency assumed; no side reactions; constant current throughout

Common mistake: Using minutes instead of seconds for time; confusing E (equivalent mass) with M (molar mass) — must divide M by n‑factor

Faraday's Second Law (Combined Electrolysis)

Symbols: W₁/W₂ = E₁/E₂ = (M₁/n₁)/(M₂/n₂); or W ∝ E ∝ M/n

Unit: Dimensionless ratio

When to use: Comparing masses of different substances deposited by the same quantity of electricity (series cells)

Conditions: Same charge passes through each electrolytic cell connected in series

Common mistake: Forgetting to include n‑factor when comparing equivalent masses; using molar mass directly without dividing by valency

Charge and Current Relationship

Symbols: Q = I × t (Coulombs = Amperes × seconds)

Unit: Q in C (Coulombs); I in A; t in s

When to use: Basic computation of total charge passed through a circuit — prerequisite for all Faraday's law calculations

Conditions: Assumes steady current; for varying current, Q = ∫I dt

Common mistake: Using t in minutes/hours without converting to seconds; confusing ampere‑hour (Ah) with Coulombs (1 Ah = 3600 C)

Current Efficiency

Symbols: Current efficiency (%) = (Actual mass deposited / Theoretical mass from Q) × 100

Unit: Percent (%)

When to use: Accounts for side reactions or incomplete deposition in practical electrolysis

Conditions: Assumes main desired reaction identified correctly; side reactions consume fraction of total charge

Common mistake: Multiplying instead of dividing; ignoring that efficiency < 100% reduces the effective charge for the main reaction

Pb‑Accumulator (Lead‑Acid Battery) Reactions

Symbols: Anode (discharge): Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻; Cathode (discharge): PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O; Overall: Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O

Unit: E°_cell ≈ 2.0 V

When to use: Understanding the most common rechargeable battery; reactions reverse during charging

Conditions: H₂SO₄ concentration decreases during discharge (density drops); both electrodes form PbSO₄ coating

Common mistake: Reversing anode/cathode assignments between discharge and charge; forgetting that both electrodes produce PbSO₄

Fuel Cell (H₂–O₂) Reaction

Symbols: Anode: 2H₂ + 4OH⁻ → 4H₂O + 4e⁻; Cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻; Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O; E°_cell = 1.23 V

Unit: E° = 1.23 V; efficiency ~60–70%

When to use: Clean energy conversion device; produces only water as by‑product

Conditions: ALKALINE electrolyte shown; acidic fuel cells have different half‑reactions but same overall equation

Common mistake: Writing O₂ reduction in acidic medium when alkaline is specified; confusing E° with actual operating voltage (~0.7 V due to overpotential)

Corrosion (Iron Rusting) — Net Reaction

Symbols: Anodic: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻; Cathodic (acidic): 2H⁺ + ½O₂ + 2e⁻ → H₂O; Overall: 2Fe + ½O₂ + 2H₂O → Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (rust)

Unit: No single E° — involves multiple steps and ambient O₂ partial pressure

When to use: Understanding electrochemical basis of corrosion; prevention methods (painting, galvanization, sacrificial anodes, cathodic protection)

Conditions: Moisture and O₂ both required; electrolyte (dissolved salts) accelerates corrosion

Common mistake: Writing Fe₂O₃ alone as rust (it is hydrated Fe₂O₃·xH₂O); attributing corrosion to purely chemical oxidation without electrochemical cell formation

Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) — Reference

Symbols: 2H⁺(1 M) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂(1 atm); E° = 0.000 V by definition

Unit: E° = 0 V by convention

When to use: Reference electrode against which all other standard reduction potentials are measured

Conditions: Platinum black coated Pt electrode; H₂ gas at 1 atm bubbling over Pt in 1 M H⁺ at 298 K

Common mistake: Thinking SHE has a measurable non‑zero potential; confusing it with calomel or Ag/AgCl reference electrodes used in practice

EMF Sign Convention and Spontaneity Criteria

Symbols: If E_cell > 0: reaction is spontaneous as written (galvanic mode); ΔG < 0. If E_cell < 0: non‑spontaneous (electrolytic mode); ΔG > 0

Unit: E in V; ΔG in J

When to use: Determining direction of spontaneous electron flow and whether external voltage is needed

Conditions: E_cell defined as E_right − E_left (cathode − anode by convention); sign depends on how cell is written

Common mistake: Using absolute values of E° without considering cell diagram orientation; confusing galvanic (positive E) with electrolytic (requires external source)

Relation Between K, E° and Temperature

Symbols: log K = nE°/(0.0591) at 298 K; general: ln K = nFE°/(RT)

Unit: K dimensionless; log base 10 or ln as noted

When to use: Connecting thermodynamic stability (K) with electrochemical driving force (E°)

Conditions: Derives from ΔG° = −RT ln K = −nFE°; K computed is for the redox reaction as written

Common mistake: Using natural log vs common log inconsistently with the 0.0591 factor (which assumes log₁₀); not matching n with the balanced equation

Effect of Dilution on Molar Conductivity

Symbols: For strong electrolytes: Λ_m = Λ°_m − B√C (Kohlrausch eq., B = constant); for weak electrolytes: Λ_m rises sharply on dilution (α increases)

Unit: Λ_m in S cm² mol⁻¹; C in mol L⁻¹

When to use: Distinguishing strong vs weak electrolytes from conductance–concentration plots; extrapolating Λ°_m

Conditions: Kohlrausch linear form (Λ_m vs √C) applies well to strong electrolytes at moderate dilution; weak electrolytes require extrapolation via Kirkwood or Onsager theory

Common mistake: Extrapolating Λ_m vs √C for weak electrolytes to find Λ°_m (fails — curve is steep at low C); not recognizing different behaviours for strong vs weak electrolytes on graphs
