C% = \frac{12}{44} \times \frac{\text{Mass of CO}_2}{\text{Mass of compound}} \times 100

Symbols: 12 = atomic mass of C, 44 = molecular mass of CO₂

Unit: Percentage (% w/w)

When to use: Calculation of carbon percentage from combustion analysis (CO₂ collected)

Conditions: Complete combustion of organic compound; all carbon converted to CO₂

Common mistake: Forgetting the ratio 12/44 instead of using full 44; confusing CO₂ mass with C mass directly

H% = \frac{2}{18} \times \frac{\text{Mass of H}_2\text{O}}{\text{Mass of compound}} \times 100

Symbols: 2 = total atomic mass of H in H₂O, 18 = molecular mass of H₂O

Unit: Percentage (% w/w)

When to use: Calculation of hydrogen percentage from combustion analysis (H₂O collected)

Conditions: All hydrogen converted to water; using Mg(ClO₄)₂ or CaCl₂ as absorbent

Common mistake: Using 1 instead of 2 for hydrogen mass; not dividing by water mass properly

\text{N}_2 \text{ volume at STP} = \frac{22.4 \times \text{Mass of compound}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}} \times \frac{\%\text{N}}{100} \times \frac{273}{273+T} \times \frac{P}{760}

Symbols: 22.4 L = molar volume at STP, T = temperature in °C, P = pressure in mmHg

Unit: Volume in litres

When to use: Kjeldahl's method — converting N₂ volume from lab conditions to STP

Conditions: Gas collected over water must have aqueous tension subtracted: P_{dry} = P_{wet} - aq. tension

Common mistake: Forgetting to correct for aqueous tension and non-STP conditions; using wet gas volume directly

\%N = \frac{28 \times V_{\text{STP}} \times 100}{22.4 \times W}

Symbols: 28 = molar mass of N₂ (g/mol), V_STP = volume of N₂ at STP (L), W = mass of compound (g)

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Direct calculation of nitrogen % from Kjeldahl method when N₂ volume at STP is known

Conditions: V_STP must be corrected to STP; compound should contain N in amine/amide form

Common mistake: Using volume at lab conditions without STP correction; forgetting factor 28 for N₂

\%N = \frac{1.4 \times M \times V}{W}

Symbols: M = molarity of H₂SO₄ used, V = volume of H₂SO₄ neutralised (mL), W = mass of compound (g), 1.4 = derived constant (28/22.4 × 100/1000)

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Kjeldahl's method — when N is converted to NH₃, absorbed in excess standard H₂SO₄, and back-titrated

Conditions: Excess acid is known; remaining acid titrated with standard NaOH; only applicable to amino/nitrogen compounds digestible by Kjeldahl

Common mistake: Not accounting for blank correction; using wrong molarity; compound with nitro/nitroso groups cannot be estimated by Kjeldahl

\%Cl = \frac{35.5 \times m_{\text{AgCl}} \times 100}{143.5 \times W}

Symbols: 35.5 = atomic mass of Cl, 143.5 = molecular mass of AgCl, m_AgCl = mass of AgCl precipitate (g), W = mass of compound (g)

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Carius method — estimation of halogen (Cl, Br, I) from silver halide precipitate

Conditions: Compound heated with fuming HNO₃ and AgNO₃ in Carius tube; precipitate dried and weighed

Common mistake: Using atomic mass of Ag instead of AgCl; wrong stoichiometric ratio; forgetting that 1 mol AgCl contains 1 mol Cl

\%Br = \frac{80 \times m_{\text{AgBr}} \times 100}{188 \times W}

Symbols: 80 = atomic mass of Br, 188 = molecular mass of AgBr, m_AgBr = mass of AgBr precipitate (g), W = mass of compound (g)

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Carius method for bromine estimation

Conditions: Same as Cl estimation procedure

Common mistake: Confusing molecular masses of AgCl (143.5) and AgBr (188)

\%I = \frac{127 \times m_{\text{AgI}} \times 100}{235 \times W}

Symbols: 127 = atomic mass of I, 235 = molecular mass of AgI, m_AgI = mass of AgI precipitate (g), W = mass of compound (g)

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Carius method for iodine estimation

Conditions: Same procedure as Cl and Br

Common mistake: Using 235 incorrectly; confusing atomic and molecular masses

\%S = \frac{32 \times m_{\text{BaSO}_4} \times 100}{233 \times W}

Symbols: 32 = atomic mass of S, 233 = molecular mass of BaSO₄, m_BaSO4 = mass of BaSO₄ precipitate (g), W = mass of compound (g)

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Carius method for sulfur estimation

Conditions: Compound heated with fuming HNO₃ and BaCl₂; BaSO₄ precipitated, filtered, dried and weighed

Common mistake: Using wrong molecular mass of BaSO₄ (233 not 137+32+64); confusing S mass with BaSO₄ mass

\%O = 100 - (\%C + \%H + \%N + \%S + \%X)

Symbols: X = any halogen present

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Calculating oxygen percentage by difference when no direct method exists

Conditions: All other elements must be quantitatively determined first

Common mistake: Omitting any element; this is an indirect method and less accurate than direct methods

\text{Empirical Formula mass} = \sum \text{(atomic mass} \times \text{number of atoms in empirical formula)}

Symbols: Depends on the empirical formula obtained

Unit: g/mol

When to use: Finding n = Molecular mass / Empirical formula mass to get molecular formula

Conditions: Molecular mass should be known (from Victor Meyer, osmotic pressure, or other method)

Common mistake: Taking n as non-integer; rounding error leading to wrong molecular formula

n = \frac{\text{Molecular mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}}

Symbols: n must be a positive integer (1, 2, 3...)

Unit: Dimensionless

When to use: Determining molecular formula from empirical formula

Conditions: Molecular mass known accurately

Common mistake: Accepting non-integer n; rounding without checking proximity to nearest integer

\text{Molecular formula} = (\text{Empirical formula})_n

Symbols: n = ratio of molecular mass to empirical formula mass

Unit: —

When to use: Writing final molecular formula after finding n

Conditions: Empirical formula and molecular mass both known

Common mistake: Forgetting to multiply subscripts by n

R\cdot F. = \frac{\text{Distance travelled by solute}}{\text{Distance travelled by solvent}}

Symbols: Rf = Retardation factor / Retention factor, always between 0 and 1

Unit: Dimensionless (0 < Rf < 1)

When to use: Paper chromatography — identifying and comparing compounds

Conditions: Stationary phase = water adsorbed on paper; mobile phase = solvent/solvent mixture

Common mistake: Measuring from wrong origin; Rf > 1 indicates error; confusing distance of spot vs distance of solvent front

Dust number = \frac{1.5}{x/m} \times \frac{1000}{V}

Symbols: x/m = volume of gas absorbed per gram of charcoal at STP (mL/g), V = volume of air in litres containing 1 grain of dust per 100 ft³

Unit: Number (dimensionless)

When to use: Adams test — measuring dust pollution in atmosphere

Conditions: 1 grain = 64.8 mg; 100 ft³ of air

Common mistake: Unit conversion errors between litres, ft³, and grain

\text{Steam distillation condition: } P_{\text{total}} = P^o_A + P^o_B = P_{\text{atm}}

Symbols: P°A = vapour pressure of immiscible organic liquid, P°B = vapour pressure of water at same temperature

Unit: Pressure (mmHg or atm)

When to use: Understanding principle of steam distillation — mixture boils below 100°C

Conditions: Liquids must be immiscible; each exerts its own vapour pressure independently

Common mistake: Thinking Raoult's law applies — it does NOT for immiscible liquids

\frac{n_A}{n_B} = \frac{P^o_A}{P^o_B} = \frac{\text{Mass}_A / M_A}{\text{Mass}_B / M_B}

Symbols: nA, nB = moles of organic liquid and water in vapour phase; PA°, PB° = vapour pressures

Unit: Mole ratio

When to use: Calculating composition of distillate in steam distillation

Conditions: Immiscible liquids; ideal behaviour assumed

Common mistake: Using mass ratio directly instead of mole ratio; forgetting molar masses

\text{Radius of capillary rise (Christie method): } r = \sqrt{\frac{2\gamma \cos\theta}{\rho g h}}

Symbols: \gamma = surface tension, \theta = contact angle (0° for pure liquids in clean glass), \rho = density, g = gravity, h = height

Unit: Length (cm or m)

When to use: Determining capillary dimensions for boiling point/capillary tube preparation

Conditions: Clean glass; liquid wets glass completely (θ ≈ 0)

Common mistake: Forgetting cosθ term; using wrong units for surface tension

\text{Molar mass from Beckmann cryoscopy: } M_2 = \frac{K_f \times w_2 \times 1000}{w_1 \times \Delta T_f}

Symbols: Kf = cryoscopic constant of solvent, w₂ = mass of solute (g), w₁ = mass of solvent (g), ΔTf = depression in freezing point

Unit: g/mol

When to use: Determining molar mass of organic compound by freezing point depression

Conditions: Non-volatile, non-electrolyte solute; dilute solution

Common mistake: Confusing Kf with Kb; forgetting the 1000 factor when masses are in grams

\Delta T_b = i \cdot K_b \cdot m = i \cdot K_b \cdot \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{w_1 \cdot M_2}

Symbols: i = van't Hoff factor, Kb = ebullioscopic constant, m = molality, w₂ = mass solute, w₁ = mass solvent, M₂ = molar mass of solute

Unit: Temperature (K or °C)

When to use: Elevation in boiling point for molar mass determination (Victor Meyer variation)

Conditions: Non-volatile solute, non-electrolyte unless i accounts for dissociation/association

Common mistake: Ignoring van't Hoff factor for electrolytes; mixing up Kb and Kf values

\Delta T_f = i \cdot K_f \cdot m

Symbols: i = van't Hoff factor, Kf = cryoscopic constant, m = molality = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{w_1 \cdot M_2}

Unit: Temperature (K or °C)

When to use: Freezing point depression for molar mass determination

Conditions: Dilute solution; solute should not associate or dissociate unexpectedly

Common mistake: Forgetting to convert w₁ to kg when using SI molality; wrong sign convention

\pi = i \cdot C \cdot R \cdot T = i \cdot \frac{n_2}{V} \cdot R \cdot T

Symbols: \pi = osmotic pressure, i = van't Hoff factor, C = molar concentration (mol/L), R = gas constant (0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹), T = temperature (K), V = volume of solution (L)

Unit: atm or Pa

When to use: Os mometric method for molar mass determination of high molecular mass substances

Conditions: Semipermeable membrane; dilute solution; temperature must be controlled precisely

Common mistake: Forgetting osmotic pressure is a colligative property; using wrong R value; not converting temperature to Kelvin

M_2 = \frac{i \cdot W_2 \cdot R \cdot T}{\pi \cdot V}

Symbols: M₂ = molar mass of solute, W₂ = mass of solute (g), R = 0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹, T = temperature (K), \pi = osmotic pressure (atm), V = volume of solution (L)

Unit: g/mol

When to use: Calculating molar mass from osmotic pressure measurement

Conditions: Known i (usually 1 for non-electrolytes like organic compounds)

Common mistake: Using Pa instead of atm with wrong R; not converting mL to L for V

\text{Zeisel's method: } \%\text{OCH}_3 = \frac{15 \times m_{\text{AgI}} \times 100}{235 \times W}

Symbols: 15 = mass of OCH₃ group, 235 = mass of AgI, m_AgI = mass of AgI obtained, W = mass of compound

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Estimation of methoxy (—OCH₃) or ethoxy (—OC₂H₅) groups

Conditions: Compound heated with HI; CH₃I formed, absorbed in AgNO₃ to give AgI

Common mistake: Using wrong molecular mass; confusing OCH₃ mass (31) with 15 (which is for CH₃I → AgI stoichiometry)

\text{Hunsberger method: } \%N = \frac{28 \times V \times 100}{22400 \times W} \quad (\text{for diazotisation})

Symbols: V = volume of N₂ evolved at STP (mL), W = mass of compound (g), 28 = molar mass of N₂, 22400 = molar volume at STP in mL

Unit: Percentage (%) by mass

When to use: Estimation of primary aromatic amines via diazotisation and nitrogen liberation

Conditions: Primary aromatic amine; cold NaNO₂ + HCl; N₂ gas collected over KOH solution

Common mistake: Using 22.4 L instead of 22400 mL when V is in mL; applying to aliphatic amines which give different products

\text{Grignard reagent: } R-X + \text{Mg} \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} R\text{-Mg-X}

Symbols: R = alkyl/aryl group, X = halogen (Cl, Br, I)

Unit: —

When to use: Preparation of Grignard reagent for structural elucidation and synthesis

Conditions: Absolutely dry ether solvent; moisture-free apparatus; inert atmosphere preferred

Common mistake: Using wet ether — Grignard reagent gets destroyed; ignoring that aryl iodides react faster than chlorides

\text{Dipole moment: } \mu = q \times d

Symbols: \mu = dipole moment (Debye, D), q = magnitude of charge, d = distance between charges

Unit: Debye (D); 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m

When to use: Determining polarity and structure of organic molecules

Conditions: Point charge approximation; useful for comparing isomers

Common mistake: Confusing Debye with SI unit; not considering vector nature for polyatomic molecules

\mu_{\text{resultant}} = \sqrt{\mu_1^2 + \mu_2^2 + 2\mu_1\mu_2\cos\theta}

Symbols: \mu₁, \mu₂ = individual bond dipole moments, \theta = angle between them

Unit: Debye (D)

When to use: Finding net dipole moment of molecules with two polar bonds (e.g., H₂O, o-dichlorobenzene)

Conditions: Two dipoles acting at an angle; vector addition

Common mistake: Taking θ as bond angle instead of angle between dipole vectors; sign error in cosθ

\text{Becke line method: } n_{\text{liquid}} > n_{\text{solid}} \Rightarrow \text{Becke line moves into liquid}

Symbols: n = refractive index of liquid or solid

Unit: —

When to use: Determining refractive index of liquid for melting point comparison or substance identification

Conditions: Mounted in oil of known/refractive index on microscope slide

Common mistake: Reversing the direction rule; not matching refractive index with melting point data
