Gravitation (गुरुत्वाकर्षण) — Formula Sheet | JEE Main Physics

JEE MainHindiFormula Sheet
F = G × (m₁ × m₂) / r²

Symbols: F = gravitational force between two masses (N), G = universal gravitational constant = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², m₁, m₂ = masses (kg), r = distance between centres of masses (m)

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: To calculate the gravitational force between any two point masses or spherical bodies; foundation for all gravitation problems in JEE Main.

Conditions: Point masses or spherically symmetric mass distributions; valid outside the shells too. Not directly applicable inside a non-uniform body without integration.

Common mistake: Using surface distance instead of centre-to-centre distance. For a shell, force is calculated using R (radius), not zero.

g = G × M / R²

Symbols: g = acceleration due to gravity at surface (m/s²), G = gravitational constant, M = mass of planet (kg), R = radius of planet (m)

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Calculates surface gravity of Earth or any planet. Used in problems involving weight, free fall, projectile motion near surface.

Conditions: Valid only on the surface of a uniform spherical body. Assumes no rotation of planet for basic form.

Common mistake: Confusing g with G. Also forgetting that g depends on M and R of the planet, not on the mass of the object falling.

g(h) = g × (1 − 2h/R) [for h << R]

Symbols: g(h) = gravity at height h above surface (m/s²), g = surface gravity, h = height above surface, R = planetary radius

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Approximate decrease in gravity with small altitude. Used in JEE Main for heights much smaller than R.

Conditions: Only valid when h << R. For large h, use exact formula g(h) = g × R²/(R+h)².

Common mistake: Applying the approximate formula for large heights. Also forgetting that gravity decreases with height.

g(d) = g × (1 − d/R) [assuming uniform density]

Symbols: g(d) = gravity at depth d below surface (m/s²), g = surface gravity, d = depth, R = planetary radius

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Gravity variation with depth inside Earth (uniform density model). Important for JEE Main numericals.

Conditions: Earth assumed to be a uniform solid sphere. Actual Earth has varying density but this approximation is standard for JEE.

Common mistake: Using this for non-uniform bodies. At centre (d = R), g = 0, which many students forget.

g_effective = g − ω²R cos²λ [at latitude λ]

Symbols: g_effective = apparent gravity considering Earth's rotation (m/s²), ω = angular velocity of Earth = 7.27 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s, R = Earth's radius, λ = latitude

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Calculating effective gravity at any latitude on rotating Earth. Gravity is minimum at equator (λ = 0°) and maximum at poles (λ = 90°).

Conditions: For a rotating spherical Earth. At poles ω²R cos²λ = 0 so g_effective = g. At equator it is minimum.

Common mistake: Forgetting the cos²λ factor. Many students use just ω²R which is correct only at equator.

U = −G × (m₁ × m₂) / r

Symbols: U = gravitational potential energy (J), G = gravitational constant, m₁, m₂ = masses (kg), r = separation between centres (m)

Unit: Joule (J)

When to use: Gravitational PE between two masses. Negative sign indicates bound system. Essential for orbital energy and escape velocity derivations.

Conditions: Reference point at infinity (U = 0 at r → ∞). Valid for point masses or spherical shells.

Common mistake: Forgetting the negative sign. Also confusing PE with potential — U is energy, V is potential per unit mass.

V = −G × M / r

Symbols: V = gravitational potential (J/kg), G = gravitational constant, M = source mass (kg), r = distance from centre of mass (m)

Unit: J/kg or m²/s²

When to use: Potential at a point due to a spherical mass. Used to find work done in bringing unit mass from infinity.

Conditions: Outside or on surface of spherical mass. Inside a shell V is constant = −GM/R.

Common mistake: Sign error. Potential is always negative for attractive gravity. Also confusing V with field intensity E.

E = −G × M × m / (2r) [for circular orbit]

Symbols: E = total mechanical energy of orbiting body (J), G = gravitational constant, M = central mass, m = orbiting mass, r = orbital radius

Unit: Joule (J)

When to use: Total energy in circular orbit. Negative value confirms bound orbit. |E| = KE = |PE|/2 — key relationship for JEE.

Conditions: Circular orbit only. For elliptical orbits, replace r with semi-major axis a.

Common mistake: Taking energy as positive. A satellite in orbit always has negative total energy.

v_orbital = √(GM/r) = √(gR²/r)

Symbols: v_orbital = orbital velocity (m/s), G = gravitational constant, M = mass of planet, r = orbital radius from centre, g = surface gravity, R = planetary radius

Unit: m/s

When to use: Velocity required for a stable circular orbit at radius r. At surface (r = R), v_orbital = √(gR) ≈ 7.9 km/s for Earth.

Conditions: Circular orbit. For elliptical, use vis-viva equation. Independent of mass of satellite.

Common mistake: Including satellite mass in formula — orbital velocity is independent of satellite mass. Also confusing orbital radius with altitude.

v_escape = √(2GM/R) = √(2gR)

Symbols: v_escape = escape velocity (m/s), G = gravitational constant, M = planet mass, R = planet radius, g = surface gravity

Unit: m/s

When to use: Minimum velocity to escape planet's gravitational field completely. For Earth, v_escape ≈ 11.2 km/s.

Conditions: Launched from surface (r = R). Independent of direction and mass of object. Assumes no atmospheric drag.

Common mistake: Thinking escape velocity depends on launch angle or mass of body. It does not — only on M and R of planet.

T = 2π√(r³/GM) = 2πr/v_orbital

Symbols: T = orbital period (s), r = orbital radius (m), G = gravitational constant, M = central mass (kg)

Unit: Second (s)

When to use: Time period of a satellite in circular orbit. Directly used with Kepler's third law for JEE problems.

Conditions: Circular orbit. Derives from equating centripetal force to gravitational force.

Common mistake: Using altitude instead of orbital radius (r = R + h). Must use distance from centre of Earth.

T² ∝ r³ [Kepler's Third Law]

Symbols: T = orbital period, r = semi-major axis (orbital radius for circle), proportionality constant = 4π²/GM for same central body

Unit: Satisfied in SI units when T in s and r in m

When to use: Comparing periods of different satellites/planets. If T₁/T₂ = (r₁/r₂)^(3/2). Very frequently tested in JEE Main.

Conditions: Applies to all bodies orbiting the same central mass. For solar system, M = mass of Sun.

Common mistake: Using diameters or radii instead of orbital radius. The law uses the actual orbital path radius.

Areal velocity = dA/dt = L/(2m) = constant [Kepler's Second Law]

Symbols: A = area swept by radius vector, t = time, L = angular momentum, m = mass of orbiting body

Unit: m²/s

When to use: Conservation of angular momentum implies equal areas in equal times. Used in elliptical orbit problems (periapsis-apoapsis relations).

Conditions: Only central forces (like gravity). Always conserved regardless of orbit shape.

Common mistake: Forgetting this applies to ANY central force, not just gravity. In elliptical orbits, speed is maximum at perigee.

g_at_pole > g_at_equator due to rotation: Δg = ω²R

Symbols: ω = angular speed of Earth = 7.27 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s, R = Earth radius = 6.4 × 10⁶ m, Δg ≈ 0.034 m/s²

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Explains why g is maximum at poles and minimum at equator. If Earth stopped rotating, g at equator would increase by ~0.034 m/s².

Conditions: Assumes spherical Earth. Real Earth is oblate which also contributes to g variation.

Common mistake: Forgetting the magnitude. The change is small (~0.3%) but conceptually important for JEE.

Weightlessness: N = m(g − a) → if a = g, N = 0

Symbols: N = normal reaction/apparent weight, m = mass, g = gravity, a = downward acceleration of frame

Unit: Newton (N) for force

When to use: In a freely falling elevator or orbiting satellite, apparent weight is zero because both accelerate at g. Explains why astronauts float.

Conditions: Valid when the entire system accelerates downward at g. In orbit, continuous free fall creates sustained weightlessness.

Common mistake: Thinking gravity disappears in space — it doesn't. Astronauts are weightless because they are in continuous free fall around Earth.

T_geo = 24 hours, h_geo ≈ 35,786 km

Symbols: T_geo = geostationary satellite period = 24 h = 86400 s, h_geo = height above Earth's surface ≈ 36,000 km

Unit: Height in km, Period in seconds

When to use: Geostationary satellite appears stationary over equator. Used in communications. Orbital radius r ≈ 42,164 km from Earth centre.

Conditions: Must orbit in equatorial plane, same direction as Earth's rotation, with period exactly 24 h.

Common mistake: Thinking any satellite at 36,000 km height is geostationary — it must also be in equatorial plane.

g_inside_shell = 0, V_inside_shell = −GM/R = constant

Symbols: g = gravitational field intensity inside shell = 0, V = potential inside shell = constant = surface value

Unit: g in N/kg or m/s², V in J/kg

When to use: Key result for shell theorem. Inside a hollow spherical shell, no net gravitational force but potential is non-zero and constant.

Conditions: Thin spherical shell with uniform mass distribution. Field is zero everywhere inside.

Common mistake: Assuming potential is zero inside shell — it is constant but NOT zero. It equals the surface potential.

g_inside_uniform_solid_sphere = GMr/R³ = g × r/R

Symbols: g(r) = gravity at distance r from centre inside uniform sphere, M = total mass, R = radius, g = surface gravity

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Gravity varies linearly with distance from centre inside Earth (uniform model). Zero at centre, g at surface.

Conditions: Uniform density sphere. For real Earth with varying density, the relation is more complex.

Common mistake: Using inverse square law inside the sphere — it is linear, not 1/r², inside a uniform solid sphere.

Binding Energy = |E_total| = GMm/(2r) [for circular orbit]

Symbols: BE = energy needed to remove satellite to infinity (J), G = gravitational constant, M = central mass, m = satellite mass, r = orbital radius

Unit: Joule (J)

When to use: Minimum energy to free a satellite from orbit. Equal to the magnitude of total orbital energy.

Conditions: Circular orbit. For escape from surface, use BE = GMm/R.

Common mistake: Confusing binding energy with escape energy from surface. Binding energy depends on orbital radius r, not just planetary radius R.
