Laws of Motion – Formula Sheet (JEE Main)

JEE MainHindiFormula Sheet
F_net = m · a

Symbols: F_net = net external force (N), m = mass (kg), a = acceleration (m/s²)

Unit: Newton (N) for force; kg·m/s² equivalent

When to use: Fundamental relation used when an object's acceleration is known or needs to be found under a set of forces.

Conditions: Mass must be constant; valid in inertial reference frames only. Direction of a is same as direction of F_net.

Common mistake: Using F = ma with only one force instead of the vector sum of all forces. Also forgetting that if F_net = 0 then a = 0 (not that v = 0).

p = m · v

Symbols: p = linear momentum (kg·m/s), m = mass (kg), v = velocity (m/s)

Unit: kg·m/s or N·s

When to use: Quantifies motion; conserved in isolated systems during collisions and explosions.

Conditions: Valid for any speed much less than c. For relativistic speeds, use p = γmv.

Common mistake: Confusing momentum with kinetic energy. Momentum is a vector; KE is a scalar. Doubling speed doubles momentum but quadruples KE.

F = dp/dt (general form of Newton’s second law)

Symbols: F = net force (N), p = momentum (kg·m/s), t = time (s)

Unit: N = kg·m/s²

When to use: Essential for variable-mass systems (rockets, conveyor belts) and collision analysis where mass changes with time.

Conditions: Applicable even when mass varies with time. Reduces to F = ma when m is constant.

Common mistake: Assuming F = ma always, even for rockets. For rockets, must use F_ext = dp/dt = m(dv/dt) − u(dm/dt), where u is exhaust speed relative to rocket.

J = ∫ F dt ≈ F_avg · Δt = Δp = m(v_f − v_i)

Symbols: J = impulse (N·s), F_avg = average force (N), Δt = time interval (s), v_f = final velocity, v_i = initial velocity

Unit: N·s or kg·m/s

When to use: Connects force acting over a time interval to change in momentum. Used extensively in collision problems.

Conditions: Force may vary with time; impulse equals area under F-t graph.

Common mistake: Treating impulse as a force. Impulse has units of momentum. Also confusing sign conventions — impulse direction follows change in momentum.

f_s ≤ μ_s · N ; f_s(max) = μ_s · N (limiting friction)

Symbols: f_s = static friction force (N), μ_s = coefficient of static friction (dimensionless), N = normal reaction (N)

Unit: Newton (N); μ_s is dimensionless

When to use: Determines whether an object remains at rest on a surface. Used to find minimum force required to initiate motion.

Conditions: Holds for dry (Coulomb) friction between solid surfaces. Normal reaction N must be correctly computed from free-body diagram.

Common mistake: Assuming f_s = μ_s N always. It is equal to μ_s N only at impending motion (limiting case); otherwise f_s adjusts to match applied force up to the maximum.

f_k = μ_k · N

Symbols: f_k = kinetic (dynamic) friction force (N), μ_k = coefficient of kinetic friction (dimensionless), N = normal reaction (N)

Unit: Newton (N); μ_k is dimensionless

When to use: Applied when two surfaces are sliding relative to each other.

Conditions: Generally μ_k < μ_s. Valid for moderate speeds; at very high speeds friction behaviour may change.

Common mistake: Using μ_s for moving objects. Once sliding begins, friction drops to μ_k N, not μ_s N.

a = g sin θ − μ_k g cos θ (block sliding down rough incline)

Symbols: a = acceleration down the plane (m/s²), g = gravitational acceleration (≈ 9.8 m/s²), θ = angle of incline, μ_k = coefficient of kinetic friction

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Computes acceleration of a block sliding down a rough inclined plane.

Conditions: θ > angle of repose (μ = tan⁻¹μ_s) for motion to start. Friction acts up the plane opposing motion.

Common mistake: Using sin θ for the normal component and cos θ for the parallel component — they are reversed. Component of weight down the plane = mg sin θ; perpendicular = mg cos θ.

a = g sin θ (smooth incline, no friction)

Symbols: a = acceleration down the plane (m/s²), g = gravitational acceleration, θ = angle of incline

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Baseline case; used when plane is frictionless.

Conditions: No friction; block slides freely down the incline.

Common mistake: Forgetting that this acceleration is independent of mass — all objects slide down a smooth incline with the same a.

N = mg cos θ (on incline, no additional vertical forces)

Symbols: N = normal reaction (N), m = mass (kg), g = gravity (m/s²), θ = angle of incline measured from horizontal

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Computes normal force on a block resting or sliding on an inclined plane.

Conditions: No other forces have components perpendicular to the plane besides weight and normal reaction.

Common mistake: Writing N = mg always. On an incline, N = mg cos θ. If an additional force has a perpendicular component, N changes accordingly.

a = (m₁ − m₂) · g / (m₁ + m₂) (Atwood machine, m₁ > m₂)

Symbols: a = acceleration of each mass (m/s²), m₁ = heavier mass (kg), m₂ = lighter mass (kg), g = gravitational acceleration

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Standard two-mass pulley system with a light inextensible string over a frictionless pulley.

Conditions: String is massless and inextensible; pulley is massless and frictionless; m₁ ≠ m₂.

Common mistake: Ignoring that both masses have the same magnitude of acceleration (connected by inextensible string). Also misidentifying the direction of tension.

T = 2 m₁ m₂ · g / (m₁ + m₂) (tension in Atwood machine)

Symbols: T = tension in the string (N), m₁ and m₂ = masses (kg), g = gravitational acceleration

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Computes string tension in a simple Atwood machine.

Conditions: Same assumptions as Atwood acceleration formula.

Common mistake: Thinking tension equals the weight of one mass. Tension is intermediate between the two weights when the system accelerates.

a = F / (m₁ + m₂) (two blocks pushed together on smooth surface)

Symbols: a = common acceleration (m/s²), F = applied external force (N), m₁, m₂ = masses (kg)

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Blocks in contact on a frictionless horizontal surface, pushed by a force.

Conditions: Surface is smooth (no friction); blocks remain in contact; force is applied horizontally.

Common mistake: Dividing force by only one mass instead of the total system mass when finding common acceleration.

f = μ · N ; tan λ = μ_s (angle of friction)

Symbols: λ = angle of friction, μ_s = coefficient of static friction

Unit: λ is an angle (degrees or radians); μ_s is dimensionless

When to use: Geometric interpretation of friction; useful in problems involving resultant of normal and friction forces.

Conditions: Defined at limiting friction only.

Common mistake: Confusing angle of friction with angle of repose — they are numerically equal but defined differently.

θ_repose = tan⁻¹(μ_s) (angle of repose)

Symbols: θ_repose = angle of repose (° or rad), μ_s = coefficient of static friction

Unit: Angle; μ_s dimensionless

When to use: Maximum incline angle before a block begins to slip.

Conditions: Block on an inclined plane; motion impends at this angle.

Common mistake: Using μ_k instead of μ_s for angle of repose, since slipping onset depends on static friction.

F_c = m v² / r = m ω² r (centripetal force)

Symbols: F_c = centripetal force (N), m = mass (kg), v = tangential speed (m/s), r = radius (m), ω = angular speed (rad/s)

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Required for any circular motion problem — identifies the net inward force.

Conditions: R must be the radius of curvature of the path; v must be instantaneous speed.

Common mistake: Treating centripetal force as a new separate force. It is the net radial component of existing forces (tension, gravity, friction, normal).

v_max (flat curve) = √(μ_s · g · r)

Symbols: v_max = maximum safe speed (m/s), μ_s = coefficient of static friction, g = gravitational acceleration, r = radius of curve (m)

Unit: m/s

When to use: Car/bike taking a horizontal curve without skidding — friction provides centripetal force.

Conditions: Road is flat (unbanked); static friction prevents lateral slipping.

Common mistake: Using μ_k instead of μ_s. Before skidding begins, static friction provides the necessary centripetal force.

v_ideal (banked road, no friction) = √(r g tan θ)

Symbols: v_ideal = ideal speed (m/s), r = radius of curve (m), g = gravitational acceleration, θ = banking angle

Unit: m/s

When to use: Design speed for a banked curve where no friction is needed to negotiate the turn.

Conditions: Road is perfectly smooth at this speed; friction is zero.

Common mistake: Forgetting that tan θ arises from resolving normal force into horizontal (centripetal) and vertical (balancing weight) components.

v_max (banked road with friction) = √[ r g (sin θ + μ_s cos θ) / (cos θ − μ_s sin θ) ]

Symbols: v_max = maximum speed without sliding up (m/s), θ = banking angle, μ_s = coefficient of static friction, r = radius, g = gravity

Unit: m/s

When to use: Banked curve with friction — finds maximum speed before vehicle slides upward along the bank.

Conditions: Friction acts down the plane (prevents sliding up). Derivation requires resolving all forces parallel and perpendicular to the banked surface.

Common mistake: Flipping the numerator/denominator signs or using μ_k instead of μ_s. Carefully identify whether friction acts up or down the plane depending on whether you seek v_max or v_min.

v_min (banked road with friction) = √[ r g (sin θ − μ_s cos θ) / (cos θ + μ_s sin θ) ]

Symbols: v_min = minimum speed without sliding down (m/s), other symbols same as above

Unit: m/s

When to use: Minimum speed to prevent sliding down on a banked curve with friction.

Conditions: Friction acts up the plane (prevents sliding down). If θ < angle of repose even at v = 0, the vehicle can remain at rest.

Common mistake: Confusing v_max and v_min expressions — the sign changes in numerator and denominator reflect the reversed friction direction.

F_pseudo = −m · a_frame (pseudo force in non-inertial frame)

Symbols: F_pseudo = pseudo (fictitious) force (N), m = mass of object (kg), a_frame = acceleration of the non-inertial reference frame (m/s²)

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Analyzing motion from an accelerating frame (e.g., elevator, accelerating cart) by introducing a backward inertial force.

Conditions: Frame must have acceleration a_frame relative to an inertial frame. Pseudo force acts opposite to frame's acceleration.

Common mistake: Applying pseudo force in an inertial frame (where it doesn't exist). Also getting the sign wrong — it always opposes the frame's acceleration.

Apparent weight in elevator: W' = m(g + a) (upward acceleration a)

Symbols: W' = apparent weight (N), m = mass (kg), g = gravity, a = upward acceleration of elevator (m/s²)

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Finding normal reaction (apparent weight) experienced by a person in an accelerating elevator.

Conditions: a is positive when elevator accelerates upward; negative (deceleration) when accelerating downward.

Common mistake: Sign errors — upward acceleration increases apparent weight, downward acceleration decreases it. At free fall (a = g downward), W' = 0.

Apparent weight in elevator: W' = m(g − a) (downward acceleration a)

Symbols: W' = apparent weight (N), m = mass (kg), g = gravity, a = downward acceleration (m/s²)

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Same scenario as above but for downward acceleration.

Conditions: If a = g, object is in weightlessness (W' = 0). If a > g, object loses contact with floor.

Common mistake: Forgetting the special case a = g leading to zero apparent weight (free fall).

T = m(g − a) for hanging mass in downward-accelerating elevator

Symbols: T = tension in string (N), m = suspended mass (kg), g = gravity, a = downward acceleration of elevator

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Tension in a string suspending a mass inside an elevator accelerating downward.

Conditions: Same sign convention as apparent weight.

Common mistake: Using T = mg even when the elevator accelerates. Tension changes with acceleration of the frame.

Chain/flexible rope on table: Force to pull hanging part = (m_hanging / m_total) · m_total · g = m_hanging · g

Symbols: Force = pulling force required (N), m_hanging = mass of hanging portion (kg), m_total = total mass (kg), g = gravity

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Finding force needed to just lift or hold a chain partially hanging over a table edge.

Conditions: Chain is uniform; friction on table surface assumed negligible unless specified.

Common mistake: Multiplying total mass by g instead of just the hanging mass portion. Only the hanging portion's weight contributes to the pulling force.

Equation of motion under constant force: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as

Symbols: v = final velocity, u = initial velocity, a = constant acceleration, t = time, s = displacement

Unit: SI units throughout (m, s, m/s, m/s²)

When to use: Kinematic equations combined with F = ma for constant-force problems (projectiles, inclined planes, pulleys with constant tension).

Conditions: Acceleration must be constant. Valid only when net force is constant in magnitude and direction.

Common mistake: Applying these when acceleration is not constant (e.g., spring force, drag force). Always verify F_net is constant first.

Net force for connected bodies: F = (m₁ + m₂ + ...) · a_common

Symbols: F = external force on system (N), mᵢ = individual masses (kg), a_common = shared acceleration (m/s²)

Unit: N and m/s²

When to use: System-level approach to find common acceleration of multiple connected objects before analyzing internal tensions.

Conditions: Connections are inextensible; all parts move together with same acceleration magnitude.

Common mistake: Drawing free-body diagrams with wrong tension directions or double-counting internal forces when applying F = ma to the whole system.

Acceleration of block on wedge (wedge on smooth surface): a_block/wedge = g sin θ / (1 + m_block cos²θ / M_wedge)

Symbols: a = relative acceleration (m/s²), θ = wedge angle, m_block = block mass, M_wedge = wedge mass, g = gravity

Unit: m/s²

When to use: Advanced connected-body problem where both block and wedge accelerate (wedge recoils on smooth floor).

Conditions: All surfaces smooth unless μ is specified. String/pulley not involved in this specific variant.

Common mistake: Assuming wedge is fixed when it's actually free to move on a smooth surface. Must use constraint equations and solve simultaneous equations.

Friction direction rule: friction opposes relative motion (or impending relative motion) between contacting surfaces

Symbols: No single formula — conceptual rule. f direction is opposite to v_rel or impending v_rel at the contact point.

Unit: N (magnitude); direction determined by relative motion

When to use: Correctly assigning friction direction in free-body diagrams — the most common source of error in Laws of Motion problems.

Conditions: For static friction, direction opposes impending motion. For kinetic friction, direction opposes actual relative sliding.

Common mistake: Assigning friction in the direction of motion of the body rather than opposing relative motion at the contact surface. Example: friction on a block on an accelerating truck acts forward (same as truck's acceleration) because it prevents the block from sliding backward relative to the truck.
