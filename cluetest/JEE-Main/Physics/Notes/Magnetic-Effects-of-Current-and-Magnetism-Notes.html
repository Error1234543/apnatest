Biot-Savart Law — Statement, Formula and Direction Rule

The Biot-Savart Law gives the magnetic field dB produced by a small current element Idl at an observation point P located at position vector r from the element.

Formula (vector form):
dB = (μ₀ / 4π) × (I dl × r̂) / r²

Scalar form (when the angle between dl and r is θ):
dB = (μ₀ / 4π) × (I dl sin θ) / r²

Here:
• μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A (permeability of free space)
• I = steady current through the element
• dl = vector length of the current element (direction = current direction)
• r = distance from the element to point P
• θ = angle between dl and r
• r̂ = unit vector along r

Direction rule (Right-Hand Thumb Rule / Cross-product rule):
Point your right-hand thumb along the direction of current (dl). Curl your fingers; the direction your fingers point at P gives the direction of dB. Mathematically it is given by the vector cross product dl × r̂. If dl × r̂ points out of the page, dB is outward (⊙); if into the page, dB is inward (⊗).

Key conditions:
• The law applies only to steady (DC) currents. It cannot be directly used for time-varying currents without modification.
• The element dl must be infinitesimally small; for finite conductors you integrate along the wire.
• Magnetic field is a vector quantity — superposition applies: B_total = Σ dB over all elements.

Common confusion: Do not confuse this with Coulomb's law. In Biot-Savart, the source is a current element (vector), while in Coulomb's law the source is a scalar charge. Also, the inverse-square dependence on r is similar, but the angular factor sin θ makes this qualitatively different.

Magnetic Field Due to a Straight Current-Carrying Conductor

For an infinitely long straight wire carrying current I, the magnetic field at a perpendicular distance R from the wire is derived by integrating Biot-Savart over the entire wire:

B = μ₀I / (2πR)

Direction: Use RHR — grip the wire with your right hand, thumb along current; curled fingers give the circular field direction around the wire. Field lines form concentric circles centered on the wire.

Finite wire case: For a straight wire segment subtending angles α and β at P (measured from the perpendicular to the wire ends), the field magnitude is:

B = (μ₀I / 4πR) × (sin α + sin β)

Special cases of the finite wire formula:
• Point P on the perpendicular bisector of a wire of length 2l: α = β, so B = (μ₀I / 4πR) × 2sinα, where sin α = l/√(l²+R²).
• End-point of a semi-infinite wire: one angle is 90°, the other is 0°, giving B = μ₀I / (4πR) — exactly half the infinite-wire value.
• Infinite wire limit: both angles → 90°, recovering B = μ₀I/(2πR).

Important note: R here is the perpendicular (shortest) distance from the wire to point P, not the slant distance. If the point lies along the line of the wire (θ = 0 or 180°), sin θ = 0 and B = 0 along that line.

Magnetic Field Due to a Circular Current Loop

Field at the centre of a circular loop of radius R carrying current I:

B = μ₀I / (2R)

For N turns tightly wound together: B = μ₀NI / (2R)

Direction: Use RHR for a loop — curl fingers in the direction of current around the loop; thumb points in the direction of B at the centre. Alternatively, look at one face of the loop: if current flows anticlockwise, that face acts as the North pole (field emerges); if clockwise, it acts as the South pole (field enters).

Field on the axial line (axis passing through the centre, perpendicular to the plane of the loop):
At a distance x from the centre:

B = μ₀IR² / [2(R² + x²)^(3/2)]

Direction: Along the axis, away from the loop on the North-pole side and toward the loop on the South-pole side.

Special cases:
• At the centre (x = 0): reduces to μ₀I/(2R), consistent.
• Far away (x >> R): B ≈ μ₀IR² / (2x³), showing the loop behaves like a magnetic dipole at large distances.

Field at an arbitrary point in space does not have a simple closed form and is generally evaluated numerically or via elliptic integrals — not required for JEE Main beyond the centre and axial cases.

Magnetic dipole moment of a current loop: m = NI A, where A = πR² is the area. Direction of m is given by RHR (same as B at centre).

Ampere's Circuital Law and Its Applications

Ampere's Circuital Law relates the line integral of B around a closed loop to the net current enclosed:

∮ B · dl = μ₀ I_enclosed

Conditions for applying Ampere's Law effectively:
• The current distribution must possess high symmetry (cylindrical, planar, or solenoidal).
• The Amperian loop should be chosen so that B is either constant in magnitude and parallel to dl, or B is perpendicular to dl on portions of the loop (contributing zero).

Application 1 — Infinitely long straight wire (re-derivation):
Choose a circular loop of radius R centred on the wire. By symmetry, B is tangential and constant on this loop:
∮ B dl = B × 2πR = μ₀I → B = μ₀I/(2πR)

Application 2 — Solid cylindrical conductor of radius a carrying uniform current I:
• Inside the conductor (r < a): I_enclosed = I(r²/a²)
B = μ₀Ir / (2πa²) — field increases linearly with r.
• On the surface (r = a): B = μ₀I/(2πa)
• Outside the conductor (r > a): I_enclosed = I
B = μ₀I/(2πr) — same as a thin wire.

Application 3 — Infinite solenoid (n turns per unit length, current I):
• Inside: B = μ₀nI (uniform, along the axis)
• Outside: B ≈ 0 (ideal solenoid)

Application 4 — Toroid (N turns, mean radius R):
• Inside the core: B = μ₀NI/(2πr), where r is the radial distance from the centre.
• Outside the toroid: B = 0
• In the empty interior region (r < inner radius): B = 0

Note: A solenoid can be thought of as a straightened toroid; a toroid has no free ends, hence no external field in the ideal case.

Solenoid and Toroid — Detailed Analysis

Solenoïd: A long coil of many turns of insulated wire wrapped closely in the form of a cylinder. When current flows, it produces a uniform magnetic field inside, resembling a bar magnet.

Ideal solenoid properties:
• B_inside = μ₀nI, directed along the axis (n = N/L, turns per unit length)
• Field outside is negligible
• Both ends act as North and South poles
• Self-inductance L = μ₀n²AL, where A is cross-sectional area

Effect of inserting a core:
• With a soft iron core of relative permeability μᵣ: B = μ₀μᵣnI = μnI
• This greatly amplifies the field; used in electromagnets.

Toroid: A solenoid bent into a closed circle (donut shape).
• Number of turns = N
• Mean radius = R
• B inside the core at radial distance r: B = μ₀NI/(2πr)
• B outside the toroid = 0 (for an ideal toroid)
• Unlike a solenoid, a toroid has no distinct poles; its field is entirely confined within the core.

Comparison table:
| Property | Solenoid | Toroid |
|---|---|---|
| Field shape | Uniform inside, non-zero outside | Confined entirely inside core |
| External field | Resembles bar magnet | Zero outside |
| B formula | μ₀nI (inside) | μ₀NI/(2πr) |
| Turns density | n = N/L (constant) | Varies as 1/r |

JEE tip: Questions frequently ask to compare B at two different radial distances inside a toroid, or to identify the correct field pattern diagram.

Force on a Moving Charge in a Magnetic Field (Lorentz Force)

When a charged particle of charge q moves with velocity v in a magnetic field B, it experiences a magnetic force:

F = q(v × B)

Magnitude: F = qvB sin θ, where θ is the angle between v and B.
Direction: Given by the right-hand rule for positive charges — point fingers along v, curl toward B; thumb gives force direction. For negative charges (electrons), the force is opposite to the thumb direction.

Key properties of the magnetic force:
• It is always perpendicular to both v and B.
• It does no work on the particle (W = F·d = 0 since F ⊥ v). Hence, a magnetic field cannot change the speed (kinetic energy) of a charged particle — only its direction.
• If v is parallel or anti-parallel to B (θ = 0° or 180°): F = 0; the particle moves undeviated.
• If v is perpendicular to B (θ = 90°): F is maximum, F = qvB.

Motion in a uniform magnetic field (v ⟂ B):
The particle moves in a circle. Equating magnetic force to centripetal force:
qvB = mv²/R → R = mv/(qB) = p/(qB)
where p = mv is the momentum.

Time period of revolution:
T = 2πR/v = 2πm/(qB)
Notice: T is independent of both v and R (for non-relativistic speeds).
Frequency (cyclotron frequency):
f = 1/T = qB/(2πm)

Motion when v makes an angle θ with B:
Decompose v into v_parallel = v cos θ (along B) and v_perp = v sin θ (perpendicular to B).
• The perpendicular component causes circular motion.
• The parallel component is unaffected (no force along B).
• Result: a helical path.

Radius of helix: R = mv sin θ/(qB)
Pitch (distance advanced per turn): p = v cos θ × T = 2πmv cos θ/(qB)

Cyclotron — Principle, Construction and Limitations

A cyclotron is a device that accelerates charged particles (protons, deuterons, α-particles) to high energies using a combination of a magnetic field and an oscillating electric field.

Principle:
A charged particle moving perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field executes circular motion. By applying an electric field across a gap at the right moment (resonance condition), the particle gains energy each time it crosses the gap, spiralling outward with increasing radius until it exits at the periphery.

Resonance condition (cyclotron condition):
The frequency of the applied RF electric field must equal the cyclotron frequency of the particle:
f_RF = qB/(2πm)
This ensures the particle is always accelerated as it crosses the gap.

Construction highlights:
• Two hollow D-shaped electrodes (Dees) placed back-to-back with a small gap.
• The Dees are placed between the poles of a strong electromagnet.
• An oscillating potential difference is applied across the gap.
• Particles are injected near the centre.

Maximum kinetic energy:
When the particle reaches the outermost radius R_max:
K_max = q²B²R_max² / (2m)

Limitations:
• Cannot accelerate electrons effectively because their small mass means they reach relativistic speeds quickly, changing their mass and breaking the resonance condition.
• Cannot accelerate neutral particles (neutrons) since they carry no charge.
• Relativistic mass increase at very high energies limits the maximum achievable energy.

JEE numerical pattern: Given B, R_max, and particle type, find K_max or vice versa.

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor and Between Parallel Wires

Force on a straight current-carrying conductor in a uniform magnetic field:

F = I(l × B)
Magnitude: F = BIl sin θ, where l is the length vector (direction = current), and θ is the angle between l and B.
Direction: Fleming's Left-Hand Rule — stretch thumb, forefinger, and middle finger mutually perpendicular. Forefinger → B, middle finger → I, thumb → F.
Special cases:
• θ = 90° (wire perpendicular to B): F = BIl (maximum)
• θ = 0° or 180° (wire parallel to B): F = 0
• Curved wire in uniform B: The force equals that on a straight wire connecting the endpoints: F = I(L_eff × B), where L_eff is the vector from start to end.

Force between two parallel current-carrying conductors:
Two long parallel wires separated by distance d, carrying currents I₁ and I₂:
Force per unit length: F/l = μ₀I₁I₂ / (2πd)
• Currents in the same direction → attraction.
• Currents in opposite directions → repulsion.

This relation is historically the basis for the definition of the ampere (before the 2019 SI redefinition).

Torque on a rectangular current loop in a uniform magnetic field:
τ = NIAB sin φ
where:
• N = number of turns
• I = current
• A = area of the loop
• B = magnetic field strength
• φ = angle between the normal to the loop and the field direction
In vector form: τ = m × B, where m = NI A is the magnetic dipole moment.
Maximum torque when φ = 90° (plane of loop parallel to B): τ_max = NIAB
Zero torque when φ = 0° or 180° (equilibrium positions).

Moving Coil Galvanometer — Principle, Construction and Calculations

A moving coil galvanometer (MCG) is an instrument used to detect and measure small electric currents.

Principle:
A current-carrying coil placed in a magnetic field experiences a torque. In a MCG, this torque is balanced by a restoring torque from a spring. The deflection is proportional to the current.

Construction:
• A rectangular/square coil of N turns and area A is suspended between the pole pieces of a permanent magnet.
• The magnet poles are curved to produce a radial magnetic field (B always perpendicular to the plane of the coil).
• A soft iron core is placed inside the coil to strengthen the field and make it radial.
• Phosphor-bronze springs provide the restoring torque and also carry current to/from the coil.
• A mirror attached to the suspension helps in reading small deflections (lamp-and-scale arrangement).

Key formulas:
• Deflecting torque: τ_d = NIAB
• Restoring torque: τ_r = kθ (k = torsional constant of the spring)
• At equilibrium: NIAB = kθ
• Current sensitivity: S_I = θ/I = NAB/k (deflection per unit current)
• Voltage sensitivity: S_V = θ/V = NAB/(kR) (deflection per unit voltage, R = coil resistance)

Important observations:
• Radial field ensures θ ∝ I (linear scale).
• Soft iron core increases B and makes the field radial.
• Current sensitivity can be increased by increasing N, A, B or decreasing k.
• However, increasing N or A also increases the moment of inertia and may slow down the response.
• Voltage sensitivity depends on coil resistance R — reducing R improves S_V if other factors are held constant.

Magnetic Dipole and Bar Magnet — Equivalent Solenoid Concept

A magnetic dipole consists of two equal and opposite magnetic poles separated by a small distance. The magnetic dipole moment is:
m = q_m × 2l (where q_m is pole strength and 2l is the separation)
Alternatively, for a current loop: m = NI A (same dimensional units: A·m² or J/T).

Field due to a bar magnet (magnetic dipole) at a point on its axis (end-on position), distance r from centre (r >> l):
B_axis = (μ₀/4π) × (2m/r³)

Field at a point on the equatorial line (broad-side-on position), distance r from centre:
B_eq = (μ₀/4π) × (m/r³)
Direction of B_eq is opposite to m.

Torque on a magnetic dipole in a uniform external field:
τ = m × B, magnitude τ = mB sin θ
Potential energy: U = -m·B = -mB cos θ
• Stable equilibrium: θ = 0° (m aligned with B), U = -mB (minimum)
• Unstable equilibrium: θ = 180° (m opposite to B), U = +mB (maximum)
Work done in rotating from θ₁ to θ₂:
W = mB(cos θ₂ - cos θ₁)

Bar magnet as an equivalent solenoid:
A bar magnet and a solenoid produce identical magnetic fields externally. This was established experimentally and theoretically. The equivalence means:
• Both have a North and South pole.
• Both produce a dipole field at large distances.
• Cutting a bar magnet transversely gives weaker magnets (not isolated poles), just as cutting a solenoid gives shorter solenoids with proportionally reduced moment.

Unlike electric dipoles, magnetic monopoles have never been observed — magnetic poles always exist in pairs.

Magnetic Materials and Their Classification

Magnetic materials are classified based on their response to an external magnetic field, characterized by magnetic susceptibility (χ_m) and relative permeability (μᵣ = 1 + χ_m).

Diamagnetic materials:
• Weakly repelled by a magnetic field.
• χ_m is small and negative (typically -10⁻⁵ to -10⁻⁹).
• μᵣ is slightly less than 1.
• Examples: copper, gold, bismuth, water, noble gases.
• Property: expel magnetic field lines slightly (partial Meissner effect analog in superconductors is perfect diamagnetism).
• Independent of temperature (mostly).

Paramagnetic materials:
• Weakly attracted by a magnetic field.
• χ_m is small and positive (typically 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻³).
• μᵣ is slightly greater than 1.
• Examples: aluminium, platinum, sodium, liquid oxygen.
• obey Curie's Law: χ_m ∝ 1/T (susceptibility decreases with increasing temperature).

Ferromagnetic materials:
• Strongly attracted by a magnetic field.
• χ_m is very large and positive (hundreds to thousands).
• μᵣ >> 1.
• Examples: iron, cobalt, nickel, gadolinium.
• Exhibit hysteresis (B does not retrace the same path when H is cycled).
• Above the Curie temperature (T_c), ferromagnetic materials become paramagnetic.
• Domain theory: microscopic regions called domains are spontaneously magnetized; in an unmagnetized sample, domains are randomly oriented.

Additional concepts:
• Retentivity: residual magnetism after removing the external field.
• Coercivity: reverse field needed to demagnetize the material.
• Soft magnetic materials (low retentivity, low coercivity): used in transformers, electromagnets.
• Hard magnetic materials (high retentivity, high coercivity): used to make permanent magnets.
• Magneto-optic effects (Faraday rotation) are occasionally asked in advanced contexts.

Earth's Magnetism — Elements, Vector Rules and Calculations

Earth behaves like a giant magnetic dipole tilted approximately 11.3° from the geographic axis.

Three elements of Earth's magnetic field at any location:
1. Magnetic declination (δ): angle between geographic north and magnetic north in the horizontal plane.
2. Magnetic inclination/dip (I or θ): angle made by the total Earth's field with the horizontal in the magnetic meridian.
• At the magnetic poles: I = 90° (field is vertical)
• At the magnetic equator: I = 0° (field is horizontal)
3. Horizontal component of Earth's field (B_H): the horizontal projection of the total field.

Vector relationships:
B_H = B cos I
Vertical component: B_V = B sin I
Total field: B = √(B_H² + B_V²)

Given B_H at a location where dip = I:
B_H = B cos I, so B = B_H / cos I
If B_H is known at one place and the dip angle changes, B_H' = B_H × (cos I'/cos I) only if B remains the same (which it doesn't across locations).

Typical values (approximate):
• B_H at Earth's surface: 0.3 × 10⁻⁴ T to 0.5 × 10⁻⁴ T (30–50 microtesla)
• Total field at poles: ~6 × 10⁻⁵ T
• Total field at equator: ~3 × 10⁻⁵ T

Aircraft/ship compass correction:
Declination must be accounted for when navigating with a magnetic compass. A positive declination means magnetic north is east of true north.

Neutral points: Where the field of a magnet exactly cancels Earth's horizontal component. These occur along the equatorial line of a horizontal bar magnet with its North pointing geographic North.

Solved Worked Examples (JEE Main Pattern)

Example 1: Two parallel wires
Two long parallel wires carry currents I₁ = 5 A and I₂ = 3 A in the same direction, separated by 0.1 m. Find the force per unit length on each wire and state whether it is attractive or repulsive.

Solution:
F/l = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd) = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 5 × 3)/(2π × 0.1) = (60π × 10⁻⁷)/(2π × 0.1) = 3 × 10⁻⁵ N/m
Same direction → attractive force.

Example 2: Electron in a magnetic field
An electron moves with speed 2 × 10⁶ m/s perpendicular to a magnetic field of 0.5 T. Find the radius of its circular path. (m_e = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)

Solution:
R = mv/(eB) = (9.1 × 10⁻³¹ × 2 × 10⁶)/(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.5)
= (18.2 × 10⁻²⁵)/(0.8 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 2.275 × 10⁻⁵ m ≈ 22.8 μm

Example 3: Toroid field
A toroid has 500 turns, mean radius 0.1 m, and carries 2 A. Find B at the mean radius. (μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷)

Solution:
B = μ₀NI/(2πr) = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 500 × 2)/(2π × 0.1) = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 1000)/(0.2π)
= 2 × 10⁻³ T = 2 mT

Example 4: Galvanometer sensitivity
A galvanometer has N = 100 turns, area A = 2 cm² = 2 × 10⁻⁴ m², B = 0.5 T, and torsional constant k = 10⁻⁸ N·m/rad. Find current sensitivity.

Solution:
S_I = NAB/k = (100 × 2 × 10⁻⁴ × 0.5)/(10⁻⁸) = (10⁻²)/(10⁻⁸) = 10⁶ rad/A

Common Mistakes and Exam-Traps

1. Wrong direction rule: Using Fleming's Left-Hand Rule for field direction instead of Right-Hand Thumb Rule. LHR gives force direction; RHR gives field direction. Keep them separate.

2. Confusing axial vs equatorial field of a bar magnet: B_axis = 2B_eq at the same distance (far field). Many students write them equal.

3. Forgetting sin θ in F = qvB sin θ: Writing F = qvB even when v is not perpendicular to B.

4. Using parallel-wire formula incorrectly: The formula F/l = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd) requires d in metres and currents in amperes. Entering cm directly gives a 100× error.

5. Solenoid vs toroid mix-up: Using B = μ₀nI for a toroid (wrong) or B = μ₀NI/(2πr) for a solenoid (wrong). Remember: solenoid = uniform inside; toroid = varies as 1/r.

6. Cyclotron frequency dependence: Forgetting that f = qB/(2πm) is independent of radius and speed (non-relativistic).

7. Earth's magnetism angle confusion: Writing B_H = B sin I instead of B cos I. Remember: dip I is measured from horizontal, so the adjacent side (horizontal) uses cosine.

8. Galvanometer as ammeter/voltmeter: Forgetting that shunt resistance is connected in parallel (ammeter) and series resistance is added in series (voltmeter). A galvanometer has high resistance — converting it to an ammeter requires a very low shunt.

9. Work done by magnetic force: Thinking magnetic force does work. It never does — it only changes direction, not speed.

10. Finite wire formula angles: Using the angle from the wire instead of from the perpendicular. The standard formula uses angles measured from the perpendicular dropped from P to the wire.

Important facts

  • Biot-Savart Law is the fundamental law for calculating magnetic fields due to steady currents; it has an inverse-square dependence on distance and an angular sin θ factor.
  • Magnetic field lines due to a straight wire form concentric circles; due to a circular loop they resemble a dipole field at large distances.
  • Inside an ideal solenoid, B = μ₀nI is uniform and independent of the cross-sectional area.
  • A toroid has zero magnetic field outside it; this distinguishes it from a solenoid which has a small external field.
  • Magnetic force never does work on a charged particle; it only changes the direction of velocity, not the speed.
  • The cyclotron frequency f = qB/(2πm) is independent of the particle's speed and orbital radius (non-relativistically).
  • Currents in the same direction in parallel wires attract; opposite directions repel.
  • A moving coil galvanometer converts current into angular deflection; current sensitivity S_I = NAB/k.
  • Earth's magnetic dip angle is 90° at the magnetic poles and 0° at the magnetic equator.
  • Ferromagnetic materials lose their ferromagnetic property above the Curie temperature and become paramagnetic.
  • μ₀/4π = 10⁻⁷ T·m/A — this constant appears in all Biot-Savart and Ampere-law calculations.
  • The magnetic dipole moment of a current loop m = NI A is analogous to an electric dipole moment p = qd.
