Introduction to Mechanics and Reference Frames

Mechanics is the branch of physics dealing with the motion of objects and the forces that cause or change that motion. It is divided into two sub-branches: kinematics (description of motion without reference to its causes) and dynamics (study of motion considering its causes, i.e., forces). Dynamics is further divided into statics (bodies at rest) and kinetics (bodies in motion).

A reference frame is essential for describing motion. An inertial reference frame is one in which Newton's First Law holds true — a body not acted upon by any external force remains at rest or moves with constant velocity. Any frame moving with constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial. A non-inertial reference frame is one that is accelerating (linearly or rotationally) relative to an inertial frame; in such frames, Newton's Laws do not hold directly unless pseudo forces are introduced. For JEE Main, the key distinction is that all frames fixed to the Earth are approximately inertial for most problems, while frames attached to accelerating vehicles require pseudo forces.

Newton's First Law of Motion and Inertia

Newton's First Law states: Every body continues in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line unless compelled by some external force to act otherwise. This law is also called the Law of Inertia. Inertia is the inherent property of a body by virtue of which it resists any change in its state of rest or uniform motion. Mass is the measure of inertia — a greater mass means greater resistance to change in motion.

Three types of inertia are recognised: (a) Inertia of rest — the tendency of a body to remain at rest. Example: when a bus suddenly starts, passengers fall backward because their lower body moves with the bus while the upper body tends to remain at rest. (b) Inertia of motion — the tendency of a body to continue in uniform motion. Example: when a moving bus brakes suddenly, passengers fall forward. (c) Inertia of direction — the tendency of a body to maintain its direction of motion. Example: mud flies tangentially from a rotating wheel; passengers lean outward when a car takes a sharp turn.

Common misconception: a force is NOT needed to keep a body moving at constant velocity. Force is only needed to CHANGE the state of motion (i.e., to produce acceleration). In the absence of friction, a body moving on a horizontal surface would continue forever without any applied force.

Newton's Second Law of Motion and Linear Momentum

Newton's Second Law quantifies force. It states that the rate of change of linear momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied external force and takes place in the direction of the force. Mathematically:

F = dp/dt

where p = mv is the linear momentum (a vector quantity measured in kg·m/s), and F is the net external force.

For constant mass, this reduces to the familiar form: F = ma, where a is acceleration and m is mass. This is the working equation used in nearly all JEE problems.

Impulse (J) is defined as the product of average force and the time interval during which it acts: J = F_avg × Δt. From the impulse-momentum theorem: J = Δp = m(v - u). Impulse has units of N·s or kg·m/s, same as momentum. This concept is critical in collision problems.

The SI unit of force is the newton (N): 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². In CGS, the unit is dyne: 1 N = 10⁵ dyne.

Variable mass systems: When mass changes with time (e.g., a rocket), the full form F = dp/dt = m(dv/dt) + v(dm/dt) must be used. For a rocket ejecting gas at relative velocity v_rel: m(dv/dt) = -v_rel(dm/dt), leading to the rocket equation v = v_rel ln(m₀/m). This is a frequent JEE topic.

Newton's Third Law of Motion and Internal/External Forces

Newton's Third Law states: To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. More precisely, if body A exerts a force on body B, then body B simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction on body A. These action-reaction pairs always act on DIFFERENT bodies and therefore never cancel each other out in the free body diagram of either body.

Important clarifications often tested:
- Action and reaction act simultaneously; neither precedes the other.
- They act along the same line (collinear).
- They are of the SAME nature (both gravitational, both contact, etc.).
- They do NOT cancel each other because they act on different bodies.

Forces are classified as internal or external depending on the system chosen. Internal forces are forces between particles within the system; they cancel in pairs and do not affect the motion of the system's centre of mass. External forces are forces exerted on the system by bodies outside it; these determine the acceleration of the centre of mass via F_ext = M·a_cm.

Common error: students often draw both action and reaction on the same free body diagram. Remember — each free body diagram shows ONLY forces acting ON that particular body.

Free Body Diagrams and Equilibrium of Concurrent Forces

A Free Body Diagram (FBD) is the single most important tool in solving mechanics problems. It is a sketch showing the body of interest isolated from its surroundings, with ALL external forces acting on it represented as vectors originating from the body.

Steps to draw a correct FBD:
1. Identify the body/object you want to analyse and isolate it mentally.
2. Draw all forces acting ON that body — gravity (mg downward from centre of mass), tension (along the string/rope away from the body), normal reaction (perpendicular to the surface, directed toward the body), friction (parallel to surface, opposing relative motion or tendency of motion), spring force (along the spring, restoring direction).
3. Choose convenient coordinate axes (typically align one axis with the direction of motion or acceleration).
4. Resolve all forces into components along the chosen axes.
5. Apply Newton's Second Law component-wise: ΣF_x = ma_x and ΣF_y = ma_y.

For equilibrium (a = 0): ΣF_x = 0 and ΣF_y = 0. For three concurrent forces in equilibrium, Lami's Theorem applies: each force is proportional to the sine of the angle opposite to it: F₁/sinα = F₂/sinβ = F₃/sinγ.

Common mistakes: drawing reaction forces that the body exerts on other objects; forgetting weight (always present near Earth's surface); assigning wrong direction to friction; including components of forces that don't act on the chosen body.

Types of Forces and Spring Force

Forces in classical mechanics can be categorised as follows:

Contact forces:
- Normal reaction (N): electromagnetic in origin, always perpendicular to the contact surface. Its magnitude adjusts automatically to prevent interpenetration of surfaces. It is a self-adjusting force up to its limiting value.
- Friction (f): opposes relative motion or tendency of relative motion between surfaces in contact. Parallel to the surface.
- Tension (T): pulling force transmitted by a string, rope, or cable. Acts along the string away from the body. Ideal strings are massless and inextensible; tension is uniform throughout such a string.
- Applied force: any external push or pull.

Non-contact (field) forces:
- Gravitational force (weight): W = mg, directed toward the centre of Earth.
- Electrostatic and magnetic forces (not extensively covered in Laws of Motion but appear in combined problems).

Spring force (Hooke's Law): F_spring = -kx, where k is the spring constant (N/m) and x is the displacement from natural length. The negative sign indicates the force is restoring (opposite to displacement). Springs in series: 1/k_eq = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂. Springs in parallel: k_eq = k₁ + k₂. Important: a spring cannot change its force instantaneously — the extension/compression cannot change discontinuously.

Constraint forces: tension in strings and normal reactions are constraint forces whose magnitudes depend on the specific situation and are determined by applying Newton's laws along with kinematic constraints.

Friction — Concept, Types, and Formulas

Friction is the force that opposes relative motion (or tendency of relative motion) between two surfaces in contact. It arises due to electromagnetic interactions between surface molecules and surface irregularities.

Types of friction:
(a) Static friction (f_s): acts when there is NO relative motion between surfaces. It is a self-adjusting force: f_s = F_applied (up to a maximum). The maximum static friction is f_s(max) = μ_s N, where μ_s is the coefficient of static friction and N is the normal reaction.
(b) Kinetic friction (f_k): acts when surfaces ARE sliding relative to each other. f_k = μ_k N, where μ_k is the coefficient of kinetic friction. Generally μ_k < μ_s.
(c) Rolling friction: much smaller than sliding friction; opposes rolling motion.

Key relationships:
- Coefficient of friction: μ = f/N (dimensionless)
- Angle of friction (λ): tan λ = μ_s = f_s(max)/N. This is the angle between the resultant of limiting friction and normal reaction.
- Angle of repose (θ): the minimum inclination of a plane at which a body just begins to slide. For a body on an inclined plane, θ = λ (angle of repose equals angle of friction).

Direction of friction: always opposes the relative motion (or tendency thereof) between the contacting surfaces. For a body on a rough inclined plane trying to slide down, friction acts UP the incline.

Friction on accelerating bodies: When a block rests on a plank and the plank accelerates, static friction provides the force accelerating the block. The maximum acceleration without slipping is a_max = μ_s g (on a horizontal surface).

Common errors: assuming f = μN always (this is only true for limiting/static max friction); taking friction direction as opposite to velocity rather than opposite to relative motion between surfaces.

Motion on Rough Inclined Plane and Horizontal Surface

Inclined plane problems are among the most frequently tested in JEE Main. Consider a block of mass m on a plane inclined at angle θ to the horizontal.

Resolving weight mg into components: mg sin θ down the plane, mg cos θ perpendicular to the plane.

Normal reaction: N = mg cos θ (when no other vertical forces act).

Case 1: Smooth incline (μ = 0):
- Down the plane: a = g sin θ
- Time to reach bottom from height h: t = √(2h/(g sin²θ))
- Velocity at bottom: v = √(2gh)

Case 2: Rough incline, block sliding DOWN:
a = g(sin θ - μ_k cos θ)
Block slides only if tan θ > μ_s (i.e., θ > angle of repose).

Case 3: Rough incline, block sliding UP:
a = g(sin θ + μ_k cos θ) (deceleration down the plane)
The block will stop and may slide back down.

Case 4: Block on horizontal rough surface, pulled by force F at angle α above horizontal:
N = mg - F sin α (normal reaction is reduced)
f = μN = μ(mg - F sin α)
a = (F cos α - f)/m
Minimum force to initiate motion: F_min = μmg/√(1 + μ²) at optimum angle α = tan⁻¹μ.

Case 5: Two blocks on inclined plane connected by string — identify whether string is taut by checking if the natural accelerations (if unconnected) would cause them to separate or overlap.

Connected Bodies — Pulleys, Strings, and Constraints

Connected body problems involve two or more bodies linked by strings, pulleys, or rods. The key is to write equations for each body separately using FBDs and then solve simultaneously, applying constraint relations.

Common configurations:

1. Single pulley (Atwood machine): Two masses m₁ and m₂ (m₁ > m₂) connected by a light inextensible string over a smooth fixed pulley.
Acceleration: a = (m₁ - m₂)g/(m₁ + m₂)
Tension: T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁ + m₂)
Distance travelled in time t: s = ½at²

2. Double pulley systems: Carefully trace the string to find constraint relations. If one end is fixed and the pulley moves, the acceleration relations follow from the inextensibility condition.

3. Blocks on horizontal/rough surfaces connected by string: Apply Newton's law to each block. For blocks A and B connected by a string on a rough horizontal surface with applied force F on A:
For A: F - T - f_A = m_A a
For B: T - f_B = m_B a
Solve for a and T.

4. Constraint equations: For inextensible strings, the magnitude of acceleration of all points on the string is the same. If a string passes over a fixed pulley, the two attached bodies have equal magnitude of acceleration (but possibly different directions). If one end is fixed to a wall and the other end is pulled, the pulley attached to the object moves half the distance the string end moves.

5. Wedge constraints: When a block slides on a movable wedge, the horizontal displacements satisfy: m_block · x_block_relative + m_wedge · x_wedge = 0 (centre of mass constraint if no external horizontal force).

Pseudo Force in Non-Inertial Reference Frames

When solving problems from a non-inertial (accelerating) reference frame, Newton's Laws appear to be violated because objects accelerate without any real force acting on them. To restore the validity of Newton's Laws in such frames, we introduce a pseudo force (also called a fictitious or inertial force).

Pseudo force formula: F_pseudo = -m × a_frame, where a_frame is the acceleration of the non-inertial frame relative to an inertial frame. The pseudo force acts on every body in the non-inertial frame in the direction OPPOSITE to the frame's acceleration.

Key rules:
- Pseudo force = m × (magnitude of frame's acceleration), directed opposite to the frame's acceleration.
- It acts through the centre of mass of the body.
- It is NOT a real force (no reaction pair exists per Newton's Third Law).
- Only use pseudo forces when working from the perspective of the accelerating frame.

Common applications:
1. Block on floor of accelerating train (acceleration a to right): From the train's frame, a pseudo force ma acts on the block to the left. If friction is sufficient (μ ≥ a/g), the block remains at rest relative to the train.
2. Pendulum in accelerating vehicle: A pendulum hanging in a vehicle accelerating horizontally with acceleration a makes an angle θ = tan⁻¹(a/g) with the vertical (toward the rear).
3. Accelerating wedge with a block on it: Analysing from the wedge's frame introduces a pseudo force on the block, simplifying the problem significantly.
4. Lift problems: In a lift accelerating upward with acceleration a, apparent weight = m(g + a). In a lift accelerating downward, apparent weight = m(g - a). In free fall (a = g), apparent weight = 0 (weightlessness).

Circular Motion and Applications of Newton's Laws

Circular motion requires a centripetal (center-seeking) acceleration a_c = v²/r = ω²r directed toward the centre of the circle. By Newton's Second Law, this requires a net centripetal force F_c = mv²/r = mω²r, provided by real forces depending on the situation.

Common circular motion scenarios:

1. Horizontal circular motion (string):
Tension provides centripetal force: T = mv²/r
If the string makes angle θ with vertical (conical pendulum):
T cos θ = mg (vertical equilibrium)
T sin θ = mv²/r (horizontal centripetal)
→ tan θ = v²/(rg), period T_period = 2π√(l cos θ/g)

2. Banked curves (no friction):
A road banked at angle θ for speed v: tan θ = v²/(rg)
With friction (coefficient μ): the safe speed range is
v_min = √[rg(μ - tan θ)/(1 + μ tan θ)] (if μ > tan θ)
v_max = √[rg(μ + tan θ)/(1 - μ tan θ)] (if μ < cot θ)

3. Vertical circular motion (string/rod):
For a particle of mass m attached to a string of length r:
At lowest point: T_bottom - mg = mv_bottom²/r → T_bottom = mv_bottom²/r + mg
At highest point: T_top + mg = mv_top²/r → T_top = mv_top²/r - mg
Minimum velocity at top to complete the circle (string): v_top(min) = √(gr)
Minimum velocity at bottom: v_bottom(min) = √(5gr)
Energy conservation: ½mv_bottom² = ½mv_top² + mg(2r)
For a rigid rod, v_top(min) = 0 (rod can support compression).

4. Car on convex/concave bridge:
Convex bridge (top): N = mg - mv²/r (normal reaction decreases)
Concave bridge (bottom): N = mg + mv²/r (normal reaction increases)
Loss of contact on convex bridge when N = 0, i.e., v = √(rg)

Apparent Weight and Elevator Problems

Apparent weight is the normal reaction force experienced by a body resting on a supporting surface. It differs from the true weight (mg) when the support is accelerating.

Elevator (lift) problems — body of mass m standing on a weighing scale in a lift:

Case 1: Lift at rest or moving with uniform velocity (a = 0):
Apparent weight W' = N = mg = True weight

Case 2: Lift accelerating upward with acceleration a:
N - mg = ma → N = m(g + a)
Apparent weight > True weight (person feels heavier)

Case 3: Lift accelerating downward with acceleration a:
mg - N = ma → N = m(g - a)
Apparent weight < True weight (person feels lighter)

Case 4: Lift in free fall (a = g):
N = m(g - g) = 0
Weightlessness — person floats inside the lift

Case 5: Lift accelerating downward with a > g (e.g., cable cut):
The body loses contact with the floor; apparent weight = 0

General rule: if the support accelerates upward (relative to ground), apparent weight increases. If downward, it decreases.

Spring balance in elevator: reads the tension in the spring, which equals the apparent weight of the suspended mass.

Two-body elevator problem: Two masses m₁ and m₂ connected by a string over a pulley inside an accelerating lift. From the lift's frame, use effective gravity g' = g ± a and apply Atwood machine formulas with g' instead of g.

Maximum Acceleration Without Slipping — Common Scenarios

A recurring JEE question type asks for the maximum acceleration a system can have without relative slipping between surfaces. The limiting condition is always f_s(max) = μ_s N.

Scenario 1: Block on a rough horizontal plank, both accelerated together.
Maximum acceleration of the system without slipping: a_max = μ_s g
(Derivation: friction on block = μ_s N = μ_s mg = ma → a = μ_s g)

Scenario 2: Block on an accelerating wedge (smooth wedge, block doesn't slip).
From wedge's frame with pseudo force: the block stays at rest relative to wedge when the component of pseudo force down the incline balances the component of weight down the incline.
a = g tan θ

Scenario 3: Two blocks stacked, lower block pulled.
If friction between blocks = μ₁ and between lower block and ground = μ₂:
Max acceleration of upper block without slipping: a₁(max) = μ₁ g
The entire system's max acceleration is limited by the smaller of the two limiting values.

Scenario 4: Truck carrying a cabinet (friction coefficient μ).
Max acceleration of truck without cabinet sliding: a_max = μg

Scenario 5: Rotating disc with a coin placed on it.
Max angular speed without slipping: ω_max = √(μg/r)
Max linear speed: v_max = √(μgr)

Scenario 6: Car rounding a curve of radius r on flat road.
Max speed without skidding: v_max = √(μrg)

Key insight: in all cases, identify which surface has the limiting friction and set f_s(max) = ma to find the maximum acceleration.

Worked Numerical Examples for JEE Main

Example 1: A block of mass 5 kg rests on a rough horizontal surface (μ_s = 0.4, μ_k = 0.3). A horizontal force of 15 N is applied.
Solution: Maximum static friction = μ_s mg = 0.4 × 5 × 10 = 20 N. Since applied force (15 N) < limiting friction (20 N), the block does not move. Friction = 15 N (self-adjusting). Acceleration = 0.

Example 2: A 2 kg block is placed on a rough incline (μ = 0.5, θ = 37°). Find acceleration.
Solution: mg sin θ = 2 × 10 × 0.6 = 12 N (down the plane)
Limiting friction = μ mg cos θ = 0.5 × 2 × 10 × 0.8 = 8 N
Since mg sin θ > μ mg cos θ (12 > 8), block slides down.
Net force = 12 - 8 = 4 N
Acceleration = 4/2 = 2 m/s² down the incline.

Example 3: Two masses m₁ = 4 kg and m₂ = 2 kg are connected by a light string over a smooth pulley (Atwood machine). Find acceleration and tension.
Solution: a = (m₁ - m₂)g/(m₁ + m₂) = (4-2)×10/6 = 20/6 = 10/3 m/s² ≈ 3.33 m/s²
T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁+m₂) = 2×4×2×10/6 = 160/6 = 80/3 N ≈ 26.67 N

Example 4: A car moves on a horizontal circular track of radius 100 m. If μ = 0.2, find the maximum speed without skidding.
Solution: v_max = √(μrg) = √(0.2 × 100 × 10) = √200 = 10√2 ≈ 14.14 m/s

Example 5: A 60 kg person stands on a weighing scale in a lift. Find the reading when the lift (a) moves up with a = 2 m/s², (b) moves down with a = 2 m/s², (c) is in free fall.
Solution: (a) N = m(g+a) = 60×12 = 720 N
(b) N = m(g-a) = 60×8 = 480 N
(c) N = m(g-g) = 0 N (weightlessness)

Important JEE-Main-Specific Facts and Exam-Weightage Notes

Laws of Motion is one of the highest-weightage chapters in JEE Main Physics. Expect 2-4 questions directly from this chapter, often combined with work-energy or friction concepts.

Must-master formulas:
- F_net = ma (fundamental equation)
- J = Δp = m(v - u) (impulse-momentum)
- f_s ≤ μ_s N (static friction inequality)
- f_k = μ_k N (kinetic friction)
- a_max without slipping = μ_s g
- v_top(min) for vertical circle = √(gr) (string)
- v_bottom(min) for vertical circle = √(5gr) (string)
- Apparent weight in elevator = m(g ± a)
- Banking angle: tan θ = v²/(rg)
- Pseudo force = ma_frame (opposite to frame acceleration)

High-yield problem types for JEE Main:
1. Multiple blocks on rough surface with friction between them
2. Pulley systems with friction
3. Lift/elevator problems with spring balances
4. Vehicle on banked roads with/without friction
5. Variable mass (rocket) — basic level
6. Circular motion in vertical plane
7. Pseudo force problems in accelerating frames

Common traps in JEE Main:
- Confusing static and kinetic friction — check whether surfaces are actually sliding before using μ_k
- Forgetting that friction can act in the direction of motion (e.g., walking, acceleration of a car — friction on driving wheels acts forward)
- In Atwood machines, mixing up which mass goes up vs down
- In vertical circular motion, using √(gr) for the bottom point instead of √(5gr)
- Not accounting for reduced normal reaction when a force is applied at an angle on a horizontal surface

Important facts

  • Newton's First Law defines inertia and inertial reference frames; a body maintains its state of rest or uniform motion unless acted upon by a net external force.
  • Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia; more mass means more resistance to change in motion.
  • Newton's Second Law: F = dp/dt; for constant mass, F = ma. Impulse J = Δp = F_avg × Δt.
  • Newton's Third Law: action-reaction pairs are equal, opposite, collinear, simultaneous, and act on DIFFERENT bodies — they never cancel in a single FBD.
  • Static friction is self-adjusting: f_s = F_applied up to f_s(max) = μ_s N. Kinetic friction is constant: f_k = μ_k N, typically μ_k < μ_s.
  • Angle of friction λ and angle of repose θ both satisfy tan λ = tan θ = μ_s.
  • In an accelerating frame, pseudo force F_pseudo = -ma_frame acts on every body in the direction opposite to the frame's acceleration.
  • For a block on a rough horizontal surface, maximum acceleration without slipping is a_max = μ_s g.
  • Vertical circular motion (string): minimum speed at top = √(gr); minimum speed at bottom = √(5gr). For a rigid rod, minimum speed at top = 0.
  • Apparent weight in an elevator: upward acceleration → m(g+a); downward acceleration → m(g-a); free fall → 0 (weightlessness).
  • Banked curve without friction: tan θ = v²/(rg). With friction, there is a range of safe speeds [v_min, v_max].
  • Atwood machine: a = (m₁ - m₂)g/(m₁ + m₂); T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁ + m₂).
  • Spring force obeys Hooke's Law: F = -kx. Springs in series: 1/k_eq = Σ1/k_i. Springs in parallel: k_eq = Σk_i.
  • Constraint relation for inextensible strings: the magnitude of acceleration is the same for all parts of the string; pulley systems require careful tracing of string length.
  • Rocket equation (variable mass): v = v_rel ln(m₀/m), derived from F = dp/dt = m(dv/dt) + v(dm/dt).

Common mistakes

  • Assuming friction always equals μN — this is only true for limiting static friction or kinetic friction. On a rough surface with no impending slip, static friction equals the applied force (self-adjusting), which may be less than μ_s N.
  • Drawing action and reaction forces on the same free body diagram — they act on different bodies and should never both appear in one FBD.
  • Confusing apparent weight with actual weight — apparent weight is the normal reaction force, not mg. A person in an accelerating lift has the same mg but a different scale reading.
  • Using v = √(gr) for the bottom of a vertical circle — √(gr) is the minimum speed at the TOP. The minimum speed at the BOTTOM is √(5gr), derived from energy conservation.
  • Forgetting that pseudo forces act in the direction OPPOSITE to the acceleration of the reference frame — a common sign error.
  • Treating friction as always opposing motion — friction opposes RELATIVE MOTION between surfaces. For a car accelerating forward, static friction on the driven wheels acts FORWARD (in the direction of motion).
  • Assuming tension is always the same on both sides of a pulley — tension is uniform only for massless, frictionless pulleys with a massless string. Real pulleys with mass/friction create tension differences.
  • Not checking whether a block actually slides on an inclined plane — always compare mg sin θ with μ_s mg cos θ first. If mg sin θ < μ_s mg cos θ, the block remains at rest with friction = mg sin θ.
  • Confusing the angle of friction with the angle of the incline — angle of friction (λ) depends only on μ_s (tan λ = μ_s), while angle of repose (θ) is the incline angle at which sliding begins. They are numerically equal but conceptually distinct.
  • Applying Lami's theorem to non-concurrent forces — Lami's theorem requires exactly three concurrent forces. It cannot be applied to systems with more than three forces or non-concurrent forces.
  • Neglecting the reduction of normal force when a force is applied at an angle on a horizontal surface — if a force F is applied upward at angle θ, N = mg - F sin θ, not mg.
  • Forgetting that in a free-falling elevator, objects inside experience weightlessness but still have mass and inertia — weightlessness means zero apparent weight, not zero mass.
