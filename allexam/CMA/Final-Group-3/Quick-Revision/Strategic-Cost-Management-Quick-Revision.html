Strategic Cost Management — Quick Revision | CMA Final Group 3

CMAEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Strategic Cost Management: The systematic integration of cost accounting information with an organization's strategic planning, formulation, and control to achieve sustainable competitive advantage. It goes beyond traditional cost reduction to examine how costs behave in relation to strategic position.
  • Activity-Based Costing (ABC): A costing methodology that assigns overhead and indirect costs to products and services based on the activities they consume, using multiple cost drivers rather than a single volume-based plant-wide rate.
  • Activity-Based Management (ABM): The use of ABC information to make operational improvements, eliminate non-value-added activities, and improve profitability through better understanding of cost drivers and value creation.
  • Target Costing: A proactive cost management technique where the selling price is determined by the market, and the allowable target cost is derived by deducting the desired profit margin from the selling price. Design and production are then oriented to achieve this target cost.
  • Life Cycle Costing: The cumulative cost of a product or service over its entire life cycle — from R&D and design through production, marketing, distribution, customer service, and eventual disposal — used for pricing, product mix, and investment decisions.
  • Kaizen Costing: A continuous improvement-based costing approach applied during the manufacturing phase, focusing on incremental, ongoing cost reductions through employee involvement, waste elimination, and process optimization.
  • Value Chain Analysis: The identification and evaluation of all primary and support activities that create value for the customer, used to determine where competitive advantage can be achieved and costs can be strategically managed.
  • Balanced Scorecard (BSC): A strategic performance management framework that translates an organization's vision and strategy into a set of interconnected performance measures across four perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Business Process, and Learning & Growth.
  • Throughput Accounting: A managerial accounting approach based on the Theory of Constraints, which defines throughput as the rate at which a system generates money through sales, treats inventory as an asset only when it is sold, and focuses on maximizing throughput per constrained resource.
  • Customer Profitability Analysis (CPA): A method of determining the net profit contributed by each customer or customer segment after allocating all direct and indirect costs, including service, support, and distribution costs specific to that customer.

Key concepts

  • Competitive Advantage Through Cost Leadership vs Differentiation: Michael Porter's generic strategies form the foundation of Strategic Cost Management. A firm pursuing COST LEADERSHIP must structure its value chain to minimize costs at every activity — scale economies, experience curve effects, capacity utilization, and lean operations are critical. A firm pursuing DIFFERENTIATION may accept higher costs if they create unique value perceived by customers. The strategic cost manager must understand which generic strategy the firm follows, as this determines whether cost reduction or value creation takes priority. A BLURRED STRATEGY (stuck in the middle) leads to poor performance — neither low cost nor differentiated enough to command premium prices.
  • Value Chain — Primary and Support Activities: Porter's Value Chain classifies activities into: PRIMARY ACTIVITIES — (1) Inbound Logistics: receiving, storing, and distributing inputs; (2) Operations: transforming inputs into final products; (3) Outbound Logistics: collecting, storing, and distributing products to buyers; (4) Marketing & Sales: enabling buyers to purchase; (5) Service: enhancing or maintaining product value. SUPPORT ACTIVITIES — (1) Procurement: sourcing of inputs; (2) Technology Development: know-how and process technology; (3) Human Resource Management: recruiting, training, compensating; (4) Firm Infrastructure: management, finance, legal. Strategic cost management examines cost relationships BETWEEN activities (e.g., investing in better procurement may reduce inspection costs in inbound logistics).
  • Cost Drivers — Structure vs Execution: Cost structure drivers determine the cost position of an activity: SCALE (extent of investment in an activity), INTEGRITY (degree to which activities are integrated), EXPERIENCE (cumulative volume of output), TECHNOLOGY (choice of process technology), and COMPLEXITY (number of product variants, features). Cost execution drivers relate to how efficiently activities are performed: CAPACITY UTILIZATION, WORKFORCE EFFECTIVENESS, QUALITY MANAGEMENT, SUPPLY CHAIN MANAGEMENT, and PRODUCT DESIGN FOR MANUFACTURABILITY. Strategic cost managers analyze both sets to identify where cost advantages or disadvantages originate.
  • Activity-Based Costing — Steps and Implementation: ABC implementation follows these steps: (1) Identify major activities performed in the organization — e.g., machine setup, quality inspection, material handling, order processing. (2) Assign overhead costs to each activity cost pool using resource drivers (e.g., percentage of time spent, square footage). (3) Identify cost objects (products, services, customers) that consume activities. (4) Calculate activity rates = Activity cost pool ÷ Total activity measure. (5) Allocate overhead to cost objects by multiplying activity rate by the cost object's consumption of the activity. ABC is most beneficial when overhead is a large proportion of total cost, products are diverse, and competition is intense. Traditional costing distorts costs in such environments by using single-volume-based drivers.
  • Activity-Based Management — Value-Added vs Non-Value-Added: ABM uses ABC data for two purposes: OPERATIONAL ABM (short-term efficiency — reducing cost of existing activities) and STRATEGIC ABM (long-term positioning — redesigning the value chain). Every activity is classified as VALUE-ADDED (VA) or NON-VALUE-ADDED (NVA). VA activities directly contribute to what the customer is willing to pay for. NVA activities include inspection, rework, waiting, unnecessary movement, and excess inventory — they consume resources but do not add customer-perceived value. The goal is to eliminate NVA activities entirely, reduce VA activity costs, and improve the ratio of VA to total activity cost.
  • Target Costing — Formula and Process: Target Cost = Expected Selling Price − Desired Profit Margin. The process involves: (1) Determine the market-determined selling price through market research and competitive analysis. (2) Establish the desired profit margin based on the company's return-on-investment objectives. (3) Compute the target cost. (4) Perform value engineering to design the product so that it meets the target cost while delivering required functionality and quality. (5) Monitor actual costs during production against the target and take corrective action. If target cost cannot be met, the product should either be redesigned, re-priced, or discontinued. Target costing shifts cost management upstream to the DESIGN PHASE, which determines 70–80% of total costs.
  • Life Cycle Costing — Phases and Decision Use: The product life cycle encompasses: RESEARCH & DEVELOPMENT → PRODUCT DESIGN → PRODUCTION → MARKETING & DISTRIBUTION → CUSTOMER SERVICE → DISPOSAL/RECYCLING. Costs are incurred differently in each phase — R&D and design involve high development costs with no revenue; production involves manufacturing costs; marketing involves promotion and distribution; service involves warranty and support.   • Kaizen Costing — Continuous Improvement Approach: Kaizen Costing is applied during the MANUFACTURING STAGE (unlike target costing which operates during design). Key features: (1) Focused on incremental, continuous cost reduction rather than radical change. (2) Relies on EMPLOYEE INVOLVEMENT at all levels — shop floor workers identify waste and suggest improvements. (3) Cost reduction targets are set periodically (monthly/quarterly) as small, achievable percentages. (4) Uses standard costing combined with variance analysis to track progress. (5) Emphasizes waste elimination (MUDA) in the three categories: OVERPRODUCTION, WAITING, and TRANSPORT. Kaizen costing works best in stable production environments where processes are well-understood and further efficiency gains are possible through small, cumulative improvements.
  • Balanced Scorecard — Four Perspectives and Strategy Map: The BSC translates strategy into measurable objectives across four interlinked perspectives: (1) FINANCIAL PERSPECTIVE — 'How do we look to shareholders?' Measures: ROCE, revenue growth, cost reduction, economic value added (EVA). (2) CUSTOMER PERSPECTIVE — 'How do customers see us?' Measures: market share, customer satisfaction, customer retention, brand perception. (3) INTERNAL BUSINESS PROCESS PERSPECTIVE — 'What must we excel at?' Measures: cycle time, defect rate, process efficiency, innovation metrics. (4) LEARNING & GROWTH PERSPECTIVE — 'Can we continue to improve and create value?' Measures: employee satisfaction, training hours, information system capability, organizational culture. A STRATEGY MAP shows the cause-and-effect links between objectives across perspectives — e.g., training employees → improved process efficiency → higher customer satisfaction → greater financial returns.
  • Theory of Constraints (TOC) and Throughput Accounting: TOC, developed by Eliyahu Goldratt, states that every system has at least one CONSTRAINT (bottleneck) that limits its output. The five focusing steps are: (1) IDENTIFY the constraint. (2) EXPLOIT the constraint — maximize its utilization without major investment. (3) SUBORDINATE everything else to the constraint — align all other activities to support the bottleneck. (4) ELEVATE the constraint — invest in increasing its capacity. (5) If the constraint is broken, go back to step 1 (avoid inertia). Throughput Accounting metrics: THROUGHPUT = Sales − Totally Variable Costs (direct materials only); INVESTMENT/INVENTORY = all money tied up in the system; OPERATING EXPENSE = all other costs. Key ratios: Throughput per unit of constrained resource, Return per investment hour, and Payback period.
  • Customer Profitability Analysis — Allocation and Decisions: CPA allocates all costs attributable to serving a customer (order processing, delivery, credit management, after-sales service, returns handling) to individual customers or segments. Many customers who appear profitable on the basis of sales alone may actually be UNPROFITABLE after full cost attribution. CPA reveals: UNPOPULAR CUSTOMERS who require excessive service, CUSTOMERS WITH HIGH ORDER FREQUENCY but small quantities, CUSTOMERS REQUIRING CUSTOM SPECIFICATIONS, and CUSTOMERS DEMANDING RAPID DELIVERY. Strategic decisions from CPA: negotiate higher prices, impose minimum order quantities, charge for special services, redesign processes to serve certain customers more efficiently, or discontinue unprofitable customer relationships.
  • Strategic Cost Manager's Role in Decision Making: The strategic cost manager contributes to: (1) STRATEGIC PLANNING — providing cost intelligence about competitors, industry cost structures, and cost positions of alternative strategies. (2) CAPACITY DECISIONS — evaluating long-term capacity requirements using life cycle costing and relevant costing techniques. (3) PRICING DECISIONS — using target costing, value-based pricing, and life cycle pricing to set competitive yet profitable prices. (4) MAKE OR BUY — analyzing total cost of ownership including relationship costs, quality risks, and strategic implications. (5) PRODUCT MIX — determining optimal mix considering constrained resources (TOC approach) and customer profitability. (6) PERFORMANCE EVALUATION — designing KPIs aligned with strategic objectives using BSC.

Important facts

  • ABC was developed by Robert Kaplan and Robin Cooper at Harvard Business School in the 1980s, building on earlier work by Johansen at Stockholm School of Economics.
  • Target Costing originated in Japan in the 1960s at Honda and Canon before being adopted widely in Western industries.
  • Approximately 70–80% of a product's costs are committed during the DESIGN phase, making target costing particularly powerful.
  • Kaizen comes from Japanese words 'Kai' (change) and 'Zen' (good) — meaning 'continuous improvement'.
  • The Balanced Scorecard was developed by Robert Kaplan and David Norton in 1992.
  • Theory of Constraints was developed by Eliyahu Goldratt and introduced in his book 'The Goal' (1984).
  • In ABC, a COST POOL is a bucket in which costs are accumulated before being allocated to cost objects.
  • A COST DRIVER is any factor that causes a change in the cost of an activity — there are two types: RESOURCE DRIVERS (link resources to activities) and ACTIVITY DRIVERS (link activities to cost objects).
  • Under Throughput Accounting, ONLY DIRECT MATERIALS are treated as variable costs; all other costs (including direct labor) are treated as operating expenses.
  • The five focusing steps of TOC must be followed IN SEQUENCE — skipping steps often leads to sub-optimization.
  • Value Engineering is a structured method for improving the value of a product by analyzing function — Value = Function / Cost.
  • A STRATEGY MAP visually displays the cause-and-effect relationships among objectives across the four BSC perspectives.
  • Customer Profitability Analysis is also called Customer Costing or Customer Profit Profiling.
  • In a Balanced Scorecard, lagging indicators (financial results) are linked to leading indicators (process and learning metrics) through causal chains.
  • Life cycle costing reveals that products may be profitable in the growth stage but unprofitable when considering the full life cycle including decline and disposal.

Exam tips

  • CMA Final Strategic Cost Management questions frequently test APPLICATION rather than rote recall. Expect case-based questions where you must identify which technique (ABC, Target Costing, Kaizen, TOC) is appropriate for a given scenario.
  • When calculating ABC rates, always verify: (a) total cost assigned to the activity pool, (b) the correct cost driver, and (c) whether the driver quantity is for the ACTIVITY or for individual cost objects.
  • For Target Costing problems, carefully distinguish between the MARKET DETERMINED selling price and any internally set price. The whole premise is that the market sets the price — not management.
  • In BSC questions, remember that FINANCIAL measures are LAGGING indicators while the other three perspectives contain LEADING indicators. Exam questions often ask you to identify which perspective contains leading vs lagging measures.
  • For TOC throughput accounting calculations, remember: Throughput = Sales Revenue − Totally Variable Costs (materials only). Do NOT subtract labor or overhead from throughput.
  • When asked to evaluate strategic cost management techniques, ALWAYS link your answer to PORTER'S GENERIC STRATEGIES — a cost leadership firm needs ABC and Kaizen; a differentiation firm needs target costing focused on value rather than just cost reduction.
  • In value chain analysis questions, distinguish between PRIMARY activities (physically creating/delivering the product) and SUPPORT activities (enabling primary activities). Misclassification is a common error.
  • For Life Cycle Costing, remember that EARLY stage costs (R&D, design) are SUNK and irreversible — they should not influence future decisions unless they affect future cash flows. This is a key principle tested in decision-making scenarios.
  • In CPA questions, always consider whether costs are TRACEABLE or COMMON. Traceable costs should be directly assigned; common costs should only be allocated if it serves a specific decision purpose — otherwise, they are irrelevant.
  • When writing descriptive answers, structure them using: CONCEPT DEFINITION → RELEVANT STRATEGY → STEPS/PROCEDURE → ADVANTAGES → LIMITATIONS → REAL-WORLD APPLICATION. This format matches the marking scheme expectations of ICMAI.

Common traps

  • Confusing TARGET COSTING with STANDARD COSTING: Target costing is FORWARD-LOOKING (set price first, derive cost) and used during DESIGN; standard costing is BACKWARD-LOOKING (set standard costs, compare with actual) and used during PRODUCTION. They are fundamentally different in timing and purpose.
  • Treating ALL overhead as variable in Throughput Accounting: Under TOC, ONLY direct materials are truly variable; direct labor is treated as a fixed operating expense in the short term. This is a frequent exam error.
  • Using ABC solely for external financial reporting: ABC is primarily an INTERNAL MANAGEMENT TOOL. Using it for external reporting is inefficient and often inappropriate because it increases complexity without proportional benefit.
  • Assuming all activities add value: Many activities in a process are NON-VALUE-ADDED (inspection, rework, storage, movement). The strategic cost manager must classify every activity as VA or NVA before taking improvement actions.
  • Applying Kaizen Costing at the wrong stage: Kaizen costing is for the MANUFACTURING phase only. Applying it to design-stage cost management is incorrect — that is where Target Costing applies. Confusing these two is a classic exam trap.
  • Forgetting that strategy maps show CAUSAL RELATIONSHIPS: A strategy map is not just a list of objectives — it must show how improvement in Learning & Growth leads to better Internal Processes, which improves Customer outcomes, which drives Financial results. Missing the directional arrows is a common mistake.
  • Ignoring the 'Inertia' step in TOC: After elevating a constraint, many organizations fail to restart the cycle at Step 1. The new constraint may be elsewhere, and continuing the old pattern violates TOC principles.
  • Allocating customer costs using arbitrary bases: Customer Profitability Analysis requires traceable costing — costs should be allocated based on ACTUAL consumption (activity-based) rather than revenue or volume proportions, which can mask unprofitable customers.
  • Thinking Life Cycle Costing only tracks costs: Life cycle costing is equally important for tracking REVENUE across stages. Only considering costs without matching revenues leads to incorrect profitability assessments.
