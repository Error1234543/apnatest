Strategic Financial Management — Quick Revision | CMA Final Group 3

CMAEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Strategic Financial Management (SFM) is the integrated management of an organization's investment decisions, financing decisions, and risk exposures across domestic and international markets, with the objective of maximizing shareholder wealth while keeping risk within board-approved tolerances.
  • Portfolio Management is the process of selecting and overseeing a group of investments (securities) that meet the long-term financial objectives and risk tolerance of the investor, using Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT) as its theoretical foundation.
  • Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) states that the expected return on any security equals the risk-free rate plus that security's systematic risk (beta) multiplied by the market risk premium: E(Ri) = Rf + βi × [E(Rm) – Rf].
  • Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), proposed by Markowitz (1952), asserts that investors can reduce unsystematic risk through diversification, and the optimal portfolio lies on the Efficient Frontier where risk is minimized for a given level of expected return.
  • Security Market Line (SML) is the graphical representation of the CAPM equation; it plots expected return against beta. Any security plotting above the SML is undervalued; below it is overvalued.
  • Transaction Exposure arises when an entity has contractual cash flows denominated in a foreign currency that are yet to be settled, creating uncertainty about the domestic-currency value of those future receipts or payments.
  • Translation Exposure (Accounting Exposure) arises from consolidating foreign subsidiaries' financial statements into the parent's reporting currency, affecting balance-sheet items and reported earnings.
  • Economic Exposure measures the change in the present value of a firm's future cash flows due to unanticipated exchange-rate movements; it is a long-run, operating-level concept distinct from transaction/translation exposure.
  • Forward Contract is a customized OTC agreement between two parties to buy/sell an asset at a specified price on a future date; it locks in the exchange rate but carries counterparty risk.
  • Futures Contract is a standardized exchange-traded forward contract with daily mark-to-market settlement, eliminating most counterparty risk through a clearing house.
  • Options give the buyer the right but not the obligation to buy (call) or sell (put) an underlying asset at a strike price on or before expiry. Premium is the price paid for this right.
  • Swaps are OTC contracts in which two parties exchange cash-flow streams — commonly Interest Rate Swaps (fixed vs floating) and Currency Swaps (principal and interest in different currencies).
  • Value at Risk (VaR) is the maximum loss not exceeded with a given confidence level over a specified time horizon. For example, a ₹10 crore VaR at 99% over one day means there is only a 1% chance of losing more than ₹10 crore tomorrow.
  • Jensen's Alpha measures abnormal return: the difference between actual portfolio return and the return predicted by CAPM. Positive alpha indicates outperformance after adjusting for systematic risk.
  • Treynor Ratio = (Rp – Rf) / βp; it measures excess return per unit of systematic risk. Unlike Sharpe ratio, it uses beta as the risk denominator, making it suitable for well-diversified portfolios.
  • Sharpe Ratio = (Rp – Rf) / σp; it measures excess return per unit of total risk (standard deviation) and is appropriate for evaluating portfolios that are not fully diversified.
  • Information Ratio = (Rp – Rb) / Tracking Error; it measures active return generated per unit of deviation from the benchmark, assessing manager skill independent of market exposure.
  • Appraisal Ratio = αp / σe; where αp is Jensen's alpha and σe is the standard deviation of residual (unsystematic) risk. It evaluates risk-adjusted performance of actively managed portfolios.
  • Modigliani-Modigliani (M²) Measure adjusts portfolio return to the risk level of the benchmark by leveraging or de-leveraging the portfolio so that its standard deviation matches the benchmark's; the difference in returns is the M² measure.
  • Dividend Irrelevance Theory (Miller & Modigliani, 1961) states that in perfect markets, dividend policy does not affect firm value or cost of equity; only investment policy matters.
  • WALT (Weighted Average Life to Maturity) is a measure used for fixed-income portfolios; it is the weighted average time until receipt of cash flows from the portfolio's securities, used in immunization strategies.
  • Immunization is a liability-matching strategy where the duration of assets equals the duration of liabilities, thereby insulating the portfolio's value from interest-rate movements.
  • Residual Income = Net Operating Profit After Tax (NOPAT) – (Capital Employed × Cost of Capital); it measures economic profit over and above the required return on capital.
  • EVA (Economic Value Added) = NOPAT – (WACC × Capital Employed); it is a specific residual-income measure widely used in strategic performance evaluation.
  • Put-Call Parity: C + Xe^(-rt) = P + S₀ for European options on non-dividend-paying stocks. It ensures no arbitrage exists between calls, puts, the underlying, and a risk-free bond.
  • Black-Scholes Model values European-style options using six variables: current stock price (S), exercise price (X), time to expiry (T), risk-free rate (r), volatility (σ), and expected dividends (if any). Formula: C = S·N(d₁) – X·e^(-rT)·N(d₂).
  • No-Arbitrage Pricing Principle: if two portfolios have identical payoffs in every possible future state, their current prices must be equal; otherwise riskless profit can be made.
  • Interest Rate Parity (IRP): covered IRP states (1 + i_d) = (F/S) × (1 + i_f); uncovered IRP states expected spot change equals the interest-rate differential. Violations create arbitrage opportunities.
  • Purchasing Power Parity (PPP): absolute PPP says S = P_domestic / P_foreign; relative PPP says percentage change in exchange rate ≈ inflation differential between two countries.
  • International Fisher Effect (IFE): the nominal interest-rate differential between two countries equals the expected percentage change in the spot exchange rate between their currencies.
  • Foreign Exchange Market Participants include central banks, commercial banks, multilateral agencies, corporate treasurers, brokers/dealers, and retail traders; it operates 24 hours across global sessions.
  • Swap Spread = Swap Rate – Government Bond Yield of comparable maturity; it reflects the credit and liquidity premium embedded in swap markets relative to risk-free sovereign yields.
  • Credit-Default Swap (CDS) is insurance against the default of a reference entity; the protection buyer pays periodic premiums and receives a payoff if a credit event occurs.
  • FX Futures vs FX Forwards: futures are exchange-traded, standardized, marked-to-market daily with margin requirements; forwards are OTC, customizable, settled at maturity, with counterparty risk.
  • Revaluation Gain/Loss on Translation: when strengthening of reporting currency reduces the domestic-currency value of foreign-net assets, a translation loss is recognized in Other Comprehensive Income (OCI).
  • Natural Hedge: matching foreign-currency inflows with outflows in the same currency to naturally offset exposure without using financial derivatives.
  • Net Investment Hedge: hedging a net investment in a foreign operation using foreign-currency borrowings or derivative contracts; gains/losses are recorded in OCI under Ind AS 110/IFRS 9.
  • Hedge Effectiveness: the degree to which changes in the fair value or cash flows of the hedging instrument offset changes in the hedged item's fair value or cash flows. A hedge is highly effective if the ratio falls within 80%–125%.
  • Lead and Lag: managing payables and receivables by accelerating (leading) or delaying (lagging) payments/receipts in response to expected currency movements.

Key concepts

  • Portfolio Selection Process: (1) Policy setting — define risk-return objectives, constraints (liquidity, legal, tax, uniqueness); (2) Security Analysis — top-down (macro → sector → security) or bottom-up approach; (3) Portfolio Construction — asset allocation, security selection, weighting; (4) Portfolio Rebalancing — periodic adjustment to maintain target weights; (5) Performance Evaluation — benchmark-relative assessment.
  • Markowitz Diversification: covariance between assets determines portfolio risk. Portfolio variance = Σw_i²σ_i² + ΣΣw_iw_jσ_ij. Risk reduction is greatest when correlations are low/negative. The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios offering maximum expected return for each level of risk.
  • Systematic vs Unsystematic Risk: systematic (market) risk cannot be eliminated by diversification and is measured by beta; unsystematic (firm-specific/idiosyncratic) risk can be largely eliminated with 15–20 securities. Total Risk = Systematic + Unsystematic.
  • Beta Interpretation: β = 1 → moves with market; β > 1 → more volatile than market (aggressive); β < 1 → less volatile (defensive); β = 0 → uncorrelated with market; β < 0 → moves opposite to market (rare, e.g., gold). Beta is estimated via regression: β = Cov(Ri,Rm) / Var(Rm).
  • Characteristics Line: the regression line of security returns against market returns; slope = beta, intercept = alpha (expected return when market return is zero). Plotting the line helps visualize risk-return relationship.
  • CAPM Implications: (a) Only systematic risk commands a risk premium; (b) Expected return depends only on beta; (c) All rational investors hold the market portfolio combined with lending/borrowing at the risk-free rate; (d) The SML applies to individual securities and portfolios alike.
  • Multi-Factor Models (Arbitrage Pricing Theory — APT): Ross (1976) proposed that expected returns are linearly related to multiple macro factors: E(Ri) = Rf + β_i1·RP_1 + β_i2·RP_2 + ... + β_in·RP_n. More flexible than CAPM but requires identifying relevant factors (inflation, GDP growth, term structure, etc.).
  • Three-Factor Model (Fama-French): adds Size (SMB) and Value (HML) factors to market factor: E(Ri) = Rf + β_mkt·(Rm-Rf) + β_smb·SMB + β_hml·HML. Explains why small-cap and value stocks earn higher returns than CAPM predicts.
  • Four-Factor Model (Carhart): adds Momentum factor (UMD) to Fama-French three factors, recognizing that past winners tend to continue winning in the short run.
  • Sharpe vs Treynor vs Jensen Debate: Sharpe uses total risk (best for single-security evaluation); Treynor uses systematic risk (best for well-diversified portfolios); Jensen uses alpha from CAPM regression (direct measure of abnormal performance). No single measure is universally superior — choice depends on portfolio diversification level.
  • Fund Manager Evaluation Metrics — when to use what: use Treynor/Information Ratio for diversified institutional portfolios; use Sharpe/M² for retail/non-diversified portfolios; use Alpha for security selection ability; use Beta for market-timing ability assessment.
  • Derivatives Pricing — Forward/Futures: Forward price F = S₀ × e^(r+ s - y)T, where s = storage cost, y = convenience yield/dividend yield. For currency forwards: F = S₀ × (1 + i_d·t) / (1 + i_f·t) using simple interest for short tenors.
  • Put-Call Parity Applications: detect mispricing → execute arbitrage (buy underpriced, sell overpriced). Also used to construct synthetic positions: synthetic call = long put + long stock + borrowing; synthetic put = long call + short stock + lending.
  • Options Greeks: Delta (Δ) = rate of change of option price w.r.t. underlying price; Gamma (Γ) = rate of change of delta; Theta (Θ) = time decay of option value; Vega (ν) = sensitivity to volatility; Rho (ρ) = sensitivity to interest rates. Call delta ∈ [0,1], Put delta ∈ [-1,0].
  • Option Strategies — Basic: (a) Straddle = long call + long put (same strike/expiry) — profits from large moves regardless of direction; (b) Strangle = long call + long put (different strikes, OTM) — cheaper but needs larger move; (c) Butterfly = bull spread + bear spread (three strikes) — profits from low volatility; (d) Spread strategies profit from directional views.
  • Swap Types and Use Cases: (a) Plain Vanilla IRS — exchange fixed for floating on notional; (b) Cross-Currency Swap — exchange principal and interest in different currencies; (c) Basis Swap — floating-for-floating (e.g., LIBOR vs MIBOR); (d) Equity Swap — equity returns for fixed/floating; (e) Commodity Swap — commodity price for fixed.
  • Swap Valuation: A swap's value to the fixed-rate receiver = PV(floating leg) – PV(fixed leg). At inception, value = 0. Between settlements, value changes with interest rates. Fix leg value = notional × fix rate × annuity factor; float leg value ≈ notional (reset to par just after payment).
  • Value at Risk (VaR) Methods: (a) Variance-Covariance (Parametric) — assumes normal distribution, uses z-score and standard deviation; fast but sensitive to distributional assumptions. (b) Historical Simulation — uses actual historical returns; non-parametric but assumes history repeats. (c) Monte Carlo Simulation — generates random scenarios; most flexible but computationally intensive.
  • VaR Interpretation Caveats: VaR is NOT a loss ceiling — losses can exceed VaR. VaR does not capture tail risk (expected shortfall/CVaR does). Different confidence levels and time horizons yield different VaRs. Model risk is significant — incorrect assumptions produce misleading results.
  • Exchange Rate Determination Theories: (a) PPP — exchange rates adjust to equalize price levels; (b) IRP — interest-rate differentials determine forward premiums/discounts; (c) IFE — interest-rate differentials predict future spot movements; (d) Relative Influence Approach — combines PPP, IRP, and expectations.
  • FFP (Financial Flow Portfolio) Approach: portfolio flows respond to interest-rate differentials and expected capital gains. Higher domestic rates attract foreign capital, appreciating the currency. This complements PPP (goods flow) and IRP (arbitrage).
  • Hedging Instruments Comparison: Forward — custom, OTC, no upfront cost, counterparty risk; Futures — exchange-traded, liquid, margin required; Options — flexibility, premium cost, limited downside; Swaps — long-term, customized, bilateral. Choice depends on exposure type, tenor, and risk appetite.
  • Natural Hedging Techniques: (a) Match currency of revenues with currency of costs; (b) Invoice in home currency where possible; (c) Operate production in the currency zone of sales; (d) Use leading/lagging for short-term offsets; (e) Netting — multinational companies net intercompany payables/receivables.
  • Netting Mechanisms: (a) Bilateral Netting — two parties offset mutual obligations; (b) Multilateral Netting — central clearing house nets all participants' positions, dramatically reducing gross exposures and transaction costs.
  • Translation Methods under Ind AS / IFRS: (a) Closing Rate Method — assets/liabilities translated at closing rate, income at average rate, equity at historical rate; differences go to OCI (Forex Translation Reserve). (b) Temporal Method — monetary items at closing rate, non-monetary at historical rate; differences go to P&L. Choice depends on functional currency assessment.
  • Functional Currency Determination: Primary indicators — currency dominating sales/pricing, currency of cost structure, currency of financing, and operational autonomy of the subsidiary. The entity must demonstrate which currency most faithfully represents its economic substance.
  • Corporate Restructuring Modes: (a) Merger — two companies combine to form a new entity; (b) Amalgamation — one absorbs another (purchase vs pooling of interests method); (c) Demerger — splitting a company into separate entities; (d) Scheme of Arrangement — court-approved compromise between company and stakeholders; (e) Buyback — company repurchases its own shares.
  • Amalgamation Accounting — Purchase Method: acquiring company records assets/liabilities of the acquiree at fair values. Goodwill = Consideration transferred + NCI + Fair value of previous equity interest – Fair value of net identifiable assets. Revaluation surplus of acquiree is recognized.
  • Amalgamation Accounting — Pooling of Interests Method: assets/liabilities recorded at book values. No goodwill is recognized. Shareholder equity is combined on a carry-forward basis. This method is restricted under Ind AS but may apply under earlier Indian GAAP for units in the same industry.
  • SEBI Takeover Code Regulations: Substantial Acquisition of Shares and takeovers (SAST) Regulations, 2011 regulate acquisitions and takeovers. Trigger threshold: 25% shareholding requires mandatory open offer. Public anchor investor provisions apply to listed entities. Disclosure norms mandate filing of SCP (Statement of Purpose) and OBO (Open Offer Offer Letter).
  • Dividend Relevance Theories: (a) Walter's Model — dividend policy affects value when return on investment (r) differs from cost of capital (k). If r > k, zero payout maximizes value; if r < k, full payout maximizes value. (b) Gordon's Model — similar logic; value = D₁/(k-g) for constant growth. (c) MM Irrelevance — under perfect markets, value is independent of dividend policy.
  • Stock Split vs Bonus Issue vs Share Buyback: Stock split increases shares proportionally without changing equity; bonus issue capitalizes reserves; buyback reduces outstanding shares and may signal undervaluation or improve EPS. Each has different tax and accounting implications.
  • Share Repurchase Methods: Open market purchase — flexible, transparent; Tender offer — fixed price, all shareholders invited; Block trade — negotiated with specific investors; Buyback from employees — under ESOP/ESPP programs. SEBI (Buyback of Securities) Regulations, 2018 govern listed companies in India.
  • Capital Structure Theories: (a) Traditional — there is an optimal debt-equity mix where WACC is minimized and firm value is maximized; moderate debt improves value up to a point then worsens it. (b) MM Proposition I (no taxes) — capital structure irrelevant; firm value determined by real assets. (c) MM Proposition II (no taxes) — cost of equity rises linearly with leverage. (d) MM with taxes — interest tax shield makes debt valuable; 100% debt is optimal.
  • Trade-Off Theory: firms balance the tax benefits of debt (interest tax shield) against the costs of financial distress (bankruptcy costs, agency costs). Optimal capital structure exists where marginal benefit = marginal cost.
  • Pecking Order Theory (Myers & Majluf): firms prefer internal financing first, then debt, then equity as a last resort due to information asymmetry. This explains why profitable firms often have lower leverage despite having access to cheap debt.
  • Agency Costs of Debt: conflict between shareholders and debtholders leads to (a) Asset substitution — taking riskier projects after debt is issued; (b) Underinvestment — rejecting positive-NPV projects to benefit debt holders; (c) Debt overhang — existing debt discourages new equity issuance. Covenants mitigate these costs.
  • Agency Costs of Equity: conflict between managers and shareholders. Managers may pursue empire building, excessive perks, or risk avoidance. Solutions include performance-based compensation, board oversight, and hostile takeover threat.
  • Lease vs Buy Decision: compare NPV of leasing (lease payments discounted at after-tax cost of debt) with NPV of buying (purchase price minus depreciation tax shield discounted at appropriate rate). Lease is preferable when lease NPV < buy NPV.
  • Adjusted Present Value (APV) Approach: APV = Base-case NPV (all-equity financed) + NPV of financing side effects (tax shield, issuance costs, distress costs). Useful when leverage changes over time, unlike WACC method which assumes constant debt ratio.
  • WACC Formula: WACC = (E/V) × Re + (D/V) × Rd × (1 – Tc), where E = market value of equity, D = market value of debt, V = E + D, Re = cost of equity, Rd = cost of debt, Tc = corporate tax rate. Must use market values, not book values.
  • Cost of Equity Estimation: (a) CAPM: Re = Rf + β(Rm – Rf). (b) Dividend Discount Model: Re = (D₁/P₀) + g. (c) Earnings Model: Re = (E₁/P₀) + g. (d) Build-up method: Re = Rf + ERP + size premium + company-specific risk premium. Use consistent risk-free rate and market-risk-premium assumptions.
  • International Cost of Equity: Re_international = Rf + β × (ERP_domestic + Country Risk Premium). Country risk can be measured via sovereign bond spread, GPD volatility, or MSCI country risk ratings. Adjust beta for emerging-market volatility if needed.
  • Foreign Currency Cash Flow Discounting: Two equivalent approaches: (a) Discount foreign cash flows at foreign-currency cost of capital, then convert at spot rate; (b) Convert foreign cash flows to domestic currency using expected spot rates (from IRP), then discount at domestic cost of capital. Both yield the same NPV if parity holds.
  • Real Option Valuation: extends traditional NPV by valuing managerial flexibility — options to expand, delay, abandon, or switch. Black-Scholes or binomial trees can value these options. Common types: investment timing option, expansion option, abandonment option, flexibility option.
  • Abandonment Option: gives the firm the right to terminate a project and recover salvage value. It adds value to a marginal project, potentially turning a negative NPV into a positive one. Critical for capital budgeting in volatile industries.
  • Performance Evaluation — Responsibility Accounting: cost centers (measured on costs), profit centers (measured on profit), investment centers (measured on ROI or EVA). Each center has defined authority over controllable items only. Controllable vs non-controllable cost distinction is crucial.
  • ROI vs Residual Income: ROI = Operating Income / Operating Assets; promotes goal congruence but may reject positive-NPV projects if they lower overall ROI. RI = Operating Income – (Minimum Required Return × Operating Assets); promotes accept/reject decisions consistent with shareholder value maximization. RI is generally preferred for evaluation.
  • Balanced Scorecard: four perspectives — Financial (profitability, revenue growth), Customer (satisfaction, retention, market share), Internal Business Process (cycle time, quality, efficiency), Learning & Growth (employee training, infrastructure, culture). Strategy maps link cause-effect relationships across perspectives.
  • EVA Implementation Steps: (1) Calculate NOPAT; (2) Determine total capital employed; (3) Calculate WACC; (4) Compute EVA = NOPAT – (Capital × WACC); (5) Adjust for accounting distortions (R&D capitalize, operating leases capitalize, LIFO inventory adjustment, pension actuarial adjustments); (6) Use EVA for incentive compensation and capital allocation.
  • Economic Profit vs Accounting Profit: Accounting profit follows GAAP/Ind AS rules and includes depreciation based on historical cost. Economic profit subtracts the opportunity cost of all capital (including equity) and adjusts for non-cash and non-operating items. EVA approximates economic profit.
  • Transfer Pricing: price charged by one division of a company to another. Goals: (a) Autonomy — allow divisions to act like independent businesses; (b) Goal congruence — align divisional decisions with corporate objectives; (c) Performance evaluation — fair measurement. Methods: market-based, cost-based (variable/full/absorption), negotiated, dual pricing.
  • Optimal Transfer Price: in a perfectly competitive output market, transfer price should equal marginal cost. In imperfect markets, it equals market price (if available) or negotiated price between variable cost and market price. Corporate tax minimization also influences transfer pricing for multinational enterprises.
  • Budgetary Control: planning through budgets, execution monitoring, variance analysis, corrective action. Master budget includes operating budgets (sales, production, direct materials, labor, overhead) and financial budgets (cash, capital expenditure, pro-forma financial statements). Flexible budgeting adjusts for actual activity levels.
  • Variance Analysis Framework: Sales Variance = (Actual Price – Standard Price) × Actual Quantity (price variance) + (Actual Quantity – Budgeted Quantity) × Standard Price (volume variance). Material/Labor/Overhead variances follow similar patterns. Investigate only material variances.
  • Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB): every expense must be justified from scratch each period, unlike incremental budgeting which adjusts prior budgets. ZBB requires decision packages ranked by priority. Increases managerial involvement but is resource-intensive.
  • Strategic Financial Management vs Traditional FM: SFM takes a long-term, holistic, forward-looking perspective integrating risk management, international finance, derivatives, M&A, and performance measurement. Traditional FM focuses on shorter-term working capital, routine financing, and basic capital budgeting.

Important facts

  • Harry Markowitz won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1990 for Modern Portfolio Theory; William Sharpe, Robert Merton, and Myron Scholes shared the 1997 prize for CAPM and options pricing. Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published the options pricing model in 1973.
  • CAPM was developed independently by Sharpe (1964), Lintner (1965), and Mossin (1966). The model assumes: homogeneous expectations, risk-free borrowing/lending, single-period horizon, no taxes/transaction costs, and divisible securities.
  • SEBI (Security and Exchange Board of India) regulates derivatives trading in India. F&O segments of NSE, BSE are the primary platforms. SEBI introduced index options in 2000, stock options and futures in 2001.
  • RBI regulates forex dealings by banks under FEMA, 1999. The forward market in India is regulated by RBI, which licenses authorized dealers. Cross-currency pairs have restrictions; major pairs (USDINR, EURINR, GBPINR, JPYINR) are freely traded.
  • Ind AS 109 (Financial Instruments) governs hedge accounting in India, converged with IFRS 9. A hedge must be formally designated and documented. Effectiveness is assessed both at inception and on an ongoing basis.
  • Schedule III of Companies Act, 2013 requires disclosure of hedge accounting under Ind AS 109. Gains/losses on effective hedging instruments are recognized in OCI for cash flow hedges and reclassified to P&L when the hedged item affects profit.
  • ICMAI's CMA Final SFM paper typically carries 100 marks with a mix of objective and descriptive questions. Section A contains compulsory short-answer/numerical questions; Section B offers choices between descriptive and case-study questions. Weightage: Portfolio Management ~15%, Derivatives ~20%, Risk Management ~15%, International FM ~20%, Capital Structure & Dividend ~15%, Performance Evaluation ~15%.
  • Under MM Proposition with taxes, the value of a levered firm = Value of unlevered firm + Tc × D, where Tc is the corporate tax rate and D is the amount of debt. The tax shield value increases linearly with debt.
  • Miller's Equilibrium (1977) with personal taxes: VL = VU + [1 – (1-Tc)(1-Te)/(1-Td)] × D, where Te = equityholder tax rate, Td = debt holder tax rate. If (1-Tc)(1-Te) < (1-Td), debt is preferred; the opposite favors equity.
  • The safe initial margin for futures contracts in India is typically 5%–10% of the contract value, with variation margin collected daily. SEBI prescribes minimum margins; exchanges may add spiking margins during high volatility.
  • Option premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value. Intrinsic value = max(S – X, 0) for calls, max(X – S, 0) for puts. Time value erodes as expiry approaches (theta decay), accelerating in the final weeks.
  • Binomial Option Pricing: builds a lattice of possible future prices over discrete time steps. At each node, the option value is the discounted expected value of the two successor nodes. Converges to Black-Scholes as steps → ∞. Suitable for American options (early exercise).
  • Put-Call Parity with dividends: C + X·e^(-rT) + PV(Dividends) = P + S₀. This ensures that a portfolio of (long call + long put + bond worth X) has the same payoff as owning the stock at expiry.
  • Covered Interest Arbitrage: (1) Borrow in low-interest currency; (2) Convert to high-interest currency at spot; (3) Invest at high interest rate; (4) Sell proceeds forward at the forward rate. Profit exists when the forward rate deviates from IRP-implied rate.
  • Uncovered Interest Arbitrage: similar to covered but without a forward contract; relies on expected future spot rate. Expected profit = interest differential – expected exchange-rate change. Risky because the future spot rate is unknown.
  • The Law of One Price is the foundation of PPP: identical goods should sell for the same price across countries when expressed in a common currency. Absolute PPP implies S = Pa/Pb; relative PPP extends this to inflation rates.
  • The Balance of Payments (BoP) identity: Current Account + Capital Account + Financial Account + Errors & Omissions = 0. A current account deficit must be financed by capital/financial account surplus.
  • India's forex reserves are managed by RBI under a basket-based system. The external debt is monitored quarterly. FDI and FPI flows significantly impact rupee appreciation/depreciation pressures.
  • M2M (Mark-to-Market) in derivatives: futures are marked to market daily; gains/losses are settled through the clearing corporation. Options premiums are not M2M'd — the buyer's max loss is the premium paid, seller's max gain is the premium received.
  • VaR Limit Setting: banks set VaR limits at business-unit and aggregate levels. Internal audits backtest VaR models quarterly. Breach of VaR limits triggers mandatory escalation and position reduction.
  • The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision mandates banks to hold capital equal to a multiple of their VaR-based risk measures (typically 3× daily VaR + stressed VaR). This framework has evolved through Basel I, II, and III.
  • Currency Swap cash flows: at inception, principals are exchanged; during the swap, interest payments are swapped in respective currencies; at maturity, principals are re-exchanged at the agreed rate. Eliminates both interest-rate and exchange-rate risk.
  • Cross-Currency Basis Swap: reflects the supply-demand imbalance for specific currencies in the swap market. Negative basis means it costs more to obtain USD funding via swaps than via direct borrowing.
  • Key Risks in SFM: Market Risk (price movements), Credit Risk (counterparty/default), Liquidity Risk (inability to exit positions), Operational Risk (system/process failures), Legal Risk (contract enforceability), Model Risk (incorrect valuation), Regulatory Risk (policy changes).
  • Delta-Neutral Strategy: holding a combination of options and the underlying such that the overall portfolio delta is zero. The portfolio is immune to small movements in the underlying price. Requires continuous rebalancing as delta changes.
  • Gamma-Neutrality: balancing the portfolio's gamma to zero so that delta remains stable for larger price moves. Achieved by combining options with different gammas.
  • Straddle vs Strangle: Straddle uses same strike (ATM), strangle uses different strikes (OTM call + OTM put). Straddle has higher cost but wider profit zone initially; strangle is cheaper but needs a bigger move to profit.
  • Rolling a Hedge: extending a forward/future position by closing the near-term contract and opening a new one for a later date. Common in commodity hedging where delivery months differ from exposure dates.
  • Cash Flow Hedge: hedge of a forecast transaction or variability in cash flows of a recognized asset/liability. Effective portion of gain/loss goes to OCI; ineffective portion to P&L. Reclassified to P&L when the hedged transaction affects profit.
  • Fair Value Hedge: hedge of exposure to changes in fair value of a recognized asset/liability or an unrecognized firm commitment. Both hedge instrument and hedged item changes flow through P&L.
  • Hedge of Net Investment: treated similarly to a cash flow hedge. Gains/losses on the hedging instrument are recognized in OCI and reclassified to P&L on disposal of the foreign operation.
  • Key SEBI Regulations for SFM-related activities: (a) SEBI (ICDR) Regulations for issuer disclosures; (b) SEBI (SAST) Regulations for takeovers; (c) SEBI (Buyback) Regulations for repurchases; (d) SEBI (Listing Obligations) for derivative disclosures; (e) FEMA regulations for cross-border transactions.
  • Companies Act, 2013 Sections relevant to SFM: Section 42 (private placement), Section 62(1)(b) (bonus shares), Section 68 (buyback), Section 173 (board meetings for approval), Section 185 (loans to directors), Section 186 (loans/investments).
  • Income Tax implications of SFM transactions: capital gains tax on sale of securities (short-term vs long-term rates), STT on derivatives, withholding tax on interest/dividends, Transfer Pricing regulations (Section 92–92F) for cross-border transactions.
  • ICDS (Income Tax Deduction and Standards) applies to companies subject to tax audit. It prescribes specific methods for valuation of inventory, recognition of forward contracts, and accounting for derivatives — sometimes differing from Companies Act/Ind AS treatment.
  • GST impact on financial services: most financial services are exempt from GST, but advisory/consultancy services provided by cost consultants attract 18% GST. Insurance premium payments attract GST at applicable rates.

Memory aids

  • CAPM Formula Mnemonic: 'Rf plus Beta times the Market's premium' → E(Ri) = Rf + βi(E(Rm)-Rf). Remember: you get paid for risk you CAN'T diversify away (beta), not total risk.
  • Sharpe vs Treynor Memory Aid: 'S'harpe looks at 'S'tandard deviation (total risk), 'T'reynor looks at 'B'eta (systematic risk) → Think: 'S' for 'S'pread (total), 'T' for 'T'argeted (systematic).
  • Put-Call Parity: 'CALL + BOND = PUT + STOCK' — a call plus a zero-coupon bond (worth X at expiry) replicates a put plus the stock. Or: 'You're CALMed by a Bond to PUT on STOCK'.
  • Option Greeks Memory: Δelta = Direction (price move), Γamma = Delta's speed (curvature), Θeta = Time decay (theta sounds like 'thee' → time passing), VeGA = Volatility, RhO = Interest rates (rho = rate of change).
  • MPT Efficient Frontier: imagine a bow-shaped curve — every point on the upper half is a portfolio where you can't get higher return without taking more risk. The 'top' of the bow is the Minimum Variance Portfolio. Above-left = good; below-right = bad.
  • Irish Coffee Trick for Put-Call Parity: Irish COFFEE → Calls, Options, Forwards, Futures, Equity. The parity relationship: C + PV(X) = P + S.
  • MM Proposition Memory: 'No taxes = no difference in capital structure'; 'With taxes = debt is king (tax shield)'; 'With personal taxes = it depends on the relative tax rates'.
  • VaR Confidence Levels: 95% → z = 1.645; 99% → z = 1.645? No — 99% → z ≈ 2.33; 97.5% → z = 1.96. Remember: '95 is 1.6, 99 is 2.3'.
  • Swap Analogy: A swap is like a relay race — each party runs their leg (pays in their currency/rate) and hands off to the other. The notional principal is the baton that gets passed back and forth but never actually exchanged in a plain vanilla IRS.
  • Exposure Hierarchy: Transaction (short-term, contractual) → Translation (accounting, balance sheet) → Economic (long-term, strategic). Think: T=Today's contract, T=Tomorrow's statement, E=Eternity's competitiveness.
  • Lead-Lag Memory: Lead = send payment early (when your payable currency is expected to appreciate); Lag = delay payment (when payable currency is expected to depreciate). Opposite for receivables.
  • Hedge Effectiveness Band: 80% to 125% — 'Between 80 and 125, you're safe and sound!' Outside this range, the hedge is deemed ineffective and accounting treatment changes.
  • Diversification Benefit: Correlation = 1 → no benefit; Correlation = -1 → maximum benefit (perfect hedge); Correlation = 0 → partial benefit. Think: 'Perfect opposites cancel completely.'
  • M² Measure: 'Modigliani-Modigliani matches the benchmark's risk — whichever portfolio earns more at that matched risk wins.' Visualize: draw two portfolios, leverage/de-leverage one to match the other's σ, then compare returns.
  • Zero-Based Budgeting: 'Start from ZERO every year — don't inherit last year's budget.' Like rebuilding from scratch each cycle, justifying every rupee anew.
  • Fama-French Factors: 'Size and Value matter too!' — SMB (Small Minus Big) and HML (High book-to-market Minus Low). Remember the mnemonic: 'Small Makes Big Money, High Value Makes Lots'.
  • Net Present Value vs IRR Conflict: when NPV and IRR give conflicting rankings, trust NPV because IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR rate (unrealistic), while NPV assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital (realistic).
  • Abandonment Option Value: 'The option to quit is like a put option on your project.' Just as a put protects against price decline, abandonment protects against project failure. It's always ≥ 0.
  • Derivatives Payoff Diagrams: Long Call = hockey stick bent right (profit above strike); Long Put = hockey stick bent left (profit below strike); Long Future = straight line through origin (symmetric); Short Call/Put = mirror images of longs.
  • SEBI F&O Regulations Quick Reference: (a) Maximum position limit = lower of exchange limit or SEBI limit; (b) Margin requirement = SPAN + Expiration risk component; (c) Delivery-based trades attracted to cash segment.

Exam tips

  • In numerical problems on CAPM, always identify whether you need to find expected return, beta, or market return — rearrange E(Ri) = Rf + β[E(Rm)–Rf] accordingly. Watch for 'risk-free rate is X%' vs ' treasury bill rate is Y%'.
  • For options pricing, practice Black-Scholes formula memorization: d₁ = [ln(S/X) + (r + σ²/2)T] / σ√T and d₂ = d₁ – σ√T. Then C = S·N(d₁) – X·e^(-rT)·N(d₂). Know how to use standard normal tables.
  • In VaR calculations, distinguish between parametric (z-score × σ) and historical simulation methods. For parametric, know the z-values: 90% = 1.28, 95% = 1.645, 99% = 2.33.
  • For forward/futures pricing, use the cost-of-carry model: F = S × e^((r+c-y)T). For currency forwards, use interest-rate parity: F = S × (1+i_d) / (1+i_f) for simple interest (short tenor).
  • When evaluating fund managers, always compute at least TWO performance measures (e.g., both Sharpe and Treynor) and explain WHY results may differ. Examiners look for analytical reasoning, not just formula application.
  • In derivatives hedging questions, clearly state: (a) the type of exposure, (b) the instrument chosen and why, (c) the hedge ratio, (d) the number of contracts, (e) the hedge effectiveness test. Structured answers score higher.
  • For SWAP valuation questions, remember: fixed-rate payer's swap value = PV(floating leg) – PV(fixed leg). Floating leg value immediately after a reset = notional amount (par value).
  • In capital structure questions, structure your answer: first state the theory, then its assumptions, then its implications, then critique/limitations, then apply to the given case. ICMAI values this analytical framework.
  • For dividend policy questions, contrast Walter's model, Gordon's model, and MM irrelevance explicitly. Show formulas, explain conditions (r > k or r < k), and discuss real-world violations of assumptions.
  • In international finance questions involving exchange rates, show the complete arbitrage calculation with direction (borrow in which currency, invest in which). State whether covered or uncovered arbitrage applies.
  • For performance measurement questions, always define the metric before calculating it. Include the interpretation step — what does a positive/negative alpha mean? What does a ratio below 1 indicate?
  • Amalgamation numericals: prepare a working note for (a) net assets taken over, (b) consideration payable, (c) goodwill/computation, (d) share capital structure post-amalgamation. Present workings clearly for partial marks.
  • In hedge accounting questions, identify whether it is a cash flow hedge, fair value hedge, or net investment hedge — each has different accounting treatment. Quote the relevant Ind AS (109/110/115) for marks.
  • For transfer pricing questions, evaluate multiple methods (arm's length, cost-plus, CUP, TNMM) and justify the most appropriate one based on the nature of the transaction and availability of comparable data.
  • Quick revision tip: make one-page summary sheets for each major topic with only key formulas, ratios, and conditions. Review these repeatedly before the exam to build recall speed.
  • Time management in the exam: allocate roughly 1.8 minutes per mark. Section A (compulsory) should take ~40% of time; Section B (choose 3 of 5) ~60%. Never spend more than 20 minutes on any single numerical question.
  • Always show formulas before substituting values — even if the final numerical is wrong, formula usage and correct setup earn step marks. ICMAI examiners award partial credit generously.
  • In theoretical questions, use headings and sub-headings. For 'Discuss', 'Examine', 'Critically evaluate' — structure as introduction → main body with points → conclusion. Bullet points are acceptable for clear presentation.
  • Remember Indian context specifically: SEBI regulations, RBI guidelines, Ind AS standards, Companies Act provisions, FEMA rules, and Income Tax implications are all fair game. Generic international answers lose marks when Indian specifics are expected.
  • Common numerical combinations to practice: (1) Portfolio expected return and standard deviation with two assets; (2) CAPM expected return and alpha; (3) Forward/futures pricing; (4) Black-Scholes call/put value; (5) VaR computation; (6) Swap valuation; (7) WACC and APV; (8) Amalgamation goodwill.
  • If stuck on a numerical, write down the relevant formula and define each variable — this often triggers recall and earns partial marks. Also attempt alternative approaches if one path seems blocked.
  • For essay-type questions on SFM topics, always conclude with a recommendation or summary judgment. ICMAI expects Cost & Management Accountants to provide actionable insights, not just describe theories.
  • Current Affairs linkage: if a question relates to recent RBI monetary policy, SEBI regulatory changes, or global market events (e.g., Ukraine war impact on commodity hedging), incorporating this context demonstrates applied understanding and scores higher.

Common traps

  • Using book values instead of market values in WACC calculation — WACC must use market-value weights for both equity and debt, as these reflect current opportunity costs.
  • Confusing systematic risk (beta) with unsystematic risk — only systematic risk is priced in CAPM. Diversifiable risk earns no premium; including it inflates required return incorrectly.
  • Applying Sharpe ratio when the portfolio is well-diversified — for diversified portfolios, Treynor ratio is more appropriate since unsystematic risk is already diversified away.
  • Assuming put-call parity holds for American options — it holds exactly only for European options. American options allow early exercise, so the parity becomes an inequality: S – X ≤ C – P ≤ S – Xe^(-rT).
  • Forgetting dividends in the Black-Scholes model or put-call parity — dividends reduce the stock price at ex-date, lowering call values and increasing put values. The adjustment is subtracting PV(dividends) from S₀.
  • Treating VaR as a maximum possible loss — VaR at 95% confidence means 5% of losses EXCEED the VaR figure. Expected Shortfall (CVaR) measures the average loss beyond VaR.
  • Applying PPP to short-term exchange rates — PPP works well over long horizons (5–10 years) but fails in the short run due to capital flows, speculation, and sticky prices.
  • Ignoring counterparty risk in forwards vs futures — forwards carry counterparty/default risk because they are OTC; futures are cleared through a CCP with daily margin, drastically reducing this risk.
  • Using the wrong discount rate for foreign cash flows — discount foreign-currency cash flows at the foreign-currency cost of capital, not the domestic rate. Converting at the wrong rate introduces bias.
  • Double-counting risk in international projects — if the cash flows already reflect currency risk (through realistic exchange-rate forecasts), do not also add a country risk premium on top unless justified.
  • Confusing hedge effectiveness testing at inception vs ongoing — a hedge is effective at inception based on design, but must be tested periodically (usually quarterly) to confirm ongoing effectiveness.
  • Treating all swaps as identical — plain vanilla IRS, basis swap, and cross-currency swap have fundamentally different risk profiles and accounting treatments. Do not apply IRS valuation logic to currency swaps.
  • Missing the tax shield timing in APV vs WACC — APV values the tax shield separately and can handle changing leverage over time. WACC assumes a constant debt ratio; using WACC when leverage varies gives incorrect NPV.
  • Incorrectly computing goodwill in amalgamation — remember: Goodwill = Consideration + NCI + FV of previous interest held – FV of net identifiable assets (including contingent liabilities). Missing any component gives wrong goodwill.
  • Not annualizing VaR correctly — 1-day VaR multiplied by √n gives n-day VaR ONLY if returns are i.i.d. and normally distributed. This assumption often fails during crises.
  • Applying PE ratio for unprofitable companies — P/E is meaningless for negative earnings. Use P/S, EV/EBITDA, or DCF instead. Don't force a P/E comparison where it doesn't apply.
  • Ignoring stamp duty and transaction costs in merger valuations — these costs affect the net benefit of a merger and should be deducted from the synergy value to arrive at the true acquisition premium.
  • Using the wrong beta for project valuation — use the asset beta (unlevered beta) of comparable firms, then relever it using the project's target capital structure. Using equity beta directly ignores the project's specific leverage.
  • Confusing economic exposure with transaction exposure — economic exposure affects the entire firm's competitive position and future cash flows over years; transaction exposure is about specific contracted amounts due in the near term.
  • Overlooking the impact of Indian tax law changes — Section 43(5) defines capital vs revenue receipts; amendments to dividend distribution tax (now subsumed under income tax) and STT rates affect SFM decisions. Stay updated with latest Finance Act provisions.
  • Assuming M&M irrelevance holds in India — India has corporate tax, personal tax, bankruptcy costs, asymmetric information, and agency problems. MM's perfect-market assumptions are violated; real-world capital structure DOES matter.
  • Incorrect hedge designation — under Ind AS 109, a hedge must be formally designated at inception with documented risk-management objective and strategy. Retrospective designation is not permitted.
