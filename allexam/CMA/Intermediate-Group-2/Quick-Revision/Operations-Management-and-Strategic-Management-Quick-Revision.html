Operations Management and Strategic Management — Quick Revision | CMA Intermediate Group 2

CMAEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Operations Management: The systematic direction and control of the processes that transform inputs (materials, labour, capital, information) into finished goods and services of specified value.
  • Strategic Management: The comprehensive set of managerial actions and decisions involved in formulating and implementing plans designed to achieve organisational objectives and sustainable competitive advantage.
  • Total Quality Management (TQM): A management philosophy that seeks long-term success through customer satisfaction by involving all employees in continuous improvement of processes, products, services, and culture.
  • Six Sigma: A data-driven methodology aiming to reduce process variation and defects to no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO), following the DMAIC framework (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control).
  • Economic Order Quantity (EOQ): The mathematically optimal order size that minimises total inventory cost, given by EOQ = √(2DS/H), where D = annual demand, S = ordering/setup cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year.
  • Just-in-Time (JIT): An inventory management philosophy where materials and components arrive exactly when needed in the production process, eliminating excess inventory and reducing carrying costs.
  • ABC Analysis: An inventory classification technique based on Pareto principle — Class A items are high-value, low-volume (tightest control); Class B moderate; Class C low-value, high-volume (simpler control).
  • Master Production Schedule (MPS): A detailed plan specifying the quantity and timing of end-item production over a specified horizon, derived from forecasts, orders, and capacity constraints.
  • Aggregate Planning: Medium-term capacity planning (typically 3–18 months) that balances demand and capacity at the product-family level before detailed scheduling.
  • Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP): Integrated software systems that unify core business processes (finance, HR, procurement, manufacturing, sales) into a single database-driven platform.
  • Supply Chain Management (SCM): The coordination and integration of all activities across suppliers, manufacturers, distributors, and customers to deliver products efficiently and meet service expectations.
  • SWOT Analysis: A strategic planning tool assessing internal Strengths and Weaknesses and external Opportunities and Threats to inform strategy formulation.
  • Porter's Five Forces: Michael Porter's framework evaluating industry competitiveness through the lenses of (1) threat of new entrants, (2) bargaining power of suppliers, (3) bargaining power of buyers, (4) threat of substitute products, (5) rivalry among existing competitors.
  • PESTLE Analysis: Macro-environmental scanning framework examining Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental factors impacting strategic decisions.
  • Competitive Advantage: The attribute that allows an organisation to outperform its rivals, typically achieved through cost leadership or differentiation (Porter's generic strategies).
  • Value Chain Analysis: A strategic tool that dissects firm activities into primary (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service) and support (procurement, technology development, HR, infrastructure) activities to identify value-creation and cost-reduction opportunities.

Important facts

  • ISO 9001:2015 is the current international standard for Quality Management Systems; it follows the Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycle and emphasises risk-based thinking and process approach.
  • ISO 14001:2015 addresses Environmental Management Systems; ISO 45001:2018 covers Occupational Health and Safety.
  • W. Edwards Deming's 14 Points for Management remain foundational to TQM — key points include constancy of purpose, drive out fear, break down barriers between departments, and cease dependence on mass inspection.
  • Joseph Juran contributed the concept of quality planning, quality control, and quality improvement (the Juran Trilogy) and the Pareto principle applied to quality (vital few, trivial many).
  • Genichi Taguchi introduced loss function — any deviation from target creates societal loss; robust design minimises sensitivity to variation.
  • Cpk (process capability index) ≥ 1.33 is generally the target for capable processes; Cp measures potential capability assuming centreing, Cpk accounts for centre shift.
  • In EOQ, total cost curve is flat near the optimum — small deviations from EOQ do not significantly increase total cost, providing practical flexibility.
  • Kanban is a visual signalling system (card, bin, electronic) used in JIT to authorise production or movement of materials — there are typically production kanbans and withdrawal kanbans.
  • Single-Minute Exchange of Die (SMED) reduces setup time to under 10 minutes, enabling smaller lot sizes and greater flexibility without sacrificing efficiency.
  • Little's Law: WIP = Throughput Rate × Flow Time — fundamental relationship in queueing theory applicable to process analysis and capacity calculations.
  • Throughput Accounting (Goldratt) focuses on Throughput (sales minus totally variable costs), Investment, and Operating Expense; maximising throughput is the priority.
  • The Theory of Constraints (TOC) identifies the system's bottleneck; the five focusing steps are: Identify the constraint, Exploit it, Subordinate everything else, Elevate the constraint, Repeat — preventing inertia from becoming the new constraint.
  • PERT (Program Evaluation Review Technique) uses three time estimates — optimistic (a), most likely (m), pessimistic (b); expected time = (a + 4m + b)/6; variance = [(b-a)/6]².
  • Critical Path Method (CPM) uses deterministic single time estimates; critical path = longest duration path through network, zero float; any delay on critical path delays project completion.
  • SCOR model (Supply Chain Operations Reference) classifies supply chain processes into Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return, Enable — standardises supply chain measurement and benchmarking.
  • Lean thinking pillars: Value (defined by customer), Value stream (map all steps), Flow (make value flow without interruption), Pull (let customer draw value), Perfection (continuous improvement).
  • Porter's Value Chain has 5 primary activities and 4 support activities; the intersection points identify where competitive advantage can be built or eroded.
  • Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim & Mauborgne) advocates creating uncontested market space rather than competing in existing industry boundaries — value innovation (differentiation + low cost simultaneously).
  • Stakeholder theory (Freeman) argues firms must balance interests of shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, community, and environment beyond shareholder primacy.
  • Core competency (Prahalad & Hamel) — uniquely valuable, difficult-to-imitate capabilities that provide access to diverse markets and contribute significantly to perceived customer benefits.

Memory aids

  • EOQ mnemonic: 'Deep Sea Heroes' → EOQ = √(2DS/H) — D=demand, S=setup/order cost, H=holding cost.
  • Quality tools order for problem-solving: 'Catch Pareto, Fish then Chart, Flow the Histogram' → Check sheet → Pareto → Fishbone → Flowchart → Histogram → Scatter → Control chart.
  • Porter's Five Forces: 'Silly Bears Throw Rocks' → Suppliers, Buyers, Threat of new Entrants, Substitute products, Rivalry.
  • PESTLE: 'Paul Eat Green Lunch Today' → Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental.
  • Balanced Scorecard four perspectives: 'Financial Customers Internal Learn' → mnemonically group as money, clients, processes, people.
  • DMAIC: 'Does Managers Always Invite Clients' → Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control.
  • Juran Trilogy: 'Plan Control Improve' → JP-CI = also sounds like a name to anchor memory.
  • Ansoff Matrix risk order: 'Penetration → Development → Product → Diversification' — remember PDP-D: the more novel the product/market combination, the higher the risk.
  • Little's Law: 'Wait In Process = Rate × Time' → WIP = λ × T — intuitive: more items in queue means longer wait.
  • TQM Deming 14 Points — focus anchor: 'Create constancy, drive fear, eliminate slogans, lead, improve constantly, train, educate, adopt transformation, drive barriers, eliminate numbers, inspire, remove obstacles, education, action.'
  • SCOR model: 'Please Send My Delivery Right Away' → Plan, Source, Make, Deliver, Return, Enable.

Exam tips

  • For numerical questions on EOQ, always verify units — demand, ordering cost, and holding cost must be on the same time basis (all annual or all per order period).
  • In capacity utilisation problems, distinguish design capacity from effective capacity before computing utilisation percentages — ICMAI frequently tests this distinction.
  • When asked about TQM principles, cite specific contributors (Deming, Juran, Taguchi) with their key contributions — names add credibility and earn marks in descriptive answers.
  • For competitive strategy questions, explicitly state that cost leadership and differentiation are mutually exclusive in Porter's framework unless a focus strategy bridges them — examiners look for this nuance.
  • In process selection questions, link layout type directly to volume-variety considerations; use a volume-variety matrix to justify your answer for structured responses.
  • When writing about ERP advantages, balance benefits against implementation challenges (cost, complexity, change resistance, data migration) — holistic answers score better.
  • For quantitative questions involving PERT/CPM, show network diagram construction steps clearly — partial credit is awarded even if the final critical path is wrong.
  • Distinguish between operation management decisions (short/medium term, tactical) and strategic management decisions (long term, directional) — the question stem usually signals which time-horizon perspective is expected.
  • Use industry-standard terminology: prefer 'throughput' over 'output', 'work-in-process' (WIP) over 'semi-finished goods', 'lead time' over 'delivery time' — precision matters in CMA exams.
  • When discussing risk in SCM, mention both mitigation strategies (dual sourcing, safety stock) and structural strategies (nearshoring, postponement, flexible contracts) to demonstrate breadth.
  • In quality-related questions, relate statistical tools to their correct application context — e.g., control charts for monitoring stability, Pareto for prioritisation, scatter diagrams for correlation, not vice versa.
  • For strategic management case-based questions, start with environmental analysis (external + internal), move to strategy choice, then address implementation — follow the formal strategic management process structure.

Common traps

  • Confusing Cp with Cpk: Cp assumes perfectly centred process; Cpk accounts for centre shift. A process can have high Cp but low Cpk if off-centre — always report Cpk.
  • Using EOQ with quantity discounts without comparing total cost at each breakpoint — the EOQ itself may not be the cost-minimising order quantity when price breaks exist.
  • Treating JIT as merely low inventory: JIT is a complete production philosophy; reducing inventory without improving quality, setup times, and supplier reliability causes stockouts, not savings.
  • Assuming critical path is fixed: in PERT with probabilistic times, a non-critical path with high variance can become critical — always consider near-critical paths.
  • Equating strategy formulation with strategy execution: well-crafted strategy fails without proper implementation (structure, culture, resources, leadership). ICMAI expects both aspects.
  • Applying Six Sigma DMAIC to innovation projects instead of improvement projects: new product development uses DMADV (Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, Verify) instead.
  • Confusing related vs unrelated diversification: related diversification shares resources/competencies (lower risk, synergy potential); unrelated is conglomerate (financial synergy only, higher management complexity).
  • Thinking TQM means 'everyone inspects': TQM shifts quality responsibility to process owners and aims to prevent defects — inspection is eliminated, not expanded.
  • Applying level strategy when demand volatility is extreme: constant-rate production with huge inventories can tie up excessive working capital; chase or mixed may be superior.
  • Ignoring behavioural aspects of change in ERP/technology implementation: technical success ≠ operational success — resistance, training gaps, and cultural mismatch commonly cause failures.
  • Overlooking backward vs forward vertical integration distinction: backward = acquiring suppliers (control inputs); forward = acquiring distributors/retailers (control delivery to customer).
  • Using PESTLE analysis as a static exercise: macro-environmental factors change continuously — strategic plans must incorporate dynamic environmental monitoring, not one-off analysis.
  • Treating all inventory equally under ABC: Class C items are wrongly given equal control effort as Class A, wasting management time; conversely, neglecting occasional Class B items can cause disruptions.
