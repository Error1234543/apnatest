CMA Intermediate – Management Accounting | Quick Revision

CMAEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Management Accounting: The systematic process of identifying, measuring, analyzing, and communicating financial and non-financial information to managers for effective planning, controlling, and decision-making within an organization.
  • Variance: The difference between a standard or budgeted figure and the actual figure achieved, expressed as favorable or adverse.
  • Standard Costing: A technique where predetermined costs are established for products/services, and actual costs are compared against standards to identify variances for control purposes.
  • Budgetary Control: The process of establishing budgets, comparing actual results with budgeted figures, and taking corrective action to ensure organizational objectives are achieved.
  • Responsibility Accounting: An accounting system that identifies areas of responsibility within an organization and reports on the performance of each responsible unit or manager.
  • Transfer Pricing: The price at which goods, services, or intangibles are transferred between divisional units or segments of the same organization, used for internal performance evaluation.
  • Balanced Scorecard: A strategic performance management framework that measures organizational performance across four perspectives – Financial, Customer, Internal Process, and Learning & Growth – rather than relying solely on financial metrics.
  • Working Capital: The difference between current assets and current liabilities; represents the short-term liquidity available to fund day-to-day operations of a business.
  • Marginal Costing: A costing technique where only variable/manufacturing costs are charged to products, while fixed costs are treated as period costs and written off against contribution.
  • Capital Budgeting: The process of evaluating and selecting long-term investment proposals whose cash flows extend beyond one year, using techniques like NPV, IRR, Payback Period, and ARR

Key concepts

  • Material Variances – Subcategories: Material Price Variance = (Standard Price – Actual Price) × Actual Quantity; Material Usage Variance = (Standard Quantity – Actual Quantity) × Standard Price; Material Yield Variance = (Actual Yield – Standard Yield from given input) × Standard Cost per Unit; Material Mix Variance = Revised Standard Cost – Standard Cost of Actual Mix; combined into Total Material Cost Variance. Key point: always compute price variance first, then usage/yield/mix carefully when materials are substitutable.
  • Labor Variances – Subcategories: Labor Rate Variance = (Standard Rate – Actual Rate) × Actual Hours; Labor Efficiency (Usage) Variance = (Standard Hours – Actual Hours) × Standard Rate; Labor Idle Time Variance = Idle Hours × Standard Rate; Labor Mix/ gang wage variations apply when multiple grades work together; total is Labor Cost Variance. Note: wage rates may differ due to overtime premiums or shift differentials.
  • Budget Types & Techniques: Fixed/Static Budget (single-level, no adjustment), Flexible Budget (adjusted to actual output level), Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB – every expense justified from scratch, not based on prior periods), Rolling/Continuous Budget (updated periodically with new period added), Differential Budgeting (focuses on changes), Master Budget (aggregate of all functional budgets), Cash Budget (projection of cash receipts and payments). ZBB stages: managerial perspective → identification of activities → alternative forms → cost-benefit analysis → ranking & funding.
  • Ratio Analysis Framework: Classification includes Liquidity Ratios (Current Ratio, Quick/Liquid Ratio), Solvency/Long-term Ratios (Debt-Equity, Proprietary, Interest Coverage), Activity/Turnover Ratios (Inventory Turnover, Debtor/Creditor Days), Profitability Ratios (Gross Profit, Net Profit, ROCE, ROE), Market Value Ratios (EPS, P/E, Dividend Yield). Interpretation requires trend analysis, inter-firm comparison, and industry benchmarks. Limitations: historical data, inflation effects, window-dressing ignored, qualitative factors excluded, varying accounting policies distort comparison.
  • CVP & Break-Even Analysis: Break-Even Point (units) = Fixed Cost ÷ Contribution per Unit; Break-Even Point (value) = Fixed Cost ÷ P/V Ratio, where P/V Ratio = (Sales – Variable Cost)/Sales × 100. Margin of Safety = Actual Sales – Break-Even Sales. Key assumptions: cost behavior (fixed vs variable separation), linear revenue and cost functions, constant selling price, sales mix stability (multi-product). Sensitivity analysis modifies BEP for changes in price, cost, or volume.
  • Capital Budgeting Decisions – Discounted Cash Flow Methods: Net Present Value (NPV) = PV of Cash Inflows – Initial Investment; accept if NPV > 0. Internal Rate of Return (IRR) = discount rate making NPV = 0; accept if IRR > required rate. Profitability Index (PI) = PV of Cash Inflows / Initial Investment; accept if PI > 1. Non-DCF Methods: Payback Period = Initial Investment / Annual Cash Inflow (when uniform); Average Rate of Return = Average Post-tax Profit / Average Investment. DCF methods superior because they account for time value of money; PI useful under capital rationing for project selection rankings.
  • Workinig Capital Management: Components include Inventory (EOQ model: EOQ = √(2 × D × S / H), where D=demand, S=order cost, H=h per unit holding cost), Receivables (credit policy evaluation: cost of carrying receivables vs benefit of increased sales), Payables (trade credit decisions, discount vs credit terms), Cash Management (Cash Budget preparation, models: Baumol model, Miller-Orr model for cash balance management). Objective is to optimize between liquidity and profitability.
  • Transfer Pricing Principles: Defined as price charged when one division transfers goods/services to another division. Pure economic theory suggests market price is ideal; practical alternatives include cost-based (full cost, cost plus %, marginal cost), negotiated price, dual pricing. Problems arise from sub-optimal divisional decisions and goal congruence failure. ICMAI expects discussion of objectives, methods, implications on performance evaluation, and tax/tariff considerations in group contexts.
  • Performance Measurement – Beyond Financials: Traditional financial ratios miss strategic drivers. Balanced Scorecard approach: map strategy → define objectives per perspective → develop measures/targets/action plans. Financial perspective: ROCE, EVA. Customer: satisfaction, retention, market share. Internal Process: cycle time, defect rate. Learning & Growth: employee skills, system capability. This holistic view prevents short-termism and aligns operations with strategy.
  • Throughput Accounting & Theory of Constraints: Developed by Goldratt; Throughput = Sales – Total Variable Cost (where TVC is typically raw materials only); Throughput per bottleneck unit = Throughput / Consumption of bottleneck resource; Return per investment unit = Throughput / Throughput invested (inventory + operating expense). Priority is to maximize throughput through bottleneck. Contrast with traditional ABS: ABC allocates overhead; throughput accounting ignores overhead allocation for decision-making. Key metrics: Throughput Accounting Ratio (TAR) = Throughput per bottleneck hour / Operating cost per bottleneck hour; accept if TAR > 1.
  • Target Costing & Life Cycle Costing: Target Costing = Market-driven price (minus desired profit margin); gap analysis between current cost estimate and target cost drives cost reduction. Steps: define product function → determine price/cost → allocate cost targets by component → redesign/eliminate gaps. Life Cycle Costing tracks costs over full product life (R&D, design, production, marketing, distribution, service, disposal). Both are strategic tools: target costing focuses on design-stage cost management, life cycle costing ensures full-cost awareness for pricing and profitability decisions. Value Chain Analysis maps all value-creating activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing, service) and supports cost positioning.

Important facts

  • Total Material Cost Variance = (SQ × SP) – (AQ × AP), decomposable into Price + Usage (+ Yield + Mix where applicable). Total Labor Cost Variance = (SH × SR) – (AH × AR), decomposable into Rate + Efficiency (+ Idle + Mix/Gang where applicable). Always ensure units of quantity match — price variance uses AQ, usage variance uses SQ.
  • Flexible Budget formula for variable items: Budgeted Variable Cost at Actual Output = Variable Cost per Unit × Actual Output. Fixed Costs in Flexible Budget remain unchanged from the original budget within relevant range unless step-fixed behavior applies.
  • P/V Ratio (Profit-Volume Ratio) = Contribution/Sales × 100. It remains constant within relevant range assuming C-V relationship holds. Higher P/V ratio indicates higher profitability. P/V ratio can also be computed as (Increase in Profit / Increase in Sales) when volume changes. This is the slope of the CVP line.
  • Break-Even Chart features: X-axis = output, Y-axis = cost/revenue. Total Cost line starts from fixed cost intercept. Sales Revenue line starts from origin. BEP is intersection of Total Cost and Sales lines. Below BEP = loss zone; above BEP = profit zone. Margin of Safety shown as horizontal distance from BEP to actual sales. Multiple BEPs possible with multi-product mix.
  • NPV decision rule: Positive NPV means project adds value; NPV = 0 is indifferent (return equals cost of capital); Negative NPV destroys value. When ranking mutually exclusive projects with unequal lives, use Equivalent Annual Annuity (EAA) method alongside NPV. For projects with different scales, PI assists comparison.
  • Working Capital Formula: Net Working Capital = Current Assets – Current Liabilities. Gross Working Capital = Total Current Assets. Cash conversion cycle = Inventory days + Receivables days – Payables days. Reduction in CCC improves liquidity without raising external finance.
  • Transfer pricing must satisfy three conditions simultaneously: goal congruence (divisional decisions aligned with organizational goals), autonomy (divisional managers free to make decisions), and performance evaluation accuracy. Market price satisfies all; cost-based often fails; negotiated price depends on relative bargaining power.
  • ZBB advantages over traditional budgeting: eliminates inherited waste, encourages innovation, allocates resources efficiently, promotes awareness of cost drivers. ZBB disadvantages: time-consuming, requires extensive documentation and training, creates resistance from middle management, may overlook qualitative factors.
  • EVA (Economic Value Added) = NOPAT – (Capital Employed × Weighted Average Cost of Capital). Unlike accounting profit, EVA deducts cost of equity capital, providing a clearer measure of true value creation. Adjustments to accounting profit include R&D capitalization, lease adjustments, and inventory LIFO reserve reversal.
  • Activity-Based Costing (ABC) vs Traditional Absorption Costing: ABC assigns overheads based on cost drivers (activities), reflecting actual consumption; absorption costing uses single plant-wide rate often based on labor hours or machine hours. ABC provides more accurate product cost, reveals unprofitable products, and supports better pricing decisions. ABC is superior for diversified product ranges with varying complexity; less advantageous when overhead proportion is small. Key term: cost pool, cost driver, cost object.

Memory aids

  • Variance formulas remembered via 'P-Q' and 'Q-S': Price variance uses Actual Qty and Standard/Actual Price; Usage/Yield/Mix variances all involve Standard Price × difference in quantities. Think: 'Price talks about money, Usage talks about matter.'
  • Flexible budget is like stretching a rubber band — variable costs stretch with output, fixed costs stay rigid. Picture a 'flexible menu' at a restaurant where portion sizes change but base prices don't.
  • P/V Ratio = 'Profit Velocity' — how fast each rupee of sales converts to profit. The steeper the line on a graph, the faster it builds contribution. High P/V ratio = high profitability per sale.
  • Break-Even Picture: imagine standing at the bottom of a valley — below BEP you're in the 'loss dip', above BEP you climb the 'profit hill'. Margin of Safety is your running start before the hill begins.
  • Capital Budgeting mnemonic 'NI-PAY': NPV → IRR → Payback, the natural hierarchy of preference. DCF always beats non-DCF because money has a time value — ₹1 today is worth more than ₹1 tomorrow.
  • Working capital = the bloodstream of the business. Inventory is where money sleeps, debtors where money travels, creditors where money borrows time. Cash conversion cycle counts how many days money is tied up in transit.
  • Transfer pricing = the bridge between divisions. Bridge too weak (underpriced) and seller division suffers; bridge too strong (overpriced) and buyer division chokes. Fair price keeps traffic flowing smoothly.
  • Balanced Scorecard = four pillars of a temple. Financial roof protects it; Customer columns support it; Internal process beams hold it; Learning & Growth foundation sustains it. Collapse one pillar = collapse the whole structure.
  • Target Costing mantra: 'Price minus Profit equals Cost' — reverse of cost-plus which says 'Cost plus Profit equals Price'. Target costing starts from outside (market), cost-plus starts from inside (factory).
  • Theory of Constraints visualized as a chain — weakest link determines strength. Bottleneck = the weak link. Throughput accounting says maximize throughput through the bottleneck because that's what constrains the entire chain.

Exam tips

  • CMA Intermediate Management Accounting carries significant weightage in Paper 7. Allocate adequate practice time to variance analysis and capital budgeting — numerical questions from these areas frequently appear with high marks.
  • For variance problems, always write the formula, substitute values clearly, and state direction (F/A) explicitly. Partial marks are awarded for correct methodology even if computation is wrong. Show unit cancellation to demonstrate dimensional consistency.
  • When answering Ratio Analysis theoretical questions, always discuss limitations and inter-firm comparability issues — ICMAI examiners expect critical analysis, not just formula recitation. Mention at least three limitations for full marks.
  • In CVP analysis, always state assumptions before solving (cost behavior, linear relationships, constant mix, selling price stability). These assumptions form the foundation of your analysis and demonstrate conceptual clarity.
  • For Capital Budgeting, learn NPV calculation using annuity tables and single-sum discount factors thoroughly. Practice both manual discounting and table-based approaches since tables may or may not be provided in the exam.
  • Transfer Pricing questions often carry theory + case-based format. Structure answer with: definition → objectives → methods → advantages/disadvantages → conclusion. Use headings and bullet points for readability under timed conditions.
  • ZBB and Balanced Scorecard are frequent theoretical topics. Prepare structured notes with steps, features, advantages, and limitations. Caselet application questions may ask you to recommend whether a company should adopt ZBB or BSC — prepare justification paragraphs.
  • For ABC costing questions, distinguish clearly between traditional absorption and ABC methods. Draw the cost flow diagram: resources → activities → cost pools → cost objects. This visual representation fetches extra presentation marks.
  • When discussing Working Capital Management, link decisions to the trade-off between liquidity and profitability. Examiners appreciate recognition that excessive working capital reduces returns, while insufficient working capital risks insolvency.
  • Review past ICMAI study material topic-wise. Focus on 'Illustrations' and 'Summary' sections — they reveal examiner preferences. Practice writing answers within time limits, as descriptive and numerical questions both require structured presentation.

Common traps

  • Mixing up variance directions: Favorable means better than standard (actual cost < standard cost for expenses, actual revenue > standard revenue), Adverse means worse. Student trap: sign reversal — always remember Expense Favorable = negative variance (saving), but Revenue Favorable = positive variance. Label clearly as F/A in exams.
  • Yield vs Mix confusion: Yield variance isolates effect of total input quantity variance; Mix variance isolates effect of proportionate blend change. Apply yield first, then mix — both use standard price but compare different bases. If no substitution is possible (single material), yield and mix are not applicable.
  • Flexible budget trap: applying variable cost rates to wrong output level (using budgeted output instead of actual output), or incorrectly treating fixed costs as variable. Fixed costs should remain unchanged unless step-fixed within the range.
  • P/V Ratio errors: computing it as Sales/Contribution instead of Contribution/Sales; not converting to percentage when asked; ignoring that it changes with sales mix in multi-product scenarios. Also, confusing BEP (units) with BEP (value) calculation.
  • Capital budgeting – NPV vs IRR conflict: mutually exclusive projects may show conflicting rankings under NPV and IRR due to scale differences or timing patterns. NPV is superior in such conflicts. Also, multiple IRRs can occur with non-conventional cash flow patterns (alternating signs).
  • Transfer pricing – ignoring opportunity cost: if a selling division operates at full capacity, the transfer price should include lost contribution from external sales. Ignoring this leads to incorrect divisional performance evaluation.
  • Working capital – confusing gross and net: Gross Working Capital = total current assets; Net Working Capital = current assets – current liabilities. Different questions require different answers. Also, working capital financing decisions: matching principle suggests long-term funds finance permanent current assets, short-term funds finance temporary current assets.
  • Accounting policy changes – ignoring comparability: ratio comparisons across years may be misleading if accounting policies changed (e.g., depreciation method switched from straight-line to WDV). Always note policy changes when interpreting trends.
  • Zero-Based Budgeting – misconception trap: ZBB does not mean zero expenditure; it means zero-based justification — every activity must justify its existence and budget level from ground zero.
  • Life Cycle vs Target Costing – conceptual mix-up: Life Cycle Costing accumulates costs over the entire product life; Target Costing sets a cost ceiling at product design stage. They serve different purposes — one is retrospective/analytical, the other is prospective/strategic.
