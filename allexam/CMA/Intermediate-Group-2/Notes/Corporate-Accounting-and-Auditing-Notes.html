Section 1: Issue of Shares — Fundamentals, Allotment and Forfeiture

Share capital represents the portion of a company's capital raised by issuing shares to shareholders. In CMA Intermediate corporate accounting, the issue of shares forms the foundation of all share-related problems and carries significant weight in examination.

Types of Share Issues:
- Issue at Par: Shares issued at face value. The most straightforward scenario.
- Issue at Premium: Shares issued above face value. The premium is transferred to Securities Premium Account under Section 47 of the Companies Act, 2013, and can be utilised only for specified purposes (writing off preliminary expenses, issuing fully paid bonus shares, paying up unrealised profits, buying back shares, etc.).
- Issue at Discount: Permitted only under Section 53 with specific conditions — previous issues must be at a premium, discount cannot exceed 10%, and particulars must be disclosed in each prospectus.

Application, Allotment and Call Mechanics:
A standard issue follows: Application → Allotment → Calls (First Call, Second Call, Final Call). Each stage has separate journal entries. Excess application money is adjusted towards allotment or calls due. If an applicant applies for more shares than allotted pro-rata, excess money is refunded or adjusted.

Pro-Rata Allotment:
When applications exceed available shares, pro-rata allotment is made. Formula: Allotment = (Shares Applied × Total Shares Available) / Total Applications Received. Surplus application money is adjusted towards the amount due on allotment/calls. Any excess beyond that is refunded.

Forfeiture of Shares:
Forfeiture occurs when a shareholder fails to pay call money despite reminders. The shares are cancelled and the shareholder loses all rights. Journal entries for forfeiture:
1. Share Capital A/c Dr. (called-up value of forfeited shares) To Forfeited Shares A/c
2. If any amount was already received (application/allotment/call), it is adjusted against the called-up value.
The Forfeited Shares Account balance represents the profit made on forfeiture. It is a capital profit transferable to Capital Reserve Account.

Re-issue of Forfeited Shares:
When forfeited shares are re-issued, the journal entry credits Forfeited Shares Account and debits Bank/Receiver. Re-issue may be at par, at a premium, or at a discount. If re-issued at a discount, the loss on re-issue is first adjusted against the Forfeited Shares balance, with any shortfall transferred to Capital Reserve. The maximum loss on re-issue cannot exceed the original forfeiture profit.

Section 2: Redemption of Debentures — Methods and Accounting Treatment

Debentures represent a loan from the public, secured or unsecured, carrying a fixed rate of interest. Redemption refers to the repayment of debenture money to debenture holders.

Methods of Redemption:
1. Redemption by Payment in Lump Sum: Debentures are redeemed on a specific date by paying the full amount. Requires creation of Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) as per Companies Act, 2013 provisions. For listed companies, DRR must be at least 25% of the debenture issue. For non-listed public companies, DRR was earlier required but subsequent amendments have modified this. The DRR is created out of profits and can be utilised only for writing off losses or paying up fully paid bonus shares.

2. Redemption by Purchase in the Open Market: The company buys its own debentures from the stock exchange/open market. This requires a special resolution. Debentures purchased are cancelled and the premium on redemption, if any, is debited to Profit and Loss Account (or Securities Premium Account if the company has sufficient balance). The Profit and Loss Account is debited for the premium on redemption if the company has insufficient securities premium.

3. Conversion into Shares: Existing debenture holders are given shares in lieu of debentures. This is governed by Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013. The conversion must be offered to all debenture holders equally.

Accounting Treatment:
- When debentures are redeemed at a premium: Profit and Loss Account (or Securities Premium Account) is debited for the premium amount. The premium is treated as a loss on redemption.
- Debenture Redemption Reserve is created annually from distributable profits before redemption begins. Entries: P&L A/c Dr. To DRR A/c. On redemption: DRR A/c Dr. To Investment in Company's Debentures A/c (if held as investment) or directly written off.
- For open market purchase: Bank A/c Dr. To Bank A/c (purchase); Debentures A/c Dr. To Investment in Debentures A/c; P&L A/c Dr. (premium, if any) To Investment in Debentures A/c.

Important Distinction: DRR is created for redemption by lump sum payment. No DRR requirement exists for redemption by purchase in open market or by conversion, though good practice often involves setting aside funds.

Section 3: Issue of Shares for Consideration Other Than Cash and Buy-Back of Securities

Issue of Shares for Non-Cash Consideration:
Companies often issue shares as payment for acquisition of assets, business undertakings, or settlement of liabilities. Key principles:
- When shares are issued for acquisition of a business: All assets and liabilities of the business are taken over at agreed values. The net assets (assets minus liabilities) are credited to the Vendor/Company acquiring the business. Any difference between consideration paid and net asset value is recorded as Goodwill or Capital Reserve.
- Journal entry: All Assets A/c Dr. (individual asset accounts), To Liabilities A/c (individual liability accounts), To Vendor/Vendee Company A/c (net consideration), To Gain on Business Transfer A/c (if any).
- When shares are issued for payment of a liability: Simply debit the relevant creditor account and credit Share Capital account. If issued at a premium, additional entry credits Securities Premium Account.

Buy-Back of Securities (Section 68, Companies Act, 2013):
A company may buy back its own securities subject to strict conditions:
- Buy-back不得超过 25% of total paid-up equity capital and free reserves in a financial year.
- Ratio to total free reserves + securities premium + capital redemption reserve must not exceed 25%.
- Debt-equity ratio must not exceed 2:1 after buy-back (unless it was below 2:1 before).
- Securities must be fully paid up.
- Board resolution is required if buy-back exceeds 25% in a year or 25% of fresh issuance.
- Notice to ROC within 7 days of passing resolution.
- No further issue of same kind of securities during 6 months from completion of buy-back.
- Shareholders holding 10% or more cannot participate.
- Offer must be through tender offer or proportionate basis (if proportionate, eligible shareholders must hold at least 10% of equity). Minimum purchase offer must not be less than 25% of total shares sought to be bought.
- Time limit: Buy-back must be completed within 12 months from the date of passing the resolution.

Accounting Treatment of Buy-Back:
- Equity Share Capital A/c Dr. (face value) To Bank A/c (buy-back price)
- Securities Premium A/c or Capital Redemption Reserve A/c Dr. To Bank A/c (for premium portion)
- If shares are cancelled, no re-issue treatment is needed since they are extinguished.
- Excess of buy-back consideration over face value is debited to Securities Premium Account or Capital Redemption Reserve or P&L Account (in that order of priority).

Section 4: Floating Charge, Fixed Charge and Related Accounting

Charges on assets are security interests created over company property to secure loans or debentures. Classification is critical for both accounting and legal purposes.

Fixed Charge:
- Created on specific identifiable assets (land, building, machinery, patents).
- The company cannot deal with the charged asset without the charge holder's consent.
- Registered on specific assets at the time of creation.
- Priority over floating charge in winding up.
- Under Section 77 of Companies Act, 2013, charges must be registered with the Registrar of Companies within 30 days.

Floating Charge:
- Created on a class of assets which change from time to time (stock-in-trade, debtors, raw materials).
- The company can deal with these assets in the ordinary course of business without the charge holder's consent.
- 'Floats' over the assets until crystallisation.
- Crystallisation occurs when the company goes into liquidation, the charge holder takes possession, or the company ceases business.
- Upon crystallisation, floating charge converts into fixed charge over the assets existing at that time.

Accounting Implications:
- Interest on secured loan: charged to P&L Account regardless of whether secured by fixed or floating charge.
- In case of default, secured creditors have priority. Fixed charge holders have first claim on the specific asset.
- Floating charge holders rank after fixed charge holders and preferential creditors (like employees' dues) in winding up.
- Disclosure: Both fixed and floating charges must be disclosed in the Balance Sheet notes and in the charge register maintained by the company.
- When debentures are secured by both fixed and floating charges, the debenture trust deed specifies the terms and the priority of repayment.

Section 5: Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) — Creation, Utilization and Compliance

Debenture Redemption Reserve is a statutory reserve created specifically to ensure that the company has adequate funds available for redeeming debentures at the time of maturity.

Legal Position:
- Originally under Section 77 of the Companies Act, 1956 and Rule 18 of Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014, public companies were required to create DRR equal to at least 25% of the debenture issue for redemption by lump sum payment.
- The Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Amendment Rules, 2019 amended this significantly: DRR is now required only for non-government companies issuing or redeeming debentures in Indian currency on or after 1st October 2019. It is NOT required for foreign currency denominated debentures or for debentures issued by government companies, NBFCs covered under RBI regulations, or infrastructure companies.
- The DRR must be created out of the profits of the company available for distribution (i.e., distributable profits).

Creation and Utilization:
- Creation Entry: Profit and Loss Appropriation A/c Dr. To Debenture Redemption Reserve A/c. This is done annually before the redemption starts.
- The DRR can only be utilised for writing off losses of the company or for paying up fully paid bonus shares issued to existing shareholders.
- It cannot be used for any other purpose. This restriction ensures that the reserve remains intact until the actual redemption date.
- On redemption, the entry is: DRR A/c Dr. To Investment in Company's Debentures A/c (if the company purchases its own debentures as an investment) or simply DRR A/c Dr. To Bank A/c (if directly utilised for redemption).
- DRR is shown under Reserves and Surplus in the Balance Sheet under the head 'Reserves and Surplus' as part of Shareholders' Funds.

Compliance Points:
- DRR must be created at least one year before the date of redemption.
- If the company has sufficient profits, DRR should be created every year until the cumulative amount equals 25% of the total debenture amount.
- In case of redemption by purchase in open market, DRR is not statutorily mandatory but companies often maintain provisions.

Section 6: Accounts of Not-for-Profit Organizations

Not-for-Profit Organizations (NFPs) include clubs, societies, trusts, hospitals, charities, and religious institutions. They operate on a surplus-deficit basis rather than profit-loss. Their accounting follows the Receipts and Payments Basis and Income and Expenditure Basis.

Receipts and Payments Account:
- This is a real account summarising all cash/bank receipts and payments during the accounting period.
- It is essentially a summary of the Cash Book.
- Includes both revenue and capital items.
- Does not distinguish between income and expenditure; it records only cash flows.
- Opening and closing balances are shown as the first and last entries respectively.
- Does not account for outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, depreciation, or accrued income.

Income and Expenditure Account:
- This is a nominal account showing only revenue items that relate to the current accounting period.
- Prepared on accrual basis matching revenue receipts with revenue expenditures.
- Surplus (credit balance) is added to the Capital Fund; Deficit (debit balance) is deducted from the Capital Fund.
- Items to be adjusted: outstanding expenses are added; prepaid expenses are deducted; depreciation is provided; income received in advance is deducted; accrued income is added; items of capital nature are excluded.

Capital Fund:
- Represents the accumulated surplus of past years plus any life membership fees and subscription received.
- Capital Fund = Opening Balance + Surplus (or - Deficit) + Life Membership Fees.
- Life Membership Fees, Entrance Fees, and Subscription received from members are capital receipts and are shown on the Liability side of the Balance Sheet (not in the Income and Expenditure Account), unless the articles specifically allow spending them.

Specific Adjustments:
- Subscription Received: Calculate the subscription due for the current year by adjusting for outstanding subscriptions and prepayments. Only the current year's due amount goes to Income and Expenditure Account.
- Special Subscriptions: If received for a specific purpose (e.g., building fund), they are treated as capital receipts and shown on the liability side of the Balance Sheet.
- Donations with a specific purpose are similarly treated as capital receipts.
- Endowment Funds are shown on the liability side of the Balance Sheet.
- Accumulated Funds vs Capital Fund terminology varies — accumulated funds are shown when there is no single capital fund, while capital fund is used for a single consolidated fund.

Section 7: Concept, Nature and Scope of Auditing

Auditing is the systematic examination of financial records and statements to express an opinion on whether they present a true and fair view in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.

Definition and Nature:
- According to ICAI, auditing is 'the independent examination of information produced by a specific entity whereby an objective expression of opinion thereon is made.'
- Auditing is both art and science — art in applying professional judgment, science in using systematic methodology.
- It is an analytical and critical investigation of accounting records.
- The auditor's report provides assurance to stakeholders about the reliability of financial statements.

Objectives of Auditing:
- Primary Objective: To report on whether financial statements give a true and fair view and comply with statutory requirements.
- Secondary Objectives: Detection and prevention of errors and frauds (though not the primary duty of auditor), checking arithmetical accuracy, ensuring compliance with laws and regulations.
- The auditor is not primarily responsible for detecting fraud but must plan the audit to provide reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements are free from material misstatement, whether caused by fraud or error.

Scope of Auditing:
- Examining accounting records and supporting documents.
- Verifying the existence and ownership of assets and liabilities.
- Evaluating internal control systems.
- Checking compliance with statutory requirements and accounting standards.
- Reporting findings to stakeholders.

Difference Between Auditing and Accounting:
- Accounting is the process of recording, classifying and summarizing transactions to prepare financial statements. Auditing is the process of examining those financial statements to express an opinion.
- Accounting is proactive; auditing is retroactive.
- The accountant prepares the statements; the auditor examines them.

Difference Between Audit and Verification:
- Audit is the entire process from planning to reporting. Verification is a specific step within auditing — it involves confirming the existence, ownership, valuation and legal title of assets and liabilities shown in the Balance Sheet. Verification is a subset of audit.

Types of Audit:
- Statutory Audit: Required by law for companies. Conducted by a qualified chartered accountant.
- Internal Audit: Conducted by the organization's own staff to examine internal controls and processes.
- Tax Audit: Required under Section 44AB of Income Tax Act for businesses exceeding prescribed turnover limits.
- Cost Audit: Required under Section 148 of Companies Act for specified industries.
- Government Audit: Conducted by CAG for government companies and entities receiving government funds.

Section 8: Audit Planning, Audit Evidence and Vouching

Audit Planning:
- Audit planning involves developing an overall strategy for the conduct of the engagement. It includes understanding the entity and its environment, assessing risks, determining materiality, and designing further audit procedures.
- Elements of audit planning: Understanding the business and industry, reviewing prior year audit work, assessing internal control system, determining audit team composition, setting materiality levels, preparing audit programme.
- An audit programme is a set of questions to be asked and procedures to be followed by the audit staff. It provides a checklist to ensure thorough coverage.

Audit Evidence:
- Audit evidence comprises all information used by the auditor to arrive at the conclusions on which the audit opinion is based.
- Evidence must be sufficient (quantity) and appropriate (quality — relevance and reliability).
- Sources of evidence: Documentary evidence (invoices, contracts, bank statements), Oral evidence (representations from management), Environmental evidence (observation of processes), Analytical evidence (ratio analysis, trend analysis).
- Reliability hierarchy: External independent evidence > Internal evidence; Original documents > Copies; Direct personal knowledge > Indirect knowledge; Effective internal controls > Weak internal controls.

Vouching:
- Vouching is the examination of documentary evidence to verify the authenticity of transactions recorded in the books of account.
- It is the cornerstone of auditing and is applied primarily to the nominal accounts in the cash book, purchases book, sales book, and journal.
- The voucher is the primary evidence — every payment should be supported by an authorized voucher.
- Vouching checks: Date, amount, name of party, authorization, nature of transaction, accounting classification, and whether the transaction relates to the correct accounting period.
- Internal vouchers: Payment vouchers, receipt vouchers, journal vouchers, contra vouchers.
- External vouchers: Invoices, bills, receipts, bank statements, contracts.
- Vouching of specific items: Purchase vouching checks goods inward note, invoice, order, GRN. Sales vouching checks invoice, goods outward note, delivery challan. Salary vouching checks attendance registers, payroll records, tax deductions.

Section 9: Internal Control, Internal Audit and Verification of Assets and Liabilities

Internal Control:
- Internal control is a process designed to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in operational efficiency, reliable financial reporting, and compliance with laws and regulations.
- Components (COSO framework): Control Environment, Risk Assessment, Control Activities, Information and Communication, Monitoring.
- Types of internal controls: Preventive controls (designed to prevent errors/frauds), Detective controls (designed to discover errors/frauds after they occur), Corrective controls (designed to correct identified problems).
- Limitations of internal control: Human error, collusion, management override, cost-benefit considerations, changing circumstances.

Internal Audit:
- Internal audit is an independent appraisal function within an organization for the review of activities and the communication of results to management.
- Unlike external audit, internal audit is continuous and covers all aspects of the organization's operations.
- Functions: Examining and evaluating the adequacy and effectiveness of internal control, reviewing operational efficiency, ensuring compliance with policies and procedures, reporting to management on gaps and improvements.
- Difference from external audit: Internal audit is conducted by employees, is continuous, covers operational matters, reports to management. External audit is conducted by independent auditors, is periodic, focuses on financial statements, reports to shareholders.

Verification of Assets and Liabilities:
- Verification is the process of confirming the existence, ownership, valuation and legal title of assets and liabilities shown in the Balance Sheet.
- Verification of Assets:
* Tangible Fixed Assets: Verify title deeds, physical inspection, depreciation calculations, disclosure in notes.
* Intangible Assets: Verify registration certificates, amortization, impairment.
* Current Assets: Stock — physical verification, valuation (cost or NRV, whichever is lower). Debtors — confirmation from debtors, age-wise analysis, provision for bad debts. Cash — cash counterfoot, bank reconciliation.
- Verification of Liabilities:
* Secured Loans: Verify loan agreements, charge documents, repayment schedules.
* Unsecured Loans: Verify agreements, terms and conditions.
* Trade Payables: Confirmation from suppliers, ageing analysis.
* Provision and Reserves: Verify calculations, statutory compliance, proper classification.

Section 10: Auditor's Report — Types and Contents

An Auditor's Report is the formal opinion issued by the auditor after completing the audit engagement. It communicates to the stakeholders whether the financial statements present a true and fair view.

Contents of an Auditor's Report (as per Companies Act, 2013 and Standards on Auditing):
- Title: 'Independent Auditor's Report'
- Addressee: Usually the members/shareholders of the company
- Opinion Paragraph: Clear statement of whether financial statements give true and fair view
- Basis for Opinion: Explanation of audit standards followed, independence, and sufficiency of evidence
- Key Audit Matters (for listed entities): Areas requiring significant auditor attention
- Management Responsibility: Statement about preparation and maintenance of financial statements
- Auditor Responsibility: Statement about conducting audit in accordance with Standards on Auditing
- Report on Other Legal and Regulatory Requirements: Specific disclosures mandated by law
- Signature: Name of auditor, firm, membership number
- Date and Place of signing

Types of Auditor's Report:
1. Unqualified Report: Issued when financial statements present a true and fair view and no material misstatements are found. This is the ideal report.

2. Qualified Report: Issued when the auditor finds material misstatements but they are not pervasive to the financial statements as a whole. The qualification is clearly stated with reasons.

3. Adverse Report: Issued when misstatements are both material and pervasive — the financial statements do not present a true and fair view at all.

4. Disclaimer of Opinion: Issued when the auditor cannot obtain sufficient appropriate evidence and the possible effects could be both material and pervasive. The auditor disclaims forming an opinion.

Emphasis of Matter Paragraph:
- Added when the auditor considers it necessary to draw users' attention to a matter properly presented/disclosed that is fundamental to understanding the financial statements but does not affect the opinion.
- Examples: Significant uncertainty about future outcomes, major catastrophe affecting the entity, early application of a new accounting standard.

Qualification vs. Adverse vs. Disclaimer:
- Qualification: Material but not pervasive — a specific issue exists but overall statements are reliable.
- Adverse: Material and pervasive — the statements as a whole are unreliable.
- Disclaimer: Possible material and pervasive limitation on scope — auditor cannot form an opinion.

Important facts

  • Shares issued at premium transfer the premium to Securities Premium Account, which can only be used for specified purposes under Section 47 of Companies Act, 2013.
  • Forfeited shares profit is transferred to Capital Reserve Account, not to Profit and Loss Account.
  • Debentures redeemed at a premium: the premium is debited to Profit and Loss Account (or Securities Premium Account if balance exists).
  • DRR (Debenture Redemption Reserve) must be at least 25% of debenture value for applicable companies, created from distributable profits, and can only be used for writing off losses or paying up bonus shares.
  • Buy-back of securities under Section 68 cannot exceed 25% of paid-up equity capital in a financial year, and debt-equity ratio post-buy-back must not exceed 2:1.
  • Life membership fees and entrance fees are capital receipts shown on the liability side of Balance Sheet in NFPs, not in Income and Expenditure Account.
  • Floating charge crystallises upon winding up, cessation of business, or when the charge holder takes possession.
  • In NFP accounting, subscription due for the year = Subscription received + Outstanding - Prepaid.
  • Stock valuation: Lower of cost and net realizable value (NRV).
  • Audit evidence must be both sufficient (quantity) and appropriate (quality — relevance and reliability).
  • Vouching checks the authenticity of transactions — date, amount, authorization, nature, period, and classification.
  • Qualified report = material but not pervasive; Adverse report = material and pervasive; Disclaimer = scope limitation that is material and pervasive.
  • Internal audit is continuous and reports to management; external audit is periodic and reports to shareholders.
  • Securities Premium Account cannot be used for writing off brokerage/commission on share/debenture issue — that is specifically allowed under Section 52.
  • Under Section 77 of Companies Act, 2013, every charge created by a company must be registered with the ROC within 30 days.

Formulas and relationships

Allotment on pro-rata basis = (Shares Applied × Total Shares Available) / Total Applications Received
Surplus application money adjusted = Total money received - (Allotment shares × Amount per share due on allotment)
Refund of excess money = Surplus application money - Amount adjusted towards allotment/calls
Forfeiture loss on re-issue = Face value - Re-issue price (when re-issued at discount)
Subsidy from forfeiture profit = Forfeiture profit - Loss on re-issue (if re-issued at discount, otherwise forfeiture profit goes entirely to Capital Reserve)
Subscription due for current year = Subscription received during the year + Outstanding subscription at end - Outstanding subscription at beginning - Advance subscription at beginning + Advance subscription at end
Cost of Stock = Raw Material consumed + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses + Work Overheads
Net Realizable Value (NRV) = Estimated selling price - Estimated costs of completion - Estimated costs to sell
Capital Fund = Opening Capital Fund + Surplus (or - Deficit) + Life Membership Fees + Capital Specific Donations
Current Asset Valuation = Lower of Cost or Net Realizable Value
Materiality threshold (guidance): Typically 5% of profit before tax or 1% of total revenue or 0.5% of total assets, depending on the context

Common mistakes

  • Confusing Forfeited Shares Account with Profit and Loss Account — forfeiture profit is a capital profit and must go to Capital Reserve, not P&L.
  • Forgetting that DRR cannot be created out of undistributable profits — it must come from distributable profits only.
  • Treating life membership fees as revenue income in NFP accounts — they are capital receipts.
  • Using 'Profit and Loss' terminology for NFPs instead of 'Surplus/Deficit on Income and Expenditure Account'.
  • Forgetting that buy-back notice must be filed with ROC within 7 days of passing the special resolution.
  • Not distinguishing between fixed charge and floating charge — fixed charge holder has priority over floating charge holder in insolvency.
  • Applying DRR rules universally — remember DRR requirements differ for government companies, NBFCs, and foreign currency debentures.
  • Recording vouchers that are not authorized — vouching must confirm proper authorization, not just mathematical accuracy.
  • Confusing vouching with verification — vouching examines transactions (income/expenditure), verification examines Balance Sheet items (assets/liabilities).
  • Writing off securities premium for general purposes — it can only be used for the six specified purposes under Section 47.
  • Forgetting to create DRR one year before the redemption date when required — this is a statutory compliance failure.
  • Treating all donations as revenue receipts in NFP accounts — donations with a specific purpose are capital receipts.
  • Missing the adjustment of opening/closing outstanding and advance subscription in NFP questions — always reconcile to find the current year's due amount.
  • Assuming all buy-back transactions require board resolution — only those exceeding 25% thresholds need board resolution; otherwise general meeting resolution suffices.
  • Not noting that the auditor's report must be signed with the auditor's membership number and the firm's seal.
