Operations Management and Strategic Management — Complete Notes

1. Introduction to Operations Management

Operations Management (OM) concerns the design, operation, and improvement of the systems that create and deliver an organization's products or services. For a cost accountant, OM is critical because operations are where costs are incurred — raw material consumption, labor efficiency, overhead absorption, and waste all flow from operational decisions.

Core definitions:
• Operations Management: The set of activities that creates value by transforming inputs (materials, labor, capital, information) into outputs (goods and services) in a value-adding process.
• Transformation Process Model: Input → Transformation Process → Output, with feedback loops and environmental constraints (technology, regulations, market demand).
• Productivity: The ratio of output to input. Multi-factor productivity considers multiple inputs; total factor productivity (TFP) captures the output per combined unit of all inputs.

Scope of OM across three planning horizons:
• Strategic (long-term): Facility location, capacity planning, technology choice, product/service design, vertical integration — decisions taken once every 3–10 years.
• Tactical (medium-term): Aggregate production planning, inventory policy, workforce leveling, sourcing strategy — typically 3–18 months horizon.
• Operational (short-term): Scheduling, dispatching, quality inspection, maintenance scheduling, job assignment — daily to weekly horizon.

OM's relationship to Cost Accounting:
• Standard costing for materials, labor, and overheads is rooted in operations standards.
• Variance analysis (material usage, labor efficiency, overhead volume) directly reflects operational performance.
• Activity-Based Costing (ABC) requires understanding of operation-level cost drivers — routing, batch size, setup frequency.
• Just-in-Time (JIT) and Lean production affect inventory valuation and wastage norms.

Key performance metrics tracked by OM:
• Throughput (units/time), cycle time, lead time, capacity utilization, OEE (Overall Equipment Effectiveness), first-pass yield, On-Time-In-Full (OTIF) delivery.

2. Product and Service Design in Operations

Product/Service design determines up to 70–80% of a product's cost before production even begins. Therefore, design decisions have enormous cost-accounting implications.

Design process steps:
1. Concept generation (brainstorming, benchmarking, reverse engineering)
2. Preliminary feasibility (market, technical, economic)
3. Detailed design (specifications, materials, tolerances)
4. Prototyping and testing
5. Design for Manufacture/Assembly (DFM/DFA)
6. Final specification and handover to production

Design for Environment (DfE):
• Focuses on minimizing environmental impact across the product life cycle — material selection, energy use in production, recyclability, end-of-life disposal.
• Relevant for cost accountants through compliance costs, extended producer responsibility (EPR) liabilities, and green accounting.

Value Engineering (VE) / Value Analysis (VA):
• VE is applied during design stage; VA is applied to existing products.
• Function analysis: Identify basic function (what the product must do) vs. secondary functions.
• Formula: Value = Function / Cost. Aim is to maintain or improve function while reducing cost.
• Steps: Information phase → Function analysis → Creative phase → Evaluation phase → Development phase → Presentation phase.
• Cost-accounting relevance: VE directly targets cost reduction without sacrificing utility, impacting standard costs, BOM (Bill of Materials), and profit margins.

Target Costing:
• Target Cost = Market Price – Desired Profit Margin.
• Used when the market price is competitive and fixed; cost must be designed down to meet it.
• Cross-functional teams work backward from target to achieve it through design, sourcing, and process improvements.
• Widely used in automotive, electronics, and manufacturing sectors.

Design considerations for cost control:
• Standardization and modularity reduce variety, simplify inventory, lower procurement costs.
• Tolerance specifications: Over-specified tolerances increase machining cost without adding value.
• Make-or-buy decisions at design stage determine whether capacity is owned or outsourced.

3. Facility Layout and Location Decisions

Facility Layout refers to the physical arrangement of departments, workstations, machines, storage areas, and auxiliary services within a plant. Layout decisions affect material handling cost, work-in-progress (WIP) inventory, production flow, and employee productivity.

Types of layout:
• Product Layout (Line/Layout): Machines arranged in sequence of operations. Suitable for high-volume, low-variety production (e.g., automobile assembly). Advantages: low material handling, fast throughput, simple scheduling. Disadvantages: high capital investment, inflexible, line balancing problems.
• Process Layout (Functional/Layout): Similar machines grouped together. Suitable for job shops and batch production. Advantages: flexibility, accommodates varied products. Disadvantages: high WIP, longer travel distance, complex scheduling.
• Fixed-Position Layout: Product remains stationary; resources come to it. Used for shipbuilding, aircraft, construction. Advantages: no product movement. Disadvantages: scheduling complexity, space requirements.
• Cellular/Layout (Manufacturing Cells): Group Technology approach — parts with similar processing requirements form cells. Hybrid of product and process layouts.
• Hybrid layouts: Combination such as product-process layout used in flexible manufacturing systems.

Line Balancing:
• In product layout, tasks are assigned to workstations so that each has approximately equal processing time.
• Key terms: Cycle time = Takt time = Available production time / Required output.
• Theoretical minimum number of workstations = Sum of task times / Cycle time.
• Balance delay = [(Sum of task times) – (Number of stations × Cycle time)] / (Number of stations × Cycle time) × 100%.
• Balancing losses represent idle time at stations and reduce effective capacity.

Facility Location Decisions:
• Strategic decision affecting long-run cost structure. Wrong location can make a plant uncompetitive.
• Quantitative methods:
– Factor Rating Method: Assign weights to factors (labor cost, transport, power, government policy), score each alternative, compute weighted score.
– Load-Distance Method: Minimize total transportation cost = Σ(load_i × distance_ij × rate_i). Distance can be Euclidean or rectilinear.
– Center of Gravity Method: For distribution centers, find location minimizing weighted distance to demand points. Coordinates: X = Σ(w_i × x_i)/Σw_i, Y = Σ(w_i × y_i)/Σw_i.
– Break-even Analysis for location: Plot total cost (fixed + variable × quantity) for each site; choose lowest-cost site at expected volume.
• Qualitative factors: Political stability, labor availability/skill, proximity to raw materials/markets, infrastructure, tax incentives, environmental regulations, community attitude.

4. Production Planning and Control (PPC)

Production Planning and Control is the nerve center of operations — it bridges the gap between order receipt and delivery. PPC ensures right product, right quantity, right time, right quality, at minimum cost.

Components of PPC:
• Production Planning: Determining WHAT to produce, HOW MUCH, and HOW. Involves forecasting, capacity planning, product mix decisions, routing, scheduling.
• Production Control: Ensuring production follows the plan. Involves dispatching, progress monitoring, inspection, correction of deviations.

Routing:
• Determining the most efficient path a product takes through the factory — sequence of operations, machines required, tools/jigs needed.
• Output: Route sheet specifying operation sequence, machine, setup time, run time per unit.

Scheduling:
• Determining WHEN each operation starts and finishes. Types:
– Gantt chart: Visual bar chart showing start-finish dates for each job.
– Loading: Assigning jobs to machines/workcenters based on capacity.
– Sequencing: Deciding the order of jobs on a machine. Rules: FCFS (First Come First Served), SPT (Shortest Processing Time — minimizes average flow time), EDD (Earliest Due Date — minimizes maximum lateness), CR (Critical Ratio = (Due date – Today)/(Processing time remaining)).

Dispatching:
• Authorizing the start of production. Issuing route sheets, material requisitions, tool requisitions, job cards.
• Shop floor control and progress reporting.

Inventory Control in PPC:
• Raw materials, WIP, finished goods — all need control through stock levels, reorder points, safety stocks. Covered separately in Section 7.

PERT and CPM (covered in Section 9) are also part of project scheduling under PPC.

5. Inventory Management — Basic Models

Inventory represents a major blocked investment for most firms. Effective inventory management balances the cost of holding inventory against the cost of stockouts and ordering.

Types of inventory:
• Raw materials, work-in-progress (WIP), finished goods, MRO (Maintenance, Repair & Operations) supplies, transit inventory.

Costs involved:
• Holding/Carrying cost (H): Storage, insurance, obsolescence, opportunity cost of capital, handling. Usually expressed as % of unit cost per year.
• Ordering/Set-up cost (S): Procurement cost per order or setup cost per production run.
• Shortage/Stockout cost: Lost sales, goodwill loss, production disruption.
• Purchase cost: Unit price × quantity.

EOQ (Economic Order Quantity) — Basic Model:
• Assumptions: Demand is constant and known, lead time is constant, no shortages allowed, only one product, replenishment is instantaneous.
• Formula: EOQ = √(2DS/H), where D = annual demand, S = ordering cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year.
• Total annual cost at EOQ = Purchase cost + (D/Q)×S + (Q/2)×H. At EOQ, ordering cost = holding cost.
• Reorder Level (ROL) = Demand during lead time = d × L, where d = daily demand, L = lead time in days.
• Maximum level = ROL + EOQ – (minimum consumption × minimum lead time).
• Minimum level = ROL – (average consumption × average lead time).
• Average inventory = Q/2.

Modified EOQ models:
• EOQ with planned shortages: Allows backorders. Optimal order size increases, average inventory decreases.
• EOQ with quantity discounts: Compare total annual cost (including purchase cost) at each discount breakpoint and at EOQ; choose the quantity yielding lowest total cost.
• Production Order Quantity (POQ) model (EPQ): When production rate (p) > demand rate (d); inventory builds up gradually. EPQ = √(2DS/H × p/(p–d)). Maximum inventory = EPQ × (1 – d/p).

Safety Stock:
• Kept to guard against demand uncertainty or lead time variability.
• With known service level (probability of not stocking out), safety stock = Z × σ_dL, where Z is the z-score for the desired service level and σ_dL is the standard deviation of demand during lead time.

ABC Analysis:
• Based on Pareto principle — a few items account for most value.
• Categorize inventory by annual consumption value (usage × unit cost):
– A items: ~10–20% of items, ~70–80% of value — tight control, frequent review, accurate records.
– B items: ~30% of items, ~15% of value — moderate control.
– C items: ~50% of items, ~5% of value — loose control, simple records, larger order quantities.
• VED Analysis: Vital, Essential, Desirable — based on criticality for production, used for spare parts.
• FSN Analysis: Fast-moving, Slow-moving, Non-moving — based on consumption rate.
• HML Analysis: High-value, Medium-value, Low-value — based on unit price.

6. Quality Management

Quality management ensures that products/services meet specified standards and customer expectations. In CMA context, quality decisions directly affect cost structure through scrap, rework, warranty claims, and brand reputation.

Definitions:
• Quality: Degree to which a set of inherent characteristics fulfills requirements (ISO 9000). Fitness for use (Juran). Conformance to requirements (Crosby).
• Total Quality Management (TQM): An organization-wide philosophy of continuous improvement involving all employees, focused on customer satisfaction.

Evolution of quality concepts:
• Inspection era: Quality checked at end of production (detect defects).
• Statistical Quality Control (SQC) era: Quality controlled during production using statistical methods.
• TQM era: Quality built into design and processes; prevention over detection.

Statistical Process Control (SPC):
• Uses control charts to monitor process stability.
• Variables control charts: X̄-chart (mean), R-chart (range), X-chart (individuals), S-chart (standard deviation).
• Attributes control charts: p-chart (fraction defective), np-chart (number defective), c-chart (count of defects), u-chart (defects per unit).
• Control limits: UCL = CL + 3σ, LCL = CL – 3σ (3-sigma limits are standard).
• process capability: Cp = (USL – LSL)/(6σ), Cpk = min[(USL–μ)/(3σ), (μ–LSL)/(3σ)]. Cp ≥ 1 indicates capable process; Cpk accounts for centering.

Seven Tools of Quality (cause-and-effect focus):
1. Check Sheet (data collection)
2. Pareto Chart (vital few — 80/20 rule)
3. Cause-and-Effect Diagram (Ishikawa/Fishbone diagram)
4. Histogram (distribution of data)
5. Scatter Diagram (correlation between two variables)
6. Control Chart (process variation)
7. Flow Chart / run chart (process mapping)

Cost of Quality (COQ):
• Prevention costs: Training, quality planning, process control, supplier evaluation.
• Appraisal costs: Inspection, testing, calibration, quality audits.
• Internal failure costs: Scrap, rework, re-inspection, downtime from defects.
• External failure costs: Warranty claims, returns, complaints, loss of goodwill, liability claims.
• TQM aims to shift spending toward prevention (lower total COQ). Classic ratio: for every ₹1 spent on prevention, internal+external failures may save ₹4–₹10.

Six Sigma:
• A data-driven methodology for eliminating defects. Goal: ≤ 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO).
• DMAIC cycle: Define → Measure → Analyze → Improve → Control.
• Roles: Champions, Master Black Belts, Black Belts, Green Belts.

ISO 9000 Series:
• ISO 9001: Quality Management Systems — requirements for certification.
• ISO 14001: Environmental Management Systems.
• ISO 22000: Food safety (if applicable).
• Seven quality management principles: Customer focus, Leadership, Engagement of people, Process approach, Improvement, Evidence-based decision making, Relationship management.

7. Maintenance Management

Maintenance ensures equipment remains in working condition, minimizing breakdowns and maximizing productive capacity. For a cost accountant, maintenance cost is a significant overhead component requiring careful control.

Types of maintenance:
• Breakdown (Reactive) Maintenance: Repair after failure. Low upfront cost but high unplanned downtime and potential for cascading failures.
• Preventive (Planned) Maintenance: Scheduled maintenance at fixed intervals (time-based or usage-based). Reduces unexpected breakdowns but may involve unnecessary servicing.
• Predictive Maintenance: Condition-based — uses monitoring techniques (vibration analysis, thermography, oil analysis) to predict failures before they occur.
• Total Productive Maintenance (TPM): A holistic approach involving all employees — operators perform routine maintenance (autonomous maintenance), aiming for Zero Breakdowns, Zero Defects, Zero Accidents. Eight pillars of TPM: 5S, Kaizen, Autonomous Maintenance, Planned Maintenance, Quality Maintenance, Early Equipment Management, Education & Training, Administration.
• Reliability-Centered Maintenance (RCM): Systematic approach to determine what maintenance is required for each asset based on its function, failure modes, and consequences.

Maintenance Cost Control:
• Budgeting: Historical data + engineering estimates; separate preventive vs. corrective budgets.
• Key ratios: Maintenance cost as % of replacement asset value (RAV); Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF); Mean Time To Repair (MTTR); Availability = MTBF/(MTBF + MTTR).
• Optimization: Trade-off between preventive maintenance cost and breakdown cost. Find optimal replacement interval that minimizes total cost per unit time.
• Spare parts management: Critical spares vs. non-critical; ABC classification; obsolescence risk.

Replacement Analysis:
• Individual asset replacement: Compare marginal cost of keeping asset one more year vs. cost of new asset. Replace when marginal cost exceeds equivalent annual cost of new asset.
• Group replacement policy: For identical items with known failure patterns (e.g., bulbs). Compare cost of replacing individually as they fail vs. periodic group replacement. Choose policy with lowest total expected cost.

8. Operations Research Techniques in Operations

Operations Research (OR) provides quantitative tools for optimizing operational decisions. CMA exams frequently test these techniques.

Linear Programming (LP):
• Used for resource allocation problems — maximizing profit or minimizing cost subject to constraints.
• Standard form: Maximize/Z = c₁x₁ + c₂x₂ + ... + cₙxₙ subject to a₁₁x₁ + a₁₂x₂ + ... ≤ b₁, etc., and xⱼ ≥ 0.
• Solution methods:
– Graphical method: For 2-variable problems. Plot constraints, identify feasible region, evaluate objective function at corner points.
– Simplex method: Iterative algorithm for multi-variable problems. Uses slack, surplus, and artificial variables. Terminates when all coefficients in Z-row are non-negative (maximization).
• Shadow price (dual price): Change in optimal objective value per unit increase in RHS of a constraint. Indicates the value of relaxing a constraint.
• Sensitivity analysis: How changes in coefficients (cⱼ, bᵢ, aᵢⱼ) affect the optimal solution. Ranges of optimality and feasibility.
• Degeneracy: When a basic variable takes value zero. Can cause cycling; resolved by perturbation.
• Unbounded solution: Feasible region extends infinitely in direction of improvement — usually indicates missing constraint.
• Infeasible solution: No point satisfies all constraints simultaneously.

Transportation Problem:
• Special LP problem: Distribute goods from m sources to n destinations at minimum cost.
• Balanced if total supply = total demand; otherwise add dummy source/destination.
• Initial basic feasible solutions:
– North-West Corner Rule: Simplest, ignores costs.
– Least Cost Method: Allocates to cell with lowest cost first.
– Vogel's Approximation Method (VAM): Uses penalty (difference between two lowest costs); gives near-optimal starting solution.
• Optimality test: MODI (Modified Distribution) method or UV method. Calculate dual variables uᵢ and vⱼ, then check reduced costs cᵢⱼ – (uᵢ + vⱼ) for all non-basic cells. If all ≥ 0, solution is optimal.
• Dummy rows/columns have zero cost. Multiple optima exist if any non-basic cell has zero reduced cost.

Assignment Problem:
• Special case of transportation where each source has supply 1 and each destination has demand 1. One-to-one matching.
• Solved by Hungarian Algorithm:
1. Row reduction: Subtract smallest element in each row from all elements in that row.
2. Column reduction: Subtract smallest element in each column from all elements in that column.
3. Cover all zeros with minimum number of lines. If lines = n, optimal solution reached.
4. If lines < n, find smallest uncovered element, subtract from all uncovered elements, add to elements at intersection of lines. Repeat.
5. Make assignments at zeros.
• Maximization problems: Convert to minimization by subtracting all elements from largest element.
• Unbalanced problems: Add dummy rows/columns.
• Prohibited assignments: Assign very high cost (M).

Waiting Line (Queue) Theory:
• Models systems where customers arrive and wait for service.
• Key parameters: Arrival rate (λ), service rate (μ), number of servers (s), queue discipline (FCFS standard).
• Little's Law: L = λW, where L = average number in system, W = average time in system.
• M/M/1 model (single server, Poisson arrivals, exponential service): ρ = λ/μ < 1 for stability. L = λ/(μ–λ), Lq = λ²/(μ(μ–λ)), W = 1/(μ–λ), Wq = λ/(μ(μ–λ)).
• Cost optimization: Balance waiting cost vs. service cost (e.g., number of checkout counters).

Game Theory (briefly):
• Decision-making in competitive situations with two or more players.
• Saddle point: Where maximin = minimax. Pure strategy solution exists.
• Mixed strategies: When no saddle point — players randomize over strategies.
• Relevant for pricing decisions and competitive strategy.

9. Project Management — PERT and CPM

Project Management techniques help plan, schedule, monitor, and control complex projects. PERT is probabilistic; CPM is deterministic.

Key terms:
• Activity: A task requiring time and resources. Represented by arrow in PERT, node in AON (Activity-on-Node).
• Event: A point in time marking completion of one or more activities. No duration.
• Path: Sequence of activities from start to finish.
• Critical Path: Longest path through the network — determines minimum project duration. Zero float/total slack.
• Float/Slack: Amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project.
– Total float = LS – ES = LF – EF
– Free float = ES(successor) – EF(activity) – duration(activity)
– Independent float = max(0, ES(successor) – LF(activity) – duration(activity))
– Interfering float = Total float – Interference float

CPM (Critical Path Method):
• Deterministic — single time estimate per activity.
• Forward pass: Calculate Early Start (ES) and Early Finish (EF). ES of first activity = 0. EF = ES + duration.
• Backward pass: Calculate Late Finish (LF) and Late Start (LS). LF of last activity = EF of last activity. LF = min(LS of successors).
• Float = LS – ES. Activities with zero float form the critical path.
• Crashing: Reducing duration of critical activities by adding resources. Crash cost per unit time = (Crash cost – Normal cost)/(Normal time – Crash time). Crash activities with lowest crash cost/time on critical path. Consider parallel paths becoming critical.

PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique):
• Probabilistic — three time estimates per activity:
– Optimistic time (a): Best case
– Most likely time (m): Most probable
– Pessimistic time (b): Worst case
• Expected time (te) = (a + 4m + b)/6
• Variance (σ²) = [(b – a)/6]²
• Project variance = Sum of variances of activities on critical path
• Project SD = √(Project variance)
• Probability calculations: Z = (Expected completion time – Scheduled completion time)/Project SD. Use standard normal table.
• Probability of completing within time T: P(Z ≤ (T – te_project)/σ_project)

Network Drawing Conventions:
• Arrow diagrams (AOA): Activities are arrows, events are nodes. Dummy activities (dashed arrows) used to show logical dependencies without consuming time/resources.
• Precedence diagrams (AON): Activities are nodes, arrows show dependencies. More intuitive and widely used now.
• Rules: No two activities can have same start and end event. Minimize dummy activities. Single start and single finish event.

10. Materials Management

Materials Management encompasses all activities related to procurement, storage, and issue of materials — a critical area for cost control.

Functions:
• Purchasing/Buying: Procurement of materials at right quality, quantity, time, price, from right source.
• Stores Management: Receipt, inspection, storage, preservation, issuance, record-keeping.
• Material Handling: Movement of materials within the facility.
• Inventory Control: Maintaining optimal stock levels.
• Scraps and Surplus Management: Disposal of waste, obsolete items.

Procurement Process:
1. Identification of requirement (MRB — Material Requisition Board)
2. Selection of source (vendor evaluation: quality, price, delivery, financial health)
3. Price negotiation and contract finalization
4. Issue of Purchase Order (PO)
5. Follow-up/expediting for timely delivery
6. Receipt and inspection
7. Comparison of PO, GRN (Goods Receipt Note), and Invoice (three-way match)
8. Payment

Vendor Development and Evaluation:
• Supplier scorecard: Quality (% acceptance), Price competitiveness, Delivery performance (on-time %), Service/responsiveness, Financial stability.
• Single sourcing vs. multi-sourcing: Trade-off between relationship depth and supply risk.
• Strategic partnerships: Long-term collaborative relationships for mutual benefit.
• EDI/E-procurement: Electronic data interchange for automated purchasing.

Materials Cost Control:
• Standard costing for materials — setting standard prices and usage.
• Price variance = (Actual Price – Standard Price) × Actual Quantity
• Usage variance = (Actual Quantity – Standard Quantity) × Standard Price
• Purchase price variance can be recorded at purchase (if price control is purchasing dept's responsibility) or at issuance (if cost control is the concern).
• Substitution of materials: Using cheaper alternatives without compromising quality.
• Just-in-Time purchasing: Deliveries synchronized with production; reduces inventory holding but increases supply chain risk.
• Bulk purchasing discounts: Evaluate whether discount savings exceed additional holding costs.

11. Introduction to Strategic Management

Strategic Management is the art and science of formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its objectives. It is concerned with long-term direction and scope.

Key definitions:
• Strategy: The pattern of decisions that determines the direction and scope of an organization over the long term, achieving advantage in a changing environment through configuration of resources.
• Strategic Management: Comprehensive concept covering formulation, implementation, and evaluation of strategies.

Levels of Strategy:
• Corporate Level: Concerned with overall scope — which businesses to compete in. Decisions include diversification, vertical integration, acquisition, divestiture, portfolio management. Answers 'Where to compete?'
• Business Level (Competitive Strategy): How to compete in each business/industry. Focus on competitive advantage — cost leadership, differentiation, or focus. Answers 'How to compete?'
• Functional Level: Strategies of specific functional areas (marketing, HR, finance, operations, R&D) supporting business-level strategy. Answers 'How to support the business strategy?'

Strategic Management Process (Sequential but iterative):
1. Strategic Analysis (Environmental scanning, SWOT, identifying stakeholders)
2. Strategy Formulation (Setting objectives, generating alternatives, selecting strategy)
3. Strategy Implementation (Structuring organization, allocating resources, managing change)
4. Strategy Evaluation and Control (Measuring performance, correcting deviations)

Mission, Vision, Values:
• Mission: Purpose of the organization — why it exists. Should be concise, clear, and inspiring.
• Vision: Aspirational future state — where the organization wants to be. Long-term (5–10+ years).
• Values: Guiding principles and ethical standards.
• Objectives: SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) targets derived from mission/vision.

Stakeholders: Owners/shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, government, community, creditors. Different stakeholders may have conflicting interests.

12. Environmental Scanning and Strategic Analysis Tools

Strategic analysis involves understanding the external and internal environment to identify opportunities, threats, strengths, and weaknesses.

PESTLE Analysis (External Macro-environment):
• Political: Government policy, stability, trade restrictions, taxation policy, labor laws.
• Economic: GDP growth, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, disposable income, unemployment.
• Social: Demographics, cultural trends, education levels, lifestyle changes, population growth.
• Technological: Innovation, R&D activity, automation, tech adoption rate, obsolescence risk.
• Legal: Employment law, consumer protection, antitrust, environmental regulation, IP rights.
• Environmental: Climate change, carbon footprint requirements, sustainability mandates, resource scarcity.

Porter's Five Forces (Industry Structure Analysis):
1. Threat of New Entrants: Determined by barriers to entry — economies of scale, capital requirements, switching costs, brand loyalty, access to distribution, government policy.
2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers: High when few suppliers, unique inputs, high switching costs, supplier forward integration threat.
3. Bargaining Power of Buyers: High when few large buyers, standardized products, low switching costs, buyer backward integration threat.
4. Threat of Substitute Products: Substitutes from different industries fulfilling same need.
5. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors: Intense when many competitors, slow industry growth, high fixed costs, low differentiation, high exit barriers.
• Combined five forces determine industry attractiveness and average profitability.

SWOT Analysis:
• Strengths and Weaknesses = Internal factors (resources, capabilities, brand, financial position)
• Opportunities and Threats = External factors (market trends, competition, regulation, technology)
• Used to match internal capabilities with external possibilities. SO strategies (use strength to exploit opportunity), WO strategies (overcome weakness by exploiting opportunity), ST strategies (use strength to avoid threat), WT strategies (minimize weakness and avoid threat).

Porter's Value Chain:
• Primary activities: Inbound logistics → Operations → Outbound logistics → Marketing & Sales → Service.
• Support activities: Firm infrastructure, HR management, Technology development, Procurement.
• Each activity adds value. Competitive advantage comes from performing activities better or differently than competitors. Cost advantage vs. differentiation advantage depends on which activities are optimized.

VRIO Framework (Internal Resource Analysis):
• Valuable: Does the resource/capability exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat?
• Rare: Is it controlled by only a few competitors?
• Inimitable: Is it costly/difficult for others to imitate?
• Organized: Is the firm organized to capture the value of the resource?
• Resources that are V,R,I,O provide sustained competitive advantage. VR only gives temporary advantage. V only gives competitive parity.

13. Strategy Formulation — Business-Level Strategies

At the business level, strategy is about creating and sustaining competitive advantage in a particular market.

Porter's Generic Strategies:
1. Cost Leadership: Become the lowest-cost producer in the industry. Advantages: survive price wars, earn higher margins at average price, deter entry. Requirements: economies of scale, efficient规模, tight cost control, standardized products, focus on operational efficiency. Risks: technological change, imitators, over-focus on cost ignoring quality/change.
2. Differentiation: Offer unique product/service valued by customers, enabling premium pricing. Sources of differentiation: quality, features, brand image, technology, customer service, dealer network. Requirements: R&D, marketing, design capability, strong brand. Risks: imitation, changing customer preferences, differentiation becoming irrelevant.
3. Focus (Niche): Serve a narrow market segment. Can be cost focus (lowest cost in segment) or differentiation focus (unique offering in segment). Risks: segment becomes attractive to broad competitors, segment shrinks, differences between segment and broader market diminish.
• Successful strategy requires fit — all activities must reinforce the chosen strategy. Being 'stuck in the middle' (no clear cost or differentiation advantage) is the worst position.

strategic Group Mapping:
• Plot competitors on 2-2 axes (e.g., price vs. quality, geographic coverage vs. product breadth) to identify strategic groups — clusters of similar strategies. Highlights mobility barriers between groups and potential blue ocean opportunities.

Ansoff Matrix (Growth Strategies):
• Market Penetration: Existing products in existing markets. Lowest risk. Increase share via pricing, promotion, distribution.
• Market Development: Existing products in new markets. Geographic expansion, new segments.
• Product Development: New products in existing markets. Innovation, R&D.
• Diversification: New products in new markets. Highest risk. Related (leveraging existing capabilities) vs. unrelated (conglomerate).

Corporate-Level Strategies:
• Growth: Expansion (internal/organic vs. external/inorganic via M&A, joint ventures, licensing).
• Stability: Pause/profit strategy — maintaining current course, often after rapid growth.
• Retrenchment: Turnaround, divestiture, liquidation — when facing decline.
• Combination: Mix of growth, stability, retrenchment across different business units.
• Vertical Integration: Forward (toward customers — retail, distribution) vs. backward (toward suppliers — raw materials). Make-or-buy decision.
• Horizontal Integration: Acquiring competitors in same industry.
• Conglomerate Diversification: Entering unrelated businesses.

14. Strategy Implementation and Control

Strategy implementation is where strategy meets reality. Many strategies fail due to poor implementation, not poor formulation.

Organizational Structure and Strategy:
• Structure follows strategy (Chandler's principle). Different strategies require different structures.
• Functional Structure: Departmentalized by function — suits single-business, cost-leadership strategy.
• Divisional/M-Form Structure: Departmentalized by product, geography, or customer — suits diversified corporations. Enables accountability and flexibility.
• Matrix Structure: Dual reporting (functional + project/product) — suits complex, innovative environments. Can cause conflict and power struggles.
• Network/Virtual Structure: Core organization coordinates externally sourced activities — suits dynamic, lean organizations.
• Choosing structure affects authority, coordination, communication, and speed of decision-making.

Resource Allocation:
• Budgeting: Annual operating budgets, capital expenditure budgets, zero-based budgeting, rolling budgets.
• Capital budgeting decisions allocate limited funds among competing strategic initiatives.
• Balanced Scorecard approach ensures resources support all four perspectives (financial, customer, internal process, learning & growth).

Leadership and Change Management:
• Leadership styles: Transformational (inspiring change), Transactional (rewards/punishments), Laissez-faire (hands-off).
• Kotter's 8-Step Change Model: Create urgency → Form coalition → Develop vision → Communicate vision → Empower action → Generate short-term wins → Consolidate gains → Anchor in culture.
• Lewin's Change Model: Unfreeze → Change → Refreeze.
• Resistance to change: Fear of unknown, loss of status/income, habit, different assessments. Address through communication, participation, training, support.

Strategy Control and Evaluation:
• Strategic control systems:
– Premise control: Checking underlying assumptions remain valid.
– Implementation control: Assessing whether plans are being executed.
– Strategic surveillance: Monitoring unanticipated developments.
– Special alert control: Rapid response to crises/emergencies.
• Balanced Scorecard (Kaplan & Norton): Four perspectives — Financial (profitability, ROCE, EVA), Customer (satisfaction, retention, market share), Internal Process (cycle time, quality, innovation), Learning & Growth (employee skills, culture, IT systems). Strategy map links cause-effect relationships across perspectives.
• Strategic audits: Systematic, comprehensive, independent examination of all aspects of strategy.
• Benchmarking: Comparing performance against best-in-class (internal, competitive, functional, generic).

15. Corporate Governance and Strategic Oversight

Corporate governance establishes the framework of rules, practices, and processes by which companies are directed and controlled. It aligns the interests of management with those of shareholders and other stakeholders.

Key concepts:
• Agency Theory: Conflict of interest between principals (shareholders) and agents (managers). Governance mechanisms reduce agency costs.
• Stakeholder Theory: Company should consider interests of all stakeholders, not just shareholders.
• Good Governance = Transparency + Accountability + Fairness + Responsibility.

ICSI/ICMAI Code of Corporate Governance (as applicable to CMA):
• Board of Directors: Composition (ratio of independent directors), responsibilities, committees (Audit Committee, Nomination & Remuneration Committee, Stakeholders Relationship Committee, CSR Committee).
• Independent Directors: Definition, appointment, tenure, removal, eligibility.
• Board Meetings: Minimum frequency, quorum, notice, agenda, minutes.
• Disclosure and Transparency: Financial reporting, related party transactions, risk management, internal controls.
• Shareholder Rights: Voting, dividends, information, participation in AGM.

Roles of Cost Accountant in Strategic Governance:
• Providing cost intelligence for board-level decisions (pricing, make-or-buy, capacity expansion).
• Participating in risk management and internal control systems.
• Supporting audit committee with cost audit reports.
• Contributing to strategic cost management — cost structure analysis, competitive positioning.
• ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) reporting and sustainability strategy.

Ethics and Strategic Decision-Making:
• Strategic decisions must consider ethical implications — not just legality.
• Ethical frameworks: Utilitarian (greatest good), Rights-based (respect individual rights), Justice-based (fairness), Virtue-based (character).
• Whistleblowing policies, codes of conduct, ethical decision-making models.

16. Strategic Cost Management and Performance Measurement

Strategic Cost Management (SCM) integrates cost management with strategic management to create and sustain competitive advantage. It goes beyond traditional cost accounting's internal focus.

Three pillars of SCM (Johnson & Kaplan / Shank & Govindarajan):
1. Strategic Position Analysis:
– Value chain analysis: Understand cost behavior and differentiation across the entire value chain (suppliers → operations → distributors → customers → service).
– Cost driver analysis: Structural cost drivers (scale, scope, experience, technology, complexity) vs. execution cost drivers (capacity utilization, plant layout efficiency, labor urge, quality programs).
– Life-cycle costing: All costs from R&D/design through production, marketing, distribution, service, and disposal.
2. Cost Advantage Strategies:
– Target costing (covered in Section 2).
– Kaizen costing: Continuous cost reduction during the manufacturing phase. Focus on incremental, ongoing improvements vs. one-time design changes.
– Activity-Based Management (ABM): Using ABC information to improve operations. Process ABM (improve efficiency of activities) and Customer ABM (profitability analysis by customer/product).
3. Cost Difference Analysis:
– Identify sources of cost difference vs. competitors (structural positions, execution effectiveness).
– Benchmarking against best practices.

Key performance indicators (KPIs) for strategic management:
• Financial: ROI, ROCE, EVA (Economic Value Added = NOPAT – (Capital × WACC)), Residual Income, Revenue Growth, Operating Margin.
• Non-financial: Market share, customer satisfaction index, defect rate, on-time delivery %, employee productivity, innovation pipeline (new product revenue %).
• Balanced Scorecard links financial and non-financial KPIs in cause-effect chains.

Competitive Strategy and Cost:
• Cost leadership requires relentless focus on structural cost drivers — scale, capacity utilization, process efficiency, input costs.
• Differentiation may increase costs (higher quality materials, R&D, branding) but commands premium prices.
• Trade-off vs. integration: Traditionally seen as trade-off; modern view suggests technology can enable both (mass customization).

Risk Management in Strategy:
• Strategic risks: Competitive threats, technological disruption, regulatory changes, geopolitical risks.
• Operational risks: Supply chain disruptions, quality failures, cyber security.
• Financial risks: Currency, interest rate, credit, liquidity.
• Enterprise Risk Management (ERM): COSO ERM framework — integrate risk management into strategic planning.

Important facts

  • EOQ = √(2DS/H); at EOQ, annual ordering cost equals annual holding cost.
  • Target Costing: Target Cost = Market Price – Desired Profit Margin. Used when market price is fixed.
  • Value = Function / Cost. VE/VA aims to maintain/improve function while reducing cost.
  • Cycle time = Available production time / Required output. Line balancing minimizes balance delay.
  • PERT expected time te = (a + 4m + b)/6; variance σ² = [(b – a)/6]².
  • Critical path = longest path through network; determines minimum project duration; has zero total float.
  • Total float = LS – ES = LF – EF. Critical activities have zero float.
  • Porter's Five Forces: Threat of new entrants, Supplier power, Buyer power, Substitute threat, Rivalry.
  • Porter's Generic Strategies: Cost Leadership, Differentiation, Focus. Being stuck in the middle is worst.
  • Cp = (USL – LSL)/(6σ); Cpk = min[(USL–μ)/(3σ), (μ–LSL)/(3σ)]. Cp ≥ 1 means capable process.
  • COQ categories: Prevention, Appraisal, Internal Failure, External Failure. TQM shifts spend to prevention.
  • ABC Classification: A-items ~10-20% of items, ~70-80% of value — tight control. C-items — loose control.
  • SwOT: Internal (Strengths, Weaknesses), External (Opportunities, Threats). SO/WO/ST/WT strategies derived.
  • VRIO: Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Organized. Only V+R+I+O gives sustained competitive advantage.
  • Balanced Scorecard 4 perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Process, Learning & Growth.
  • Ansoff Matrix: Market Penetration, Market Development, Product Development, Diversification (risk increases left to right).
  • Material Price Variance = (AP – SP) × AQ; Material Usage Variance = (AQ – SQ) × SP.
  • Little's Law in queuing: L = λW (average number in system = arrival rate × average time in system).
  • TPM goal: Zero Breakdowns, Zero Defects, Zero Accidents. 8 pillars include 5S, Kaizen, Autonomous Maintenance.
  • EVA = NOPAT – (Capital × WACC). Measures economic profit after deducting cost of all capital.
  • Hungarian Algorithm for assignment: Row reduce → Column reduce → Cover zeros → Iterate if needed → Assign.
  • VAM (Vogel's Approximation Method) gives near-optimal initial solution for transportation problems.
  • Strategic Cost Drivers: Structural (scale, scope, experience, technology) vs. Execution (capacity utilization, layout, quality).
  • Target Costing applies during DESIGN stage; Kaizen Costing applies during PRODUCTION stage.

Formulas and relationships

EOQ = √(2DS/H), where D=annual demand, S=ordering cost per order, H=holding cost per unit per year
Total annual cost at EOQ = Purchase cost + (D/Q)×S + (Q/2)×H; at EOQ, ordering cost = holding cost
Reorder Level (ROL) = d × L; Minimum level = ROL – (avg consumption × avg lead time); Maximum level = ROL + EOQ – (min consumption × min lead time)
Balance Delay = [(Σ task times) – (N × CT)] / (N × CT) × 100%, where N=no. of workstations, CT=cycle time
Theoretical minimum workstations = Σ(task times) / Cycle time
EPQ (Production Order Quantity) = √(2DS/H × p/(p–d)), where p=production rate, d=demand rate
Maximum inventory in EPQ = EPQ × (1 – d/p)
Safety Stock = Z × σ_dL, where Z=z-score for service level, σ_dL=std dev of demand during lead time
Cp = (USL – LSL)/(6σ); Cpk = min[(USL–μ)/(3σ), (μ–LSL)/(3σ)]
PERT expected time te = (a + 4m + b)/6; variance σ² = [(b – a)/6]²
Project variance = sum of variances of activities on critical path; Project SD = √(project variance)
Z = (Scheduled time – Expected project time) / Project SD for probability calculation
Total float = LS – ES = LF – EF; Free float = ES(successor) – EF(activity) – duration(activity)
Crash cost per unit time = (Crash cost – Normal cost) / (Normal time – Crash time)
M/M/1 Queue: L = λ/(μ–λ); Lq = λ²/(μ(μ–λ)); W = 1/(μ–λ); Wq = λ/(μ(μ–λ)); ρ = λ/μ must be < 1
Little's Law: L = λW
Material Price Variance = (Actual Price – Standard Price) × Actual Quantity
Material Usage Variance = (Actual Quantity – Standard Quantity) × Standard Price
Value = Function / Cost (Value Engineering)
Target Cost = Market Price – Desired Profit Margin
EVA = NOPAT – (Capital × WACC)
Center of Gravity: X = Σ(w_i × x_i)/Σw_i, Y = Σ(w_i × y_i)/Σw_i
Break-even quantity = Fixed Cost / (Selling Price – Variable Cost per unit)

Common mistakes

  • Confusing EOQ with EPQ: EOQ assumes instantaneous replenishment; EPQ accounts for gradual production. Using EOQ formula when production is gradual overstates inventory.
  • In PERT, forgetting that only activities ON the critical path contribute to project variance. Including non-critical path activities inflates the variance estimate.
  • In assignment problems, forgetting to convert a maximization problem to minimization before applying the Hungarian algorithm. Must subtract all elements from the largest element first.
  • In transportation problems, confusing MODI method with the initial allocation methods (NWC, Least Cost, VAM). MODI is for OPTIMIZING an already obtained basic feasible solution.
  • Mixing up Total Float and Free Float: Free float is the portion of total float that can be delayed without affecting the early start of ANY successor. Total float is the maximum delay possible without delaying project completion.
  • In line balancing, computing balance delay incorrectly: must divide by (Number of stations × Cycle time), not just cycle time.
  • Forgetting that in sensitivity analysis of LP, shadow prices are valid only within the range of feasibility (allowable increase/decrease in RHS). Outside that range, the basis changes.
  • In ABC analysis, classifying items purely by unit price rather than annual consumption value (unit price × annual usage). A high-price, low-usage item may be C-class.
  • In control charts, using 2-sigma limits instead of the standard 3-sigma. 2-sigma increases false alarms (Type I error); 3-sigma is the industry standard unless specified otherwise.
  • Confusing Cost Leadership with simply cutting costs indiscriminately. Cost leadership is about structural cost advantages, not arbitrary cost reduction that harms differentiation or quality.
  • In SWOT, listing external factors as Strengths/Weaknesses (internal) or vice versa. Strengths/Weaknesses are INTERNAL; Opportunities/Threats are EXTERNAL.
  • In assignment problems, not handling prohibited assignments by assigning a very high cost (M) to that cell, causing the algorithm to avoid it.
  • Applying EOQ when demand is seasonal or irregular. EOQ assumes constant demand; use modified models or finite planning horizon approaches for variable demand.
  • Forgetting to check for degeneracy in transportation problems (number of basic allocations ≠ m + n – 1). Degeneracy requires epsilon allocation.
  • In strategy implementation, confusing organizational structure with strategy. Structure should follow strategy, not the other way around. Changing structure without changing strategy (or vice versa) leads to misalignment.
