1. Introduction to Business Economics

Business Economics is the application of economic theory and methodologies to business decision-making. It bridges the gap between abstract economic theory and practical business problems. As a discipline, it uses concepts from microeconomics (individual/firm-level behavior) and macroeconomics (aggregate economy-wide variables) to help managers make informed choices regarding pricing, output, resource allocation, investment, and risk.

Scope of Business Economics:
The scope encompasses demand analysis, production and cost analysis, market structure analysis, capital budgeting, pricing policies, profit management, and macroeconomic factors affecting business such as inflation, unemployment, fiscal policy, and trade cycles. Modern business economics also integrates quantitative methods, operations research, and data analytics for better forecasting and strategy.

Significance for a Cost Accountant:
A Cost Accountant must understand economic forces because costing decisions are never isolated from market conditions. Pricing decisions depend on elasticity, cost classifications interact with production theory, and strategic decisions like capacity expansion rely on national income trends and monetary policy. Economic literacy enables cost professionals to contribute beyond bookkeeping into strategic value creation.

2. Law of Demand and Its Determinants

Demand refers to the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices during a given period of time, ceteris paribus (all other factors remaining constant). The Law of Demand states that, other things being equal, there is an inverse relationship between the price of a commodity and the quantity demanded — as price rises, quantity demanded falls, and vice versa.

Demand Schedule: A tabular representation showing the relationship between price and quantity demanded. Example: Price ₹10 → Qd 100 units; Price ₹8 → Qd 120 units; Price ₹6 → Qd 150 units.

Demand Curve: The graphical representation of the demand schedule, sloping downward from left to right, reflecting the inverse price-quantity relationship. It is derived by plotting price on the Y-axis and quantity on the X-axis.

Determinants of Demand (non-price factors):
• Income of the consumer — normal goods see increased demand with rising income; inferior goods see decreased demand.
• Prices of related goods — substitutes (tea/coffee): if price of coffee rises, demand for tea rises. Complements (car/petrol): if price of petrol rises, demand for cars falls.
• Tastes and preferences — fashion, advertising, cultural shifts.
• Population size and structure — larger population increases market demand.
• Expectations of future prices — anticipatory buying raises current demand.
• Government policies — taxes, subsidies, price controls.
• Seasonal and climatic factors — relevant for agricultural and seasonal products.

3. Law of Supply

Supply refers to the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at various prices during a given period, ceteris paribus. The Law of Supply states that there is a direct (positive) relationship between price and quantity supplied — as price rises, quantity supplied increases, and vice versa. This positive relationship exists because higher prices provide greater incentive and profitability for producers to expand output.

Supply Schedule: A table showing different prices paired with corresponding quantities supplied. Example: Price ₹20 → Qs 50; Price ₹25 → Qs 80; Price ₹30 → Qs 120.

Supply Curve: Graphically plotted with price on Y-axis and quantity on X-axis, the supply curve slopes upward from left to right.

Determinants of Supply:
• Price of the commodity itself (movement along the curve).
• Prices of related goods — substitutes in production (e.g., wheat vs. rice on same land), complements in production (joint products).
• Prices of inputs/factors of production — higher input costs reduce supply.
• Technology — improvements lower costs and increase supply.
• Nature of climate — crucial for agriculture.
• Government policies — taxes reduce supply; subsidies increase supply.
• Objectives of the firm — profit maximization vs. market share.
• Expectations of future prices.

4. Elasticity of Demand

Elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in one of its determinants. It is a numerical expression of the degree of sensitivity.

Types of Elasticity:

A. Price Elasticity of Demand (Ed):
Formula: Ed = -(ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q)
The negative sign is used to make the value positive since demand curve slopes downward. Ed is expressed as a positive number.
• Perfectly elastic (Ed = ∞): Any small price rise causes quantity demanded to drop to zero.
• Perfectly inelastic (Ed = 0): Quantity demanded does not change at all with price.
• Unitary elastic (Ed = 1): Percentage change in quantity equals percentage change in price.
• Relatively elastic (Ed > 1): Quantity changes more than proportionately to price.
• Relatively inelastic (Ed < 1): Quantity changes less than proportionately to price.

B. Income Elasticity of Demand (Ey):
Ey = (ΔQ/ΔY) × (Y/Q)
Positive Ey indicates normal goods; negative Ey indicates inferior goods.

C. Cross Elasticity of Demand (Exy):
Exy = (ΔQx/ΔPy) × (Py/Qx)
Positive cross elasticity indicates substitutes; negative indicates complements.

Determinants of Price Elasticity of Demand:
• Nature of the commodity — necessities are inelastic, luxuries are elastic.
• Number of alternatives — more substitutes mean higher elasticity.
• Degree of necessity — essential goods have inelastic demand.
• Possibility of deferred consumption.
• Number of uses — goods with multiple uses tend to be more elastic.
• Price level — high-priced items tend to be more elastic.
• Time factor — demand becomes more elastic over longer periods.

5. Theory of Consumer Behavior

Consumer behavior theory explains how consumers allocate their limited income among various goods to maximize satisfaction (utility).

Cardinal Approach (Marshallian):
Utility is measured in numerical units called 'utils'. Key laws:
• Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility: As a consumer consumes more units of a commodity, the additional utility (marginal utility) derived from each successive unit keeps diminishing, eventually reaching zero and potentially becoming negative. Total utility continues to rise until MU reaches zero, after which TU falls.
• Consumer's Equilibrium: A consumer is in equilibrium when the marginal utility per rupee spent on each good is equal: MUx/Px = MUy/Py. At this point, total utility is maximized.
• Law of Equi-Marginal Utility (General Equilibrium): A consumer will distribute his income among various commodities so that the marginal utility per rupee is the same across all purchases.

Ordinal Approach (Hicks and Allen):
Utility cannot be measured cardinally but can be ranked in order of preference. This approach uses indifference curve analysis.

Indifference Curve (IC):
A curve showing all combinations of two goods that give the consumer equal satisfaction. Properties:
• IC slopes downward from left to right.
• IC is convex to the origin (due to diminishing marginal rate of substitution).
• Higher IC represents higher satisfaction.
• Two ICs never intersect.
• IC is向下斜向右，凸向原点。

Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS): The rate at which a consumer is willing to substitute one good for another while maintaining the same level of satisfaction. MRSxy = -ΔY/ΔX, and it diminishes along an IC.

Budget Line (Price Line): Shows all combinations of two goods that a consumer can purchase with a given income at given prices. Consumer equilibrium occurs where the budget line is tangent to the highest possible IC.

6. Theory of Production

Production is the process of creating utility — transforming inputs (factors of production: land, labor, capital, organization) into outputs (goods and services).

Short Run vs. Long Run:
• Short Run: At least one factor is fixed (usually capital); only variable factors (like labor) can be changed.
• Long Run: All factors are variable; the firm can change the scale of production.

Law of Variable Proportions (Short Run):
Also known as the Law of Diminishing Returns. As equal increments of a variable factor (labor) are added to fixed factors (capital), initially total product (TP) increases at an increasing rate, then at a diminishing rate, and finally TP starts falling. The law has three stages:
• Stage I: TP increases at increasing rate, then at decreasing rate. AP rises to its maximum.
• Stage II: TP continues to increase but at a diminishing rate. AP falls but MP is still positive. This is the rational stage of production.
• Stage III: TP starts falling. MP becomes negative. This stage is irrational.

Relationship among TP, AP, and MP:
• When MP > AP, AP rises.
• When MP = AP, AP is at its maximum.
• When MP < AP, AP falls.
• TP is maximum when MP = 0.

Law of Returns to Scale (Long Run):
When all factors are increased proportionately:
• Increasing Returns to Scale: Output increases more than proportionately.
• Constant Returns to Scale: Output increases proportionately.
• Decreasing Returns to Scale: Output increases less than proportionately.

Causes of Increasing Returns: Specialization, indivisibilities of capital, managerial efficiency, marketing economies.
Causes of Diminishing Returns: Optimum ratio disturbance, scarcity of variable factor.

7. Cost Concepts and Cost Curves

Cost is the value of resources sacrificed to produce output. For CMA purposes, cost classification is fundamental.

Classification of Costs:
• Fixed Costs (FC): Do not vary with output in the short run (rent, insurance, salaries).
• Variable Costs (VC): Vary directly with output (raw materials, direct labor, power).
• Total Cost (TC) = FC + VC
• Average Fixed Cost (AFC) = TC/Output — continuously diminishing curve, rectangular hyperbola.
• Average Variable Cost (AVC) = VC/Output — U-shaped.
• Average Cost (AC) = TC/Output = AFC + AVC — U-shaped.
• Marginal Cost (MC) = ΔTC/ΔQ or ΔVC/ΔQ — cuts AC and AVC at their minimum points.

Relationships:
• MC intersects AC and AVC at their respective minimum points.
• When MC < AC, AC falls.
• When MC > AC, AC rises.
• When MC = AC, AC is at minimum.
• The vertical distance between AC and AVC equals AFC at every output level.

Shape of Cost Curves:
• AFC is a rectangular hyperbola (continuously falls).
• AVC, AC, and MC are U-shaped due to the law of variable proportions (initially diminishing returns, then increasing returns).

Cost concepts crucial for CMA:
• Opportunity Cost: Value of the next best alternative foregone.
• Implicit Cost: Non-monetary opportunity cost (own resources used).
• Explicit Cost: Out-of-pocket monetary payments.
• Sunk Cost: Past cost that cannot be recovered — irrelevant for decision-making.
• Transfer Cost: Cost incurred when shifting resources between departments.

8. Market Structures

Market structure refers to the organizational and other characteristics of a market that influence the nature of competition and pricing.

A. Perfect Competition:
Features: Large number of buyers and sellers, homogeneous product, free entry and exit, perfect knowledge, price takers (no single firm can influence price).
• Firm's demand curve is perfectly horizontal (perfectly elastic) at the market price.
• AR = MR = Price (constant).
• In short run, firm may earn super-normal profits, normal profits, or suffer losses depending on whether P > AC, P = AC, or P < AC.
• In long run, firms earn only normal profits (P = min AC). Entry/exit of firms ensures this adjustment.
• Allocative efficiency: P = MC. Productive efficiency: P = min AC.

B. Monopoly:
Features: Single seller, unique product, barriers to entry (legal, technical, natural), price maker.
• Demand curve is downward sloping (the market demand curve).
• MR lies below the demand curve because to sell more, the monopolist must lower price on all units.
• Equilibrium: MC = MR, and MC cuts MR from below.
• Monopolist can earn super-normal profits even in long run due to entry barriers.
• Price is set above MC — leads to deadweight loss (allocative inefficiency).
• Price discrimination: charging different prices to different customers for the same product (first, second, third degree).

C. Monopolistic Competition:
Features: Many sellers, differentiated products (real or perceived), free entry/exit, some price-setting power.
• Downward-sloping demand curve but more elastic than monopoly.
• In short run, earns super-normal profits (like monopoly).
• In long run, entry of competitors erodes profits — earns only normal profits (tangent to AC at equilibrium output).
• Excess capacity exists — firm does not produce at minimum AC.
• Non-price competition is key: advertising, branding, packaging.

D. Oligopoly:
Features: Few large sellers dominating the market, interdependence among firms, barriers to entry, homogeneous or differentiated products.
• Price rigidity is a key feature — explained by the kinked demand curve model.
• Kinked demand curve: rivals follow price cuts but not price increases, leading to a discontinuity in MR and price rigidity even when costs change.
• Cartels and collusion possible but often illegal.
• Game theory applies — Nash equilibrium, Prisoner's Dilemma.

9. Introduction to Management

Management is the process of planning, organizing, directing, and controlling the efforts of organization members and using all organizational resources to achieve stated objectives efficiently and effectively.

Definition by Management Experts:
• Henry Fayol: 'To manage is to forecast, plan, organize, command, coordinate and control.'
• F.W. Taylor: 'Management is the art of knowing what you want to do and then seeing that it is done in the best and cheapest way.'
• Luther Gullick: 'Management is the art of getting things done through and with people in formally organized groups.'
• Harold Koontz: 'Management is the art of obtaining maximum results with minimum risks and efforts.'

Nature and Characteristics of Management:
• Management is a social process involving human effort.
• Management is goal-oriented — aims at achieving organizational objectives.
• Management is pervasive — applicable to all types of organizations (business, non-business, government).
• Management is a continuous process — not a one-time activity.
• Management is an intangible force — felt everywhere but not visible.
• Management is multidimensional — involves managing work, people, and operations.
• Management is dynamic — adapts to changing environmental conditions.
• Management is both science and art — science because of systematic body of knowledge; art because of personalized application.

Levels of Management:
• Top Level: Board of Directors, CEO, MD — responsible for policy formulation, overall direction, and external relations.
• Middle Level: Department heads, branch managers — responsible for implementing policies, coordinating activities, and acting as communicators between top and bottom levels.
• Supervisory/Front-line Level: Foremen, supervisors — responsible for day-to-day operations, worker guidance, and quality control.

10. Functions of Management

The five primary functions of management form the core framework for understanding managerial work.

1. Planning:
Planning is deciding in advance what to do, how to do it, when to do it, and who will do it. It bridges the gap between where we are and where we want to go.
• Features: Primacy (planning precedes all other functions), pervasive (at all levels), futuristic (goal-oriented toward future), continuous process, decision-making.
• Types: Strategic plans (long-term, overall direction), tactical/buffer plans (middle-level, medium-term), operational plans (short-term, detailed).
• Steps: Setting objectives → Developing premises → Identifying alternatives → Evaluating alternatives → Selecting the best alternative → Implementing → Follow-up action.
• Limitations: Rigidity, resistance to change, external uncertainty, cost and time involved.

2. Organizing:
Organizing is determining what tasks are to be done, who is to do them, how tasks are to be grouped, who reports to whom, and where decisions are to be made.
• Process: Identification of activities → Departmentation (grouping of activities — by function, product, geography, customer, process) → Assignment of duties → Delegation of authority → Coordination.
• Organizational Structure types: Line organization (simplest, direct authority flow), Functional organization (specialization-based), Line-and-staff organization (line managers plus specialist advisors), Matrix organization (dual reporting — project and functional).

3. Staffing:
Staffing is manning the organization structure and keeping it manned through proper selection, training, development, performance appraisal, and compensation of employees. 'Right person for the right job' is the guiding principle.

4. Directing:
Directing is initiating organizational actions to accomplish goals. It includes supervision, motivation, leadership, and communication.
• Supervision: Watching over workers to ensure quality and quantity targets are met.
• Motivation: Stimulating people to act in desired ways.
• Leadership: Influencing and guiding followers toward goal achievement.
• Communication: Exchange of information, ideas, and understanding between people.

5. Controlling:
Controlling is measuring performance against standards, comparing actual with planned performance, and taking corrective action when deviations occur. It ensures that organizational goals are achieved as per plans.
• Steps: Setting standards → Measuring actual performance → Comparing with standards → Analyzing deviations → Taking corrective action.
• Techniques: Budgetary control, Ratio analysis, Break-even analysis, Standard costing, Internal audit, Flexible budgets, Management information systems.

11. Management Thought and Scientific Management

The evolution of management thought provides theoretical foundations that shape modern practice. Four major schools are essential for CMA Foundation:

Scientific Management (F.W. Taylor):
Known as the 'Father of Scientific Management,' Taylor introduced systematic methods to improve industrial efficiency. Key contributions:
• Science, not rule of thumb — replace经验做法 with scientifically studied methods.
• Harmony, not discord — management-worker cooperation instead of conflict.
• Cooperation, not individualism — team effort over solo work.
• Development of each person to greatest efficiency — training and selection.
• Maximum output instead of restricted output.

Taylor's Techniques:
• Study and Time Motion: Breaking tasks into components and timing them.
• Differential Piece Rate System: Two-rate system where efficient workers get higher rate and inefficient workers get lower rate.
• Functional Foremanship: Separation of planning from execution; eight specialists (four planning, four executing).
• Standardization: Uniform conditions of production.
• Exception Principle: Managers deal only with exceptional matters, routine matters delegated.
• Mental Revolution: Fundamental change in attitude of management and workers toward each other.

Criticism: Mechanistic view of workers, ignoring human needs; not suitable for all situations; led to worker alienation.

Administrative Management (Henri Fayol):
Fayol focused on the entire organization rather than individual tasks. He identified 14 Principles of Management:
1. Division of Work — specialization increases efficiency.
2. Authority and Responsibility — rights to give orders with corresponding responsibility.
3. Discipline — obedience, respect, and proper conduct.
4. Unity of Command — one superior per employee.
5. Unity of Direction — one head, one plan for a group of activities.
6. Subordination of Individual Interest to General Interest.
7. Remuneration — fair and equitable pay.
8. Centralization — balance between centralization and decentralization.
9. Scalar Chain — line of authority from top to bottom.
10. Order — people and materials in the right place at the right time.
11. Equity — kindness and justice in treating employees.
12. Stability of Tenure — job security and time for adaptation.
13. Initiative — encouraging employees to develop and carry out plans.
14. Esprit de Corps — promotion of team spirit and harmony.

12. Motivation and Leadership Theories

Motivation is the internal and external drives that stimulate initiative and persistency in goal-directed behavior. Understanding motivation is critical for effective management.

Motivation Theories:

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (Abraham Maslow):
Needs are arranged in a five-tier pyramid:
1. Physiological needs — food, shelter, clothing, basic survival.
2. Safety needs — security, protection from physical and emotional harm.
3. Social (Belongingness) needs — friendship, affection, acceptance.
4. Esteem needs — self-respect, recognition, status.
5. Self-Actualization — realization of full potential, personal growth.
Lower-order needs must be sufficiently satisfied before higher-order needs become motivators. This is a sequential theory.

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory:
• Motivators (Satisfiers): Achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, advancement — these create job satisfaction.
• Hygiene Factors (Dissatisfiers): Company policy, supervision, salary, working conditions, interpersonal relationships — their absence causes dissatisfaction but presence alone does not motivate.
Key insight: Satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposites; they are separate continua.

McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y:
• Theory X assumes workers are inherently lazy, avoid responsibility, need coercion and control.
• Theory Y assumes workers are self-motivated, seek responsibility, are creative problem solvers.
Modern management favors Theory Y assumptions.

Vroom's Expectancy Theory:
Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence. Workers are motivated when they believe effort leads to performance, performance leads to rewards, and rewards are valued.

Leadership Theories:
• Trait Theory: Focuses on inherent qualities of leaders (intelligence, confidence, integrity).
• Behavioral Theories: Task-oriented vs. relationship-oriented leadership (Ohio State and University of Michigan studies).
• Situational/Contingency Theories: Effective leadership depends on the situation (Fiedler's Model).
• Transformational vs. Transactional Leadership: Transformational inspires change and vision; transactional focuses on rewards and punishments for compliance.

13. Control and Communication

Control is the monitoring of activities to ensure they are accomplished as planned and correcting any significant deviations. Without control, planning becomes ineffective.

Characteristics of Control:
• Control is goal-oriented.
• Control is pervasive at all levels.
• Control is continuous process.
• Control is future-oriented (feedforward).
• Control is a measuring device.
• Control implies corrective action.

Types of Control:
• Feedforward Control: Anticipates problems before they occur (preventive). Example: Pre-employment testing, preventive maintenance.
• Concurrent Control: Takes place during the activity. Example: Real-time quality checks, direct supervision.
• Feedback Control: Occurs after the activity is completed. Example: Performance appraisals, financial statements analysis. This is the most common type.

Control Techniques:
• Budgetary Control: Using budgets as standards to measure performance. Includes operating budgets, cash budgets, flexible budgets.
• Ratio Analysis: Financial ratios (liquidity, profitability, leverage, turnover) to assess performance.
• Break-Even Analysis: Determines the output level where total revenue equals total cost (no profit no loss). BEP = Fixed Cost / (Price - Variable Cost per unit).
• Standard Costing: Pre-determining standard costs and comparing with actual costs to compute variances.
• Internal Audit: Independent examination of financial and operational records.
• Personal Observation: Direct visual inspection of activities.
• Critical Point Control: Focusing control efforts on key critical points.
• Management Information System (MIS): Systematic collection and processing of data for managerial decision-making.

Communication:
Communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas, feelings, and understanding between two or more persons. It is the lifeblood of any organization.
• Process: Sender → Encoding → Message → Channel/Medium → Decoding → Receiver → Feedback → Noise.
• Types: Formal (follows official chain — downward, upward, horizontal) and Informal (grapevine — unofficial, spontaneous).
• Barriers to Communication: Language barriers, semantic barriers, psychological barriers (prejudice, emotion), organizational barriers (hierarchical distance), physical barriers (noise, distance), cultural barriers.
• Overcoming Barriers: Active listening, feedback, simple language, appropriate channel selection, empathy, reducing hierarchical distance, use of technology.

Important facts

  • Law of Demand: Inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, ceteris paribus.
  • Law of Supply: Direct relationship between price and quantity supplied, ceteris paribus.
  • Elasticity of Demand formula: Ed = -(ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q). Unitary elastic when Ed = 1.
  • MC curve cuts AC and AVC at their minimum points. When MC < AC, AC falls; when MC > AC, AC rises.
  • In perfect competition, AR = MR = Price (horizontal demand curve for the firm). In monopoly, MR < Price (downward-sloping demand curve).
  • Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility: Each additional unit gives less additional satisfaction than the previous one.
  • Stage II of production (MP declining but positive) is the rational stage of production where firms operate.
  • Consumer equilibrium in ordinal approach: Budget line is tangent to the highest attainable indifference curve.
  • Fayol's 14 Principles include Division of Work, Unity of Command, Scalar Chain, and Esprit de Corps.
  • Maslow's Hierarchy: Physiological → Safety → Social → Esteem → Self-Actualization.
  • Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory: Motivators create satisfaction; Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction.
  • McGregor's Theory X assumes workers are lazy; Theory Y assumes they are self-motivated.
  • Break-Even Point (BEP) = Fixed Cost / Contribution per unit, where Contribution = Price - Variable Cost.
  • Sunk Cost is irrelevant for decision-making as it cannot be recovered.
  • Oligopoly is characterized by interdependence among firms; Kinked Demand Curve explains price rigidity.
  • Monopolistic Competition combines features of monopoly (product differentiation) and perfect competition (many sellers, free entry).
  • Three stages of Control: Feedforward, Concurrent, and Feedback.
  • Scalar Chain refers to the line of authority from the topmost to the lowest level of the organization.

Formulas and relationships

Income Elasticity of Demand (Ey) = (ΔQ/ΔY) × (Y/Q)
Cross Elasticity of Demand (Exy) = (ΔQx/ΔPy) × (Py/Qx)

Contribution = Selling Price - Variable Cost

Common mistakes

  • Confusing movement along a demand/supply curve (caused by price change) with a shift of the entire curve (caused by non-price determinants). This is the most frequently tested distinction.
  • Forgetting that MC intersects AC and AVC at their minimum points, not maximum points.
  • Assuming that when MR = MC, the firm always earns profit. MR = MC is only the profit-maximization (or loss-minimization) condition; actual profit depends on whether P > AC at that output.
  • Mixing up Microeconomics (study of individual units — firm, consumer) with Macroeconomics (study of economy-wide aggregates — GDP, inflation, unemployment). Business Economics primarily applies microeconomic concepts.
  • Thinking that in Perfect Competition, firms are price makers. They are price takers. Only monopolies and oligopolies have some degree of price-making power.
  • In Monopoly, assuming that the firm sets output where MR = MC and charges the price given by MC. The monopolist charges the price on the demand curve corresponding to the MR = MC output level — which is above MC.
  • Believing that sunk costs should be considered in decision-making. Sunk costs are historical and irreversible; they must be ignored in forward-looking decisions.
  • Assuming that all five Maslow's needs must be fully satisfied before moving to the next level. In reality, needs are partially satisfied sequentially, not in strict lockstep.
  • Confusing Herzberg's Hygiene factors with motivators. Hygiene factors (salary, working conditions) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate; true motivators are achievement, recognition, and responsibility.
  • In oligopoly, thinking that all firms will follow price changes. The kinked demand curve model specifically states that rivals follow price cuts but NOT price increases, causing the kink and price rigidity.
  • Assuming that Average Cost curve and Marginal Cost curve are always symmetric U-shapes. AFC is always diminishing (rectangular hyperbola) and never U-shaped.
  • Confusing Internal Rate of Return (IRR) with Break-Even Point. IRR relates to capital budgeting; BEP relates to cost-volume-profit analysis. These are distinct concepts.
  • Mistaking 'decentralization' with 'delegation.' Decentralization is a policy of dispersing decision-making authority throughout the organization; delegation is the process by which a manager assigns authority to subordinates.
