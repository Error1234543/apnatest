Fundamentals of Financial and Cost Accounting — Complete CMA Foundation Notes

CMAEnglishNotes

1. Meaning, Objectives and Scope of Accounting

Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring, recording, classifying, summarising, analysing, interpreting and communicating financial information about an enterprise to interested users. It serves as the language of business and provides a structured framework for tracking economic activities.

Objectives of Accounting:
• To maintain systematic and permanent records of all financial transactions, eliminating reliance on memory and reducing errors and omissions.
• To ascertain the result of operations — whether the business has earned profit or suffered loss during a period, through preparation of the Trading and Profit & Loss Account.
• To ascertain the financial position of the enterprise at a given date through the Balance Sheet, showing assets, liabilities and owners' equity.
• To provide relevant financial information to various users — owners, management, creditors, investors, government, employees and others — for decision-making.
• To serve as evidence in legal disputes and tax assessments, maintaining documents required under various statutory provisions.
• To assist management in planning, controlling and policy formulation through ratio analysis, budgets and cost reports.

Scope of Accounting:
Traditional scope covered book-keeping and financial statement preparation. Modern scope extends to cost accounting, management accounting, taxation, financial management, auditing, income determination, asset valuation, inflation accounting and environmental/sustainability reporting. The CMA Foundation syllabus encompasses both financial accounting and cost accounting as integral parts of this broader scope.

2. Accounting Concepts, Conventions and Principles

These form the conceptual foundation that guides the preparation of financial statements and ensures consistency and reliability.

Fundamental Accounting Concepts:
• Business Entity Concept: The business and its owners are separate legal and accounting entities. Personal transactions of the owner are not recorded in the business books. Capital introduced by the owner is treated as a liability of the business towards the owner.
• Accounting Period Concept: The indefinite life of a business is divided into intervals (usually one year) for periodic evaluation of performance and financial position. Annual accounts are prepared for each accounting period.
• Going Concern Concept: It is assumed that the business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future and will not be forced to cease operations or liquidate. This justifies recording assets at historical cost and providing for depreciation over useful lives rather than at realisable values.
• Money Measurement Concept: Only transactions that can be expressed in monetary terms are recorded in the books. Qualitative aspects such as employee skill, management quality or market reputation are not recorded.
• Dual Aspect Concept: Every transaction has a dual effect — a give-and-take aspect. This gives rise to the fundamental accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity. The double-entry system is based on this concept.
• Accrual Concept: Revenue and expenses are recognised when they are earned or incurred, regardless of when cash is actually received or paid. This contrasts with cash basis accounting where only cash transactions are recorded.

Fundamental Accounting Conventions (GAAP — Generally Accepted Accounting Principles):
• Convention of Disclosure: Full and fair disclosure of all material information should be made in financial statements, even if it requires footnotes or schedules, to enable users to make informed decisions.
• Convention of Consistency: Accounting policies and methods should remain consistent from one period to another so that comparisons across periods are meaningful. Changes, if any, must be disclosed.
• Convention of Conservatism (Prudence): Anticipate no profit but provide for all possible losses. When two methods give different values, the more conservative one is adopted. This guards against overstatement of assets and profits.
• Convention of Materiality: Only material (significant) items need strict adherence to accounting principles. Immaterial items may be treated pragmatically — for example, low-value assets may be expensed rather than capitalised and depreciated.

Key Accounting Principles (summarised):
• Revenue Recognition Principle: Revenue is recognised when transferred or service is rendered, not necessarily when cash is received.
• Matching Principle: Expenses of a period are matched with the revenues of the same period to determine true profit.
• Expense Recognition Principle: Expenses are recognised when the economic benefit is consumed or obligation is incurred.
• Historical Cost Principle: Assets are initially recorded at their purchase cost, though revaluation may occur under specific standards.
• Realisation Principle: Profit is considered realised when ownership of goods/services passes to the buyer.

3. Double-Entry System and Accounting Equation

The Double-Entry System is the most widely accepted system of book-keeping. Under this system, every transaction affects at least two accounts — a debit and a corresponding credit of equal amount. This ensures arithmetic accuracy and reflects the dual-aspect nature of every transaction.

The Accounting Equation:
Assets = Liabilities + Capital
This equation must always remain in balance. Any transaction will change the equation but never disturb its equality.

Illustrative Examples:
• Owner introduces cash capital ₹5,00,000: Assets (Cash) increases by ₹5,00,000; Capital increases by ₹5,00,000.
• Purchased goods for cash ₹50,000: One asset (Stock) increases, another asset (Cash) decreases by the same amount — equation remains balanced.
• Purchased goods on credit from Rahul ₹20,000: Assets (Stock) increases by ₹20,000; Liabilities (Creditors) increase by ₹20,000.
• Paid rent ₹5,000: Assets (Cash) decrease by ₹5,000; Capital decreases by ₹5,000 (expense reduces profit which reduces capital).

Rules of Double Entry (Traditional Approach):
• Debit the receiver, Credit the giver — applies to personal accounts.
• Debit what comes in, Credit what goes out — applies to real accounts.
• Debit all expenses and losses, Credit all incomes and gains — applies to nominal accounts.

Modern (Golden) Rules — based on account type and nature:
| Account Type | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | Debit | Credit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit |
| Income/Revenue | Credit | Debit |
| Expenses/Losses | Debit | Credit |

These modern rules are easier to apply universally without memorising three separate traditional rules. All journal entries in the CMA Foundation examination should follow these rules consistently.

4. Books of Original Entry — Journal and Journal Proper

The Journal is the book of first record where transactions are recorded chronologically with complete particulars before being posted to the Ledger. Each entry includes the date, names of accounts debited and credited, amounts, and a brief narration.

Format of a Journal Entry:
Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit (₹) | Credit (₹)
The account to be debited is written first, indented slightly. The account to be credited is written next, starting from the margin. A brief narration explaining the transaction is written below, preceded by 'To'.

Important Journal Entries for CMA Foundation:
• Opening entries: Dr All appropriate assets/a/c, Cr All appropriate liabilities/a/c, Cr Capital a/c (for assets/liabilities not shown in the trial balance).
• Purchase/Sale of assets on credit: Dr Purchases/Assets a/c, Cr Supplier a/c.
• Cash discount allowed/received: If trade discount is given, it is not recorded in books; only net amount is entered. Cash discount is recorded separately — allowed as an expense, received as income.
• Bills of exchange acceptance: Dr Bills Receivable a/c, Cr Acceptance a/c (when accepting a bill); Dr Drawee a/c, Cr Bills Payable a/c (when issuing a bill).
• Bad debts written off: Dr Bad Debts a/c, Cr Debtor a/c.
• Provision for bad debts: Dr Provision for Doubtful Debts a/c (P&L), Cr Provision for Doubtful Debts (Balance Sheet liability side).
• Depreciation: Dr Depreciation a/c, Cr Asset a/c or Accumulated Depreciation a/c.
• Capitalising commission/expenses: Dr Goodwill/Asset a/c, Cr Preliminary Expenses/Commission a/c.
• Final adjustment entries before trial balance extraction.

Journal Proper is used for: opening and closing entries, adjustment entries, rectification entries, transfer entries (e.g., closing nominal accounts to P&L), and any transaction that does not fit in special-purpose journals.

Important distinction: Trade discount is never recorded in the books of accounts — only the net price after trade discount is recorded. Cash discount, however, is recorded when it is actually allowed or received.

5. Ledger — Preparation, Balancing and Transposition

The Ledger is the principal book where journal entries are posted class-wise. Each account is maintained on a separate page or leaf. It provides a complete summary of all transactions affecting a particular account.

Format of a Ledger Account (T-Account style):
Left side = Debit side, Right side = Credit side.
Each side shows Date, Particulars, Folio (J.R./page reference), and Amount.
Balancing: After entering all transactions for a period, totals of both sides are computed. The difference is the balance, which is carried down (c/d) on the shorter side and brought down (b/d) on the opposite side in the next period.

Types of Balances:
• Debit Balance: When total debits exceed total credits — typical of assets and expenses.
• Credit Balance: When total credits exceed total debits — typical of liabilities, capital and incomes.

Posting Rules:
• From Journal: Debit side of journal → Debit side of respective ledger account. Credit side of journal → Credit side of respective ledger account.
• From Subsidiary Books: Special postings (e.g., discount column of Cash Book posted to Discount Allowed and Discount Received ledgers separately).
• Internal ledger transfers: When one account is credited and another debited within the ledger itself, reference the source account in the ledger folio column.

Common Errors in Ledger:
• Transposition error: Digits reversed while posting (e.g., posting ₹54 as ₹45). The difference is divisible by 9.
• Compensating error: Two or more errors cancel each other out, so the trial balance still agrees. These are difficult to detect.
• Omission: A transaction completely omitted from the ledger — trial balance will agree but accounts are wrong.
• Commission of error: Wrong amount posted to the right account on the right side — trial balance agrees but the account is incorrect.
• Error of original entry: Wrong amount recorded in the journal and similarly posted to the ledger — trial balance agrees but both are wrong.
• Complete reversal of entry: Debit and credit sides interchanged — affects trial balance balance by twice the error amount.

6. Trial Balance — Purpose, Preparation and Common Errors

A Trial Balance is a statement prepared with debit and credit balances extracted from the Ledger to test the arithmetical accuracy of book-keeping. It is not an account but a summary statement.

Purpose of Trial Balance:
• To verify the arithmetic accuracy of double-entry book-keeping — if total debits equal total credits, postings are likely correct (though not guaranteed, as some errors do not affect the trial balance).
• To serve as the basis for preparing final accounts — profits/losses and financial position are derived from trial balance figures.
• To facilitate easy reference and summarisation of ledger balances.

Positions of Account Balances in Trial Balance:
• Assets (Cash, Bank, Debtors, Stock, Furniture, Machinery, Building, Goodwill) → Debit side.
• Liabilities (Creditors, Loans, Outstanding expenses, Unearned revenue) → Credit side.
• Capital → Credit side (increases credit, decreases debit).
• Income/Revenue → Credit side.
• Expenses/Losses → Debit side.
• Drawings → Debit side.
• Bills Receivable → Debit side; Bills Payable → Credit side.
• Provision for Doubtful Debts → Credit side.
• Discount Allowed → Debit side; Discount Received → Credit side.
• Sales Return (Returns Outwards) → Debit side; Purchases Return (Returns Inwards) → Credit side.

Errors Not Detected by Trial Balance:
1. Errors of omission — transaction not recorded at all.
2. Errors of commission — wrong account but correct side and amount.
3. Errors of principle — violating accounting principles (e.g., capital expenditure debited to revenue account).
4. Compensating errors — multiple errors cancelling each other.
5. Errors of original entry — wrong amount in both journal and ledger.
6. Complete reversal of entries — debit and credit interchanged.

Note: These errors do not prevent the trial balance from agreeing but will lead to incorrect financial statements. They require investigation and rectification entries.

7. Final Accounts — Trading, Profit & Loss and Balance Sheet

Final Accounts consist of the Trading Account, Profit and Loss Account, and Balance Sheet. They are prepared at the end of each accounting period to ascertain profit or loss and the financial position.

Trading Account:
Prepared to find Gross Profit or Gross Loss. It deals only with direct costs and direct revenues related to the core trading activity.
Debit side: Opening Stock, Purchases (less Returns), Direct Expenses (Carriage Inwards, Octroi, Wages, Factory Expenses, Fuel, Power, Manufacturing Expenses, Insurance on goods).
Credit side: Sales (less Returns), Closing Stock.
Gross Profit is transferred to the Profit and Loss Account.

Profit and Loss Account:
Prepared to find Net Profit or Net Loss. It deals with indirect revenues and expenses.
Debit side: All indirect expenses — Office Expenses, Salaries, Rent, Rates, Taxes, Insurance, Depreciation, Bad Debts, Provision for Bad Debts, Discount Allowed, Bank Charges, Advertisement, Sinking Fund Charge, Royalty, Legal Charges, Audit Fees, Commission.
Credit side: All indirect incomes — Interest received, Commission received, Discount received, Profit on sale of assets, Rent received, Salaries recovered, Miscellaneous income.
Net Profit is transferred to Capital; Net Loss is deducted from Capital.

Balance Sheet:
Shows the financial position on a given date. It has two sides: Assets side and Liabilities side.
Liabilities side: Capital (plus Net Profit/less Net Loss), Long-term borrowings, Current liabilities (Creditors, Outstanding expenses, Unearned revenue), Bills Payable, Provisions (Provision for Taxation, Provision for Doubtful Debts, Sinking Fund).
Assets side: Non-current assets (Fixed Assets at cost less accumulated depreciation, Long-term investments, Goodwill, Preliminary expenses), Current assets (Closing stock, Debtors less Provision for Doubtful Debts, Bills Receivable, Prepaid expenses, Accrued income, Cash and bank balances, Short-term investments).

Important Adjustments Frequently Required:
• Outstanding expenses: Add to relevant expense in P&L; show as current liability in Balance Sheet.
• Prepaid expenses: Deduct from relevant expense in P&L; show as current asset in Balance Sheet.
• Accrued income: Add to relevant income in P&L; show as current asset in Balance Sheet.
• Unearned income: Deduct from relevant income in P&L; show as current liability in Balance Sheet.
• Depreciation: Reduce fixed assets in Balance Sheet; charge to P&L.
• Bad debts written off: Deduct from Debtors in Balance Sheet; charge to P&L.
• Provision for bad debts: Further reduce Debtors in Balance Sheet.
• Goods taken for personal use: Deduct from Purchases in Trading Account; show on Liabilities side of Balance Sheet (reduces capital effectively).

8. Rectification of Errors and Bank Reconciliation Statement

Rectification of Errors involves correcting mistakes found in the books after the trial balance has been prepared or after errors have been detected during final account preparation.

General Rules for Rectification:
1. If the error affects only one account, debit or credit that account directly.
2. If the error involves wrong narration but the correct accounts, the mistake in narration can be corrected by a simple memorandum entry; no formal journal entry is needed.
3. If the error involves wrong amount but correct accounts, pass a supplementary entry for the difference.
4. If a transaction has been completely recorded in the wrong account but on the correct side and amount, reverse the original entry and pass the correct one.
5. If an omission error is discovered, pass the complete entry that was originally omitted.
6. If a compensating error is identified, rectify each error individually.

Common Rectification Scenarios:
• Goods purchased from Rahul ₹5,000 was entered in Rahul's account as ₹500: Pass Dr Rahul a/c ₹4,500, Cr Purchases a/c ₹4,500.
• Sale of goods to Mohan ₹3,000 was debited to Sohan: Pass Dr Sohan a/c ₹3,000, Cr Mohan a/c ₹3,000 (reversing the wrong posting).
• Purchases book overstated by ₹1,000: Pass Dr Purchases a/c ₹1,000, Cr Suspense a/c ₹1,000 (if suspense account exists).
• Sale of old furniture ₹2,000 was recorded as sale of goods: Pass Dr Furniture a/c ₹2,000, Cr Sales a/c ₹2,000, then Dr Sales a/c ₹2,000, Cr Furniture a/c ₹2,000 (or combine in one entry).
• Depreciation on machinery ₹10,000 was forgotten: Pass Dr Depreciation a/c ₹10,000, Cr Machinery a/c ₹10,000.

Suspense Account:
When the trial balance does not agree, the difference is temporarily placed in a Suspense Account until the errors are found and rectified. It is closed by appropriate rectification entries. If errors remain untraceable, the balance is disposed of as per the materiality and convention of conservatism.

Bank Reconciliation Statement (BRS):
BRS reconciles the difference between the bank balance as per the Cash Book and the bank balance as per the Bank Pass Book. Differences arise due to:
• Time lag: Cheques issued but not yet presented for payment; cheques deposited but not yet collected by the bank.
• Items recorded in one book but not the other: Bank charges, interest on overdraft, direct deposits by customers, standing instructions not recorded in Cash Book.
• Errors: Bank errors or customer errors not reflected in the other book.
Starting point: Begin with either the Cash Book balance or the Pass Book balance and adjust for the above differences to arrive at the reconciled figure.

9. Introduction to Cost Accounting — Concepts, Classification and Cost Sheets

Cost Accounting is a branch of accounting that deals with the accumulation, classification, allocation, and analysis of costs for determining product/service costs, controlling costs, and aiding managerial decision-making. It provides detailed cost information to management for effective planning and control.

Objectives of Cost Accounting:
• Ascertaining cost — determining the cost of each product, job, process or service.
• Cost control — comparing actual costs with standard/budgeted costs and taking corrective action.
• Cost reduction — systematically reducing the unit cost of products/services without compromising quality.
• Providing data for pricing decisions.
• Facilitating budgeting and variance analysis.
• Assisting in making buy-or-make, accept-or-reject, and continue-or-drop decisions.

Classification of Costs — extremely important for CMA Foundation:
By Element:
• Material Cost — direct material and indirect material (factory consumables).
• Labour Cost — direct labour (workers directly engaged in production) and indirect labour (supervisors, maintenance staff).
• Expenses — direct expenses (royalty, hire of machinery) and indirect expenses (rent, insurance, rates).
• Prime Cost = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses.
• Works/Factory Cost = Prime Cost + Factory Overheads.
• Cost of Production = Works Cost + Office and Administrative Overheads.
• Cost of Goods Sold = Cost of Production + Opening Finished Goods – Closing Finished Goods.
• Total Cost = Cost of Goods Sold + Selling and Distribution Overheads.

By Behavior:
• Fixed Costs — remain constant in total irrespective of changes in output level (e.g., rent, insurance).
• Variable Costs — vary proportionately with output (e.g., raw materials, direct wages).
• Semi-variable/Mixed Costs — contain both fixed and variable components (e.g., electricity bill with fixed connection charge + variable usage charge).

By Function:
• Production/Manufacturing Cost
• Administration Cost
• Selling Cost
• Distribution Cost
• Research and Development Cost

By Normality:
• Normal Cost — incurred under normal operating conditions.
• Abnormal Cost — arises due to unusual circumstances (e.g., abnormal idle time, spoilage beyond normal levels).

By Predictability/Controllability:
• Controllable vs. Uncontrollable Costs.
• Relevant vs. Irrelevant Costs (for decision-making).

Cost Sheet Format:
• Shows item-wise breakup from prime cost to total cost per unit.
• Useful for pricing, cost comparison, and trend analysis.
• Typically prepared for a specific period or batch.

10. Material Costing — Receiving, Valuation and Control

Material Costing deals with the cost of raw materials from procurement to consumption in production. It aims to ensure adequate supply at minimum cost while avoiding excessive inventory.

Procurement Cycle:
• Identification of requirements → Preparation of purchase indent → Selection of supplier → Negotiation of price → Placing purchase order → Receiving goods → Inspection → Entry in goods receipt note → Payment.

Relevant Costs in Procurement:
• Purchase price (net of trade discount).
• Carriage inward, freight, insurance, octroi, import duties.
• Less: Trade discount, cash discount, rebates, returns.
• Storage costs, handling costs.

Methods of Valuation of Material Issues (Inventory Pricing):
• First-In-First-Out (FIFO): Materials purchased first are issued first. In a period of rising prices, closing stock is valued at higher prices and issues at older, lower prices.
• Last-In-First-Out (LIFO): Materials purchased last are issued first. In rising prices, closing stock is valued at lower, older prices.
• Weighted Average Price (WAP): Average cost is calculated after each receipt and applied to issues. This smoothens price fluctuations.
• Specific Identification Method: Used for unique, identifiable items where the actual cost of specific units is tracked.

Standard Methods of Material Control:
• Economic Order Quantity (EOQ): The optimal order quantity that minimises the total of ordering costs and holding costs. Formula: EOQ = √(2 × D × S / H), where D = annual demand, S = ordering cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per annum.
• Reorder Level: The inventory level at which a new order must be placed. Reorder Level = Maximum Usage × Maximum Lead Time.
• Min Level: Reorder Level – (Normal Usage × Normal Lead Time).
• Max Level: Reorder Level + EOQ – (Min Usage × Min Lead Time).
• Avg Level: (Max Level + Min Level) / 2, or Min Level + (EOQ / 2).
• Danger Level: Generally higher than min level; above this, urgent action is required.

ABC Analysis (Always Better Control):
Classifies inventory based on value/consumption:
• A items: High value, low quantity — tight control, frequent review.
• B items: Moderate value and quantity — moderate control.
• C items: Low value, high quantity — minimal control.

Key principle: Focus control efforts on the most valuable items (A class) for maximum benefit.

11. Labour Cost — Time Rate, Piece Rate and Incentive Schemes

Labour Costing deals with ascertainment and control of costs related to human effort in production.

Methods of Wage Payment:
• Time Rate System: Wages are paid based on time worked, regardless of output. Simple but may not encourage productivity.
• Piece Rate System: Wages are paid based on the number of units produced. Encourages efficiency but may compromise quality.
• Incentive Systems — designed to motivate workers and increase productivity:
– Halsey Plan: Worker gets time rate for hours worked + 50% of time saved × time rate. Bonus = 50% × (Standard time – Actual time) × Rate per hour.
– Halsey-Weir Plan: Similar to Halsey but bonus percentage varies (typically starts at 20% and increases to 50% as efficiency improves).
– Taylor's Differential Piece Rate: Two rates — higher rate for meeting/exceeding standard, lower rate for falling short. Strong incentive to achieve standards.
– Rowan Plan: Bonus = (Time saved / Standard time) × Time taken × Rate per hour. Bonus is capped at 100% of time wage.
– Merrick's Differential Piece Rate: Three-tier rate system based on percentage of standard achieved (e.g., 110%, 100%, 90%).

Idle Time and Overtime:
• Idle Time: Time paid for but not utilised in production (power failure, machine breakdown, weather). Normal idle time is absorbed into overhead; abnormal idle time is charged to Costing Profit & Loss Account.
• Overtime: Work beyond normal hours. Overtime wages are treated as follows:
– If overtime is at the request of the employer: absorbed into overhead or allocated to the specific job.
– If overtime is due to worker's inefficiency: charged entirely to that worker/job.
– If overtime is abnormal (e.g., rush order): charged to Costing P&L.

Labor Turnover: Measures the rate of replacement of workers. Methods include separations method, replacements method, and flux method. High turnover increases recruitment, training and loss of productivity costs.

Computation of Labor Cost per Unit:
Total Labor Cost / Total Units Produced = Labor Cost per Unit. Include direct wages, overtime premium (if applicable), statutory contributions (PF, ESI), and benefits.

12. Overheads — Classification, Apportionment and Absorption

Overheads are indirect costs that cannot be directly traced to a specific cost unit. They include indirect material, indirect labor, and indirect expenses.

Classification of Overheads:
• By Function: Factory/Works overheads, Office/Administrative overheads, Selling overheads, Distribution overheads, Research & Development overheads.
• By Nature: Material overheads, Labor overheads, Expense overheads.
• By Behavior: Fixed overheads, Variable overheads, Semi-variable overheads.

Absorption of Overheads — Critical Topic:
Overheads are charged to cost units using an Overhead Absorption Rate (OAR):
OAR = Total Estimated Overheads / Total Estimated Basis of Apportionment

Common Bases of Apportionment:
• Rent, Rates, Insurance of factory → Area occupied (sq. ft./sq. m.)
• Depreciation of machinery → Value of machinery or machine hours
• Power → Horsepower of machines or machine hours
• Lighting → Number of lamps or area
• Supervisory staff → Number of workers or wage ratio
• Canteen expenses → Number of employees or wage ratio
• Transport → Weight of materials or distance
• Heating → Area or number of radiators

Single Rate vs. Multiple Rate:
• Single Rate: One OAR for all overheads — suitable when overheads are small and uniform.
• Multiple Rate: Different OARs for different groups of overheads — more accurate, used when overheads vary significantly in nature.

Absorption costing includes all manufacturing costs (fixed and variable) in product cost. Under absorption costing, under-absorbed or over-absorbed overheads arise when actual overheads differ from absorbed overheads.
• Under-absorption = Absorbed Overheads < Actual Overheads → Added to cost of sales / charged to P&L.
• Over-absorption = Absorbed Overheads > Actual Overheads → Deducted from cost of sales / credited to P&L.

Capacity Calculation:
Absolute Capacity = Maximum possible production.
Practical Capacity = Absolute capacity minus unavoidable non-production time (maintenance, breaks, downtime).
Normal Capacity = Practical capacity adjusted for normal idle time (holidays, vacations). This is typically used as the basis for fixing OARs.

13. Marginal Costing — Contribution, Break-Even and Decision Making

Marginal Costing is a technique where only variable costs are charged to cost units, while fixed costs are treated as period costs and written off against contribution in the period incurred.

Key Concepts:
• Marginal Cost: The variable cost of producing one additional unit. It includes direct material, direct labor, and variable overheads.
• Contribution = Sales – Variable Cost (also called Margin in some contexts, though strictly Margin = Sales – Marginal Cost).
• Break-Even Point (BEP): The level of output where Total Revenue equals Total Cost (fixed + variable). At BEP, there is neither profit nor loss.
BEP (Units) = Fixed Costs / Contribution per Unit
BEP (Value) = Fixed Costs / (Contribution / Sales) × 100 = Fixed Costs / P/V Ratio
• P/V Ratio (Profit-Volume Ratio) = (Contribution / Sales) × 100. It indicates the rate at which profit changes with sales.
• Margin of Safety = Actual Sales – Break-Even Sales. It shows how much sales can fall before the business starts incurring losses.
Margin of Safety (Units) = Total Units – BEP Units
Margin of Safety (Value) = Total Sales Value – BEP Sales Value
Margin of Safety Ratio = (Margin of Safety / Actual Sales) × 100

Relationships:
• Profit = Contribution – Fixed Costs
• At BEP, Profit = 0, so Contribution = Fixed Costs
• P/V Ratio = Change in Profit / Change in Sales

Applications in Decision Making:
• Determining the effect of a change in selling price, variable cost, or fixed cost on profit.
• Deciding whether to accept a special order at a reduced price (contribution is positive and fixed costs are covered).
• deciding the optimal product mix when a limiting factor exists (allocate resources to the product with highest contribution per unit of limiting factor).
• Shut-down decision: Continue operation if contribution covers some fixed costs; shut down only if contribution is negative or cannot cover avoidable fixed costs.

Assumptions of Marginal Costing:
• Costs can be clearly classified as fixed or variable.
• Selling price per unit remains constant.
• Variable cost per unit remains constant.
• Fixed costs remain constant within the relevant range.
• Production equals sales (no change in inventory levels).

14. Process Costing — Basic Concepts and Equivalent Production

Process Costing is used when goods pass through one or more processes before completion. Each process is treated as a separate cost centre, and costs are accumulated process-wise. Commonly used in industries like chemicals, textiles, food processing, pharmaceuticals, and oil refining.

Features:
• Production is continuous and sequential.
• Output of one process becomes input of the next.
• Costs are accumulated process-wise.
• Average cost per unit is computed for each process.
• WIP is valued at each stage.

Key Terms:
• Input: Materials or semi-finished goods fed into a process.
• Process Loss: Reduction in output during processing (spoilage, evaporation, waste).
– Normal Process Loss: Expected in ordinary course; cost is absorbed by good output. No value is assigned to normal loss unless there is scrap value, in which case scrap value is credited to the process account.
– Abnormal Process Loss: Unexpected loss; valued at cost per unit and transferred to Costing P&L Account.
• Process Gain: Output in excess of input due to efficient operation. Valued at market price and credited to the process account; the gain is distributed among good output and closing WIP.
• Spoilage: Defective output that may be reworked or sold as scrap.
• Scrap: Low-value residual material from the process.
• By-products: Secondary products of lesser value than the main product. Treated either by assigning negligible cost or by crediting their sales value to the process account.
• Joint Products: Two or more products of relatively similar importance produced simultaneously from the same process up to the split-off point. Common costs are apportioned using physical units, sales value at split-off, or net realisable value methods.

Equivalent Production (Units):
Used to value work-in-progress. Since WIP is not 100% complete, it is expressed in terms of fully completed units.
Equivalent Units = Actual WIP units × Percentage of completion.
Methods:
• FIFO Method: Assumes opening WIP is completed first. Opening WIP equivalent units = Units in opening WIP × (100% – % already completed). Units started and completed during the period are at 100%.
• Weighted Average Method: Combines opening WIP with current production. Average cost per unit = (Total cost of opening WIP + Current period cost) / (Equivalent units of opening WIP + Equivalent units started and completed + Equivalent units of closing WIP).
• First-Time-Through Method: Only fully completed units are considered as equivalent units; WIP is ignored for average cost calculation.

15. Job Costing, Batch Costing and Contract Costing

These are specialized forms of job costing adapted for different types of production environments.

Job Costing:
Used when work is executed according to specific customer orders or jobs. Each job is a separate cost unit. Common in printing presses, engineering workshops, furniture manufacture, repair shops, and construction.
• Job Cost Sheet is prepared for each job recording direct material, direct labor, direct expenses and applied overheads.
• Overheads are apportioned to jobs using predetermined overhead rates.
• Profit is determined as the difference between job cost and agreed contract price.
• If a job is incomplete at the end of the period, the cost sheet shows the value of closing WIP.

Batch Costing:
A variant of job costing where a group (batch) of identical units is produced together. Each batch is treated as a cost unit. Used in pharmaceuticals, garment manufacturing, foundries, and toy production.
• Cost per unit = Total batch cost / Number of units in the batch.
• If a batch produces some defective units, the cost is spread over the good units only.
• Holding cost of unfinished batches and cost of rework are also considered.

Contract Costing:
Used for long-term contracts, typically in construction (buildings, bridges, roads, ships). Contracts may span several accounting periods.
• Contract Account is maintained for each contract. All costs (material, labor, direct expenses, site overheads, head office overheads) are accumulated.
• Value of work certified = Quantity of work completed × Contract rate.
• Value of work uncertified = Work done but not yet certified by the client.
• WIP for Balance Sheet purposes = Value of work certified + Cost of work uncertified – Advance received – Retention money.
• Profit estimation: When contract is < 25% complete, usually no profit is taken to P&L. Between 25%–50%, a prudent portion of profit is transferred to Reserves. Above 50%, profit can be taken proportionally to work certified.
• Notional profit = (Work Certified + Work Uncertified) – Total costs incurred to date.
• Materials on site and plant on site are treated as assets and not expensed.

16. Revision Summary — High-Yield Points and Common Mistakes

High-Yield Revision Points for CMA Foundation:
• Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital. Always balance after every transaction.
• Golden Rules of Accounting: Personal (debit receiver, credit giver), Real (debit what comes in, credit what goes out), Nominal (debit expenses/losses, credit incomes/gains).
• Trade discount is NOT recorded in books; cash discount IS recorded when allowed/received.
• Trial Balance proves arithmetical accuracy only; it does NOT prove absolute correctness.
• Errors not revealed by Trial Balance: omission, commission, principle, compensating, original entry errors.
• In Final Accounts: Outstanding expenses → add in P&L, liability in BS. Prepaid expenses → deduct from P&L, asset in BS. Accrued income → add in P&L, asset in BS. Unearned income → deduct from P&L, liability in BS.
• Depreciation reduces asset value in BS and is an expense in P&L. It is a non-cash expense.
• Bad debts written off reduce debtors in BS and are expenses in P&L. Provision for bad debts further reduces debtors.
• Prime Cost = Direct Material + Direct Labor + Direct Expenses.
• Works Cost = Prime Cost + Factory Overheads.
• Cost of Production = Works Cost + Office Overheads.
• Cost of Goods Sold = Cost of Production + Opening FG – Closing FG.
• Total Cost = COGS + Selling & Distribution Overheads.
• EOQ = √(2DS/H). Reorder Level = Max Usage × Max Lead Time.
• BEP (Units) = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per Unit.
• P/V Ratio = (Contribution ÷ Sales) × 100.
• Margin of Safety = Actual Sales – BEP Sales.
• Profit = (P/V Ratio × Sales) – Fixed Costs, or Contribution – Fixed Costs.
• Under-absorption = Actual overheads > Applied overheads; Over-absorption = vice versa.
• Normal loss has no cost; its scrap value reduces the cost of good output. Abnormal loss is valued and charged to Costing P&L.
• Equivalent units under FIFO exclude work done in earlier periods; weighted average includes them.

Common Mistakes to Avoid:
• Mixing up opening stock (trading account debit) with closing stock (trading account credit, balance sheet asset).
• Forgetting to adjust for outstanding/prepaid items in final accounts.
• Recording trade discount in the books.
• Treating depreciation as a cash outflow.
• Using LIFO/FIFO incorrectly in periods of changing prices.
• Forgetting that provision for bad debts is an additional deduction from debtors (over and above bad debts written off).
• Confusing contribution with gross profit — contribution deducts ALL variable costs including variable overheads.
• Applying wrong base for overhead apportionment (e.g., using number of employees for rent instead of area).
• Not separating normal and abnormal process losses in process costing.
• Taking profit on incomplete contracts without considering the stage of completion and prudence convention.

Important facts

  • Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital — this must always balance.
  • Trade discount is excluded from books; cash discount is recorded only when actually allowed or received.
  • Going concern concept justifies charging depreciation rather than valuing assets at realizable values.
  • Accrual concept requires recognizing revenues and expenses when earned/incurred, not when cash moves.
  • Conservatism (prudence) means anticipate no profit but provide for all possible losses.
  • Trial balance verifies only arithmetical accuracy, not correctness of accounts.
  • Six errors are not detected by trial balance: omission, commission, principle, compensating, original entry, and complete reversal.
  • In the Balance Sheet, provision for doubtful debts is shown as a deduction from debtors (asset side).
  • Prime Cost = Direct Material + Direct Labor + Direct Expenses.
  • Works Cost = Prime Cost + Factory/Manufacturing Overheads.
  • Cost of Production = Works Cost + Office & Administrative Overheads.
  • Cost of Goods Sold = Cost of Production + Opening Finished Goods – Closing Finished Goods.
  • Total Cost = Cost of Goods Sold + Selling & Distribution Overheads.
  • EOQ = √(2DS/H), where D=annual demand, S=ordering cost per order, H=holding cost per unit per annum.
  • Reorder Level = Maximum Usage × Maximum Lead Time.
  • BEP (units) = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution per Unit.
  • P/V Ratio = (Contribution ÷ Sales) × 100.
  • Margin of Safety = Actual Sales – Break-Even Sales.
  • Profit = (P/V Ratio × Sales) – Fixed Costs.
  • Under-absorption occurs when actual overheads exceed applied overheads; the difference is charged to Costing P&L.
  • Normal process loss is not valued; its scrap value, if any, is credited to the process account.
  • Abnormal process loss is valued at cost per unit and transferred to Costing P&L Account.
  • FIFO assumes first goods in are first goods out; LIFO assumes last goods in are first out.
  • Weighted Average cost per unit = (Total cost of opening WIP + Current period cost) ÷ Total equivalent units.
  • For incomplete contracts under 25%, generally no profit is taken to P&L (prudence).
  • ABC Analysis classifies inventory into A (high value, tight control), B (moderate), and C (low value, loose control).
  • Outstanding expenses are added to expenses in P&L and shown as current liabilities in BS.
  • Prepaid expenses are deducted from expenses in P&L and shown as current assets in BS.

Common mistakes

  • Recording trade discount in the books of accounts — trade discount is never recorded; only the net amount after trade discount is entered.
  • Confusing opening stock (debited to Trading Account) with closing stock (credited to Trading Account and shown as current asset in Balance Sheet).
  • Treating depreciation as a cash outflow — it is a non-cash expense that allocates the cost of an asset over its useful life.
  • Applying the wrong basis for overhead apportionment — for example, apportioning rent by number of employees instead of area occupied.
  • Valuing normal process loss — normal loss should have no cost; only abnormal loss is valued and charged to Costing P&L.
  • Forgetting to adjust for outstanding and prepaid items in final accounts, leading to incorrect profit and asset/liability figures.
  • Considering provision for bad debts as a separate expense line rather than an additional deduction from debtors in the Balance Sheet.
  • Using LIFO when prices are falling — this would overstate closing stock and understated profit; choose the method appropriate to the situation.
  • Taking full notional profit on incomplete contracts — prudence requires only a portion of profit to be recognized, especially when contract is less than 50% complete.
  • Confusing contribution with gross profit — contribution deducts all variable costs (including variable overheads), while gross profit only deducts direct costs.
  • Calculating EOQ without converting all costs to the same time basis — ordering cost and holding cost must both relate to the same period (usually annually).
  • Mixing up the positions of asset and liability balances in the Trial Balance — e.g., placing a creditor balance on the debit side.
  • Ignoring the difference between absolute capacity, practical capacity, and normal capacity when calculating overhead absorption rates.
  • Computing equivalent units incorrectly under the FIFO method — remember that opening WIP is only completed to the extent of remaining work in the current period.
