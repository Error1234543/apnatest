1. Law: Meaning, Sources, and Classification

Law is a system of rules created and enforced through social or governmental institutions to regulate behaviour. In India, legal sources are classified broadly into written (statutory) and unwritten (customary, judicial precedent, equity) sources. Statutory law includes constitutions, acts passed by Parliament and State Legislatures, delegated legislation, and rules/regulations framed thereunder. Case law arises from judicial decisions under the doctrine of stare decisis. Customary law operates where written law is silent and a long-standing, reasonable custom is proved.

Classification of law relevant for CMA includes Civil Law versus Criminal Law, Substantive Law versus Procedural Law, and Public Law versus Private Law. Civil law deals with rights and duties between individuals (contracts, property, torts), while criminal law deals with offences against the state. Substantive law creates rights and duties; procedural law prescribes the machinery for enforcing them. The distinction matters because questions often test whether a provision belongs to the substantive or procedural category. For professional practice, students must distinguish between a contract (civil remedy), a tort (civil wrong giving rise to compensation), and a criminal offence (prosecutable by the state).

2. The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Essentials of a Valid Contract

Section 2(h) defines a contract as an agreement enforceable by law. An agreement becomes a contract only when it satisfies the essentials laid down in Sections 10–30.

• Section 10 — Every agreement made by free consent, parties competent to contract, with a lawful consideration and object, and not hereby declared void, is a contract.
• Offer and Acceptance — A valid contract requires a lawful offer (Section 2(a)) and its lawful acceptance (Section 2(b)). The acceptance must be absolute and unconditional (Section 7) and communicated properly (Section 4).
• Lawful Consideration (Sections 2(d), 25) — Consideration means something in return (quid pro quo). It must be real, not illusory; may move at the promisor's desire; and must be lawful (Section 23). Past, present, or future consideration is all valid.
• Competency to Contract (Section 11) — A person is competent if: (a) of the age of majority (18 years), (b) of sound mind, and (c) not disqualified by any law.
• Free Consent (Sections 13–22) — Consent is free when not caused by coercion (Section 15), undue influence (Section 16), fraud (Section 17), misrepresentation (Section 18), or mistake (Section 20–22).
• Lawful Object and Consideration (Section 23) — Consideration or object is unlawful if forbidden by law, defeats any provision, is fraudulent, involves injury to another, or is deemed immoral or opposed to public policy.
• Void Agreements (Sections 24–30) — Agreements in restraint of marriage (Sec.26), trade (Sec.27), legal proceedings (Sec.28), uncertain meanings (Sec.29), or wagering (Sec.30) are void.
• Voidable Contracts (Sections 19, 19A) — When consent is caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, or misrepresentation, the contract is voidable at the option of the aggrieved party.
• Unenforceable contracts arise due to technical defects such as lack of stamping or written form where required.

3. Performance, Breach, and Quasi-Contracts

Performance of contracts is governed by Sections 37–52. Section 37 mandates that parties must perform their promises unless law excuses non-performance. Time is the essence of a contract when expressly agreed or revealed by nature — delay by one party entitles the other to rescind (Sec.55). Rules of performance apply to reciprocal promises (Sec.38–41) — order of performance depends on express terms, nature of transaction, or legal implication.

Breach of contract (Sections 73–75) gives rise to damages. Section 73 provides compensation for loss or damage arising naturally in the usual course of things (normal damages) or which the parties knew to be likely to result (special/remote damages). The injured party must mitigate losses. Section 74 covers liquidated damages — a genuine pre-estimate of loss. Section 75 awards compensation even to a party who has partially performed where the other party prevents performance.

Quasi-contracts (Sections 68–72) are obligations resembling contracts but not true contracts. They are implied by law to prevent unjust enrichment:
• Necessary goods supplied to incapable persons (Sec.68)
• Reimbursement of interested payer (Sec.69)
• Obligation of person enjoying benefit of non-gratuitous act (Sec.70)
• Finder of goods (Sec.71)
• Mistaken payment or delivery (Sec.72)
These sections are frequently tested through assertion-reason type questions asking whether a liability arises from contract or quasi-contract.

4. The Sale of Goods Act, 1930

The Act (Sections 1–65) regulates contracts for the sale of goods. A 'contract of sale of goods' means a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer property in goods to the buyer for a price (Section 4). Goods include movable property other than actionable claims and money; standing crops and things attached to land which are agreed to be severed before sale are also goods.

Sale versus Agreement to Sell (Section 5): In a sale, property passes immediately; in an agreement to sell, it passes at a future time or subject to conditions. The consequences differ on insolvency of buyer/seller, risk (res perit domino in sale), and right to resell.

Conditions versus Warranties (Sections 12–14): A condition is a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract; breach allows repudiation plus damages. A warranty is a collateral stipulation; breach gives damages only, not rejection. Section 16 lays down implied conditions: (a) seller's title, (b) quiet possession, (c) freedom from undisclosed charges, (d) merchantable quality (where seller deals in goods of that description), and (e) fitness for purpose where buyer relies on seller's skill/judgment.

Unpaid Seller's Rights (Sections 45–61): An unpaid seller has a lien on goods (Sec.47), right of stoppage in transit (Sec.48–52), and right of resale (Sec.54). As against the buyer, the unpaid seller retains a right of stoppage and resale.

5. Partnership Act, 1932 — Formation, Rights, Duties, and Dissolution

Section 4 defines partnership as the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. A partnership firm exists de facto from the date of agreement, not from registration.

Registration (Section 58): An application for registration must be signed by all partners and filed with the Registrar. Registration is not mandatory but an unregistered firm cannot sue third parties or other partners to enforce rights arising from the contract (Section 69).

Relations of Partners to Each Other (Sections 11–17): In the absence of agreement, profit and loss are shared equally (Sec.13). No partner is entitled to remuneration (Sec.13), must indemnify for negligence (Sec.14), must render true accounts and full information (Sec.12), and must indemnify for fraud. Partners must carry on business to the greatest common advantage.

Admission, Retirement, and Expulsion (Sections 31–33): Admission requires unanimous consent (unless varied). Retirement may be by notice (3 months unless agreed otherwise) or by agreement. Expulsion is permissible only if conferred by express agreement and acted upon reasonably.

Dissolution (Sections 40–44): Dissolution by agreement (Sec.40); compulsory dissolution on adjudication of all partners as insolvent or on unauthorised dealings (Sec.41); dissolution on happening of certain contingencies (Sec.42) — expiry of term, completion of venture, death, insolvency (unless continued by agreement); notice of dissolution (Sec.43). After dissolution, partners' authority continues only for acts necessary to wind up affairs (Sec.46).

6. The Companies Act, 2013 — Basic Provisions

The Companies Act, 2013 replaced the 1956 Act and is administered by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. Key definitions: 'Company' means a company incorporated under this Act (Sec.2(12)); 'Share' means share in the share capital of a company, whether or not constituted by stock (Sec.2(84)); 'Debenture' includes debenture stock, bonds, and any security recognised as debt (Sec.2(12)).

Types of companies (Section 2): Public company (minimum 7 members, minimum 2 directors), Private company (maximum 200 members, restriction on transfer, minimum 2 members/directors), One Person Company (OPC — single member, Sec.2(62)), Producer Company, Section 8 Company (non-profit), Government Company (≥51% held by Central/State Govt.), Holding and Subsidiary companies (Sec.2(46),(87)).

Incorporation: Promotion, naming, drafting MoA and AoA, filing with Registrar, obtaining Certificate of Incorporation (Sec.7). The MoA states the company's objects and powers; the AoA contains rules for internal management. Doctrine of ultra vires — acts beyond MoA are void.

Shares and Debentures: Equity shares carry voting rights; preference shares carry fixed dividend priority. Prospectus invitation requirements, allotment procedures, and issue of securities are regulated under Chapters V–VII. Directors' appointments, qualifications, duties (Sec.166), and liabilities are covered in Chapter X. Corporate Governance provisions, CSR (Sec.135), and whistleblower policy are key compliance areas.

7. Consumer Protection Act, 2019

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 replaced the 1986 Act with stronger enforcement mechanisms and expanded consumer rights.

Definition of Consumer (Section 2(7)): Any person who hires services for consideration; includes any beneficiary with consent; does not include free services or services where consideration is paid by another government body. A 'complaint' (Sec.2(10)) is any allegation in writing by a consumer alleging a wrong or deficiency.

Consumer Rights (Section 18): Right to be protected against goods/services hazardous to life and health; right to be informed about quality, potency, purity, standard, and price; right to be heard and assured representation; right to choose (access to competitive market); right to seek redressal; right to consumer education.

Deficiency and Unfair Trade Practices: 'Deficiency' (Sec.2(11)) means any fault, imperfection, shortcoming, or inadequacy in the quality, nature, or manner of performance of a service. 'Unfair trade practice' (Sec.2(45)) includes false representation, deceptive descriptions, fraudulent conduct, offering gifts/bonuses as prizes without disclosing terms, and hoarding/re-selling at inflated prices.

Dispute Redressal Mechanism (Sections 34–43): District Commission (up to ₹50 lakh pecuniary jurisdiction), State Commission (₹50 lakh to ₹2 crore), National Commission (above ₹2 crore and appellate jurisdiction). E-filing is mandated. Mediation centres may be set up. Orders are enforceable as decrees of a civil court.

8. Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

A negotiable instrument is a document entitling the lawful possessor to a sum of money payable either to bearer or to order (Section 13). The three principal instruments are Promissory Note (Sec.4), Bill of Exchange (Sec.5), and Cheque (Sec.6).

Promissory Note: An instrument containing an unconditional undertaking by the maker to pay a certain sum only to a specified person or bearer (Sec.4). Essential features: writing, unconditional promise, certainty of parties and amount, signature of maker, and delivery.

Bill of Exchange: An instrument containing an unconditional order by the drawer, signed, directing a certain person (drawee) to pay a certain sum to a specified person or bearer (Sec.5). Parties: Drawer, Drawee/Acceptor, Payee.

Cheque (Section 6): A bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and payable on demand. Features distinguishing a cheque: always drawn on a banker, always payable on demand, no acceptance needed, cross-cheque provisions under Sections 123–131, stop-payment mechanisms, and electronic clearing services.

Dishonour (Section 138 context for cheques): Dishonour by non-payment or non-acceptance. Holder must give notice of dishonour within the prescribed period. Under Section 138, dishonour of a cheque for insufficiency of funds is a criminal offence punishable with imprisonment up to two years or fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.

9. Business Communication — Fundamentals and Process

Communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas, feelings, and meaning between two or more persons through a recognised channel so that understanding is achieved. In business, effective communication is essential for coordination, decision-making, motivation, and control.

The Communication Process (Elements):
• Sender/Source — Originator of the message who encodes an idea.
• Encoding — Converting thoughts into words, symbols, or gestures suitable for transmission.
• Message — The content being communicated.
• Channel/Medium — The path through which the message travels (verbal/written, oral/electronic, face-to-face, digital).
• Receiver — The person for whom the message is intended.
• Decoding — Interpreting and making sense of the message.
• Feedback — The receiver's response indicating understanding, completing the loop.
• Noise — Any interference that distorts the message (physical noise, semantic barriers, psychological barriers).

Key Models: Linear Model (one-way, no feedback — outdated for business), Interactive Model (adds feedback and fields of experience), Transactional Model (simultaneous encoding/decoding with context and relationship). The Shannon-Weaver model highlights that effective communication requires noise reduction at source, channel, and receiver ends.

10. Types of Communication and Barriers

Classification of Business Communication:

By Mode:
• Verbal Communication: Oral (meetings, calls, presentations, interviews) and Written (reports, memos, emails, letters). Oral is faster and allows immediate feedback but is less accurate; written provides permanent record but is slower.
• Non-Verbal Communication: Body language, facial expressions, gestures, posture, eye contact, paralanguage (tone, pitch, volume), proxemics (space), haptics (touch). Research suggests a large proportion of emotional meaning is conveyed non-verbally. Inconsistencies between verbal and non-verbal cues create confusion.

By Direction:
• Downward Communication: From superiors to subordinates — instructions, policies, feedback. Risks: distortion through hierarchy, filtering.
• Upward Communication: Subordinates to superiors — reports, suggestions, grievances. Often blocked by fear or bureaucracy.
• Horizontal/Lateral Communication: Between peers at same level — coordination, problem-solving, inter-departmental liaison.
• Diagonal/Informal Communication: Between different levels and departments; includes grapevine — unofficial communication channels that spread quickly but may carry misinformation.

Barriers to Effective Communication:
• Physical/Environmental: Noise, distance, faulty equipment, poor lighting, interruptions.
• Semantic/Linguistic: Jargon, ambiguous words, language differences, technical terminology misunderstood by laypersons.
• Psychological: Prejudice, preconception, emotions, selective perception, closed-mindedness, lack of attention.
• Organisational: Rigid hierarchy, Information overload, role ambiguity, insufficient training in communication skills.
• Cultural: Different norms, values, communication styles across regions/generations/gender. These barriers cause message distortion, delayed feedback, conflict, and reduced organisational efficiency.

11. Effective Business Correspondence and Report Writing

Effective Business Correspondence follows the 7 Cs principle: Clear, Concise, Correct, Complete, Coherent, Concrete, Courteous. The structure of a formal letter includes: Letterhead/Sender's address, Date, Reference number, Inside address, Subject line, Salutation, Body (opening stating purpose, middle giving details, closing stating action expected), Complimentary close, Signature block, Enclosures, CC (carbon copy).

Types of Business Letters: Inquiry, Order, Complaint, Claim, Adjustment, Covering, Sales, Shipment, Collecting, Circulars, Circular-cum-advertisement. For complaint and claim letters, the tone should be firm but courteous, stating facts clearly with supporting documents referenced, specifying the exact relief sought.

Email Etiquette: Use clear, specific subject lines; avoid all caps or excessive punctuation; maintain professional tone; use proper salutations and sign-offs; keep paragraphs short; attach files mentioned; BCC for mass communications; never use email for confidential matters that require secure channels.

Report Writing: A report is a systematic presentation of facts and analysis for a specific purpose. Structure: Title page, Letter of Transmittal, Table of Contents, Executive Summary, Introduction/Objectives, Methodology, Findings, Conclusions, Recommendations, Appendices. Types: Formal/Informal, Internal/External, Short/Long, Periodic/Frequency-based. The executive summary must stand alone and capture the essence without requiring the reader to read the full report.

12. Résumé Writing, Interview Skills, and Presentation Techniques

A Résumé is a targeted, well-formatted summary of an individual's qualifications, experience, and achievements tailored to a specific job. Key principles: Reverse chronological or functional format depending on career stage; keep it concise (2 pages for early career, maximum 4 pages); tailor each résumé to the specific job description using keywords from the posting; use action verbs and quantify achievements (numbers, percentages, timelines); include sections for contact information, career objective/summary, education, work experience, skills, certifications, and references (available on request).

Common résumé mistakes to avoid: Spelling/grammar errors, vague statements without quantification, irrelevant personal details (photo, marital status, religion where not requested), exaggeration, inconsistent formatting, and generic objectives that do not align with the target role.

Interview Preparation: Research the organisation, understand the job role, prepare STAR-format responses (Situation, Task, Action, Result), rehearse answers to common questions, prepare questions to ask the interviewer, dress professionally, arrive 15 minutes early, bring multiple copies of résumé and supporting documents. Group discussions assess communication, leadership, teamwork, and analytical thinking — participate actively but do not dominate; build on others' points; maintain respectful disagreement.

Presentation Skills: Structure an effective presentation with a clear introduction (hook and roadmap), well-organised body (3–5 main points supported by evidence), and memorable conclusion (summary and call to action). Visual aids should enhance, not replace, the presenter. Practice pacing, eye contact, voice modulation, and handling Q&A. Anticipate difficult questions and practice concise responses. Technology checks should happen before the audience arrives.

Important facts

  • A contract is an agreement enforceable by law (Section 2(h), Indian Contract Act). An agreement not enforceable by law is called a void agreement.
  • For a contract to be valid, parties must be competent — age of majority is 18 years; a person of unsound mind cannot contract (Section 11).
  • Coercion includes committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the IPC; undue influence involves a real or apparent authority over the other party's will.
  • In a sale of goods, risk follows ownership (res perit domino) — the buyer bears risk once property passes even if goods remain with the seller.
  • An unpaid seller's lien allows retention of goods until payment; this right is lost when the seller delivers goods to a carrier without reserving the right of disposal.
  • Under Section 69 of the Contract Act, a person who interests himself in the payment of another's debt (which he is bound to pay) is entitled to reimbursement from the primary debtor.
  • Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business (Section 4, Partnership Act). Sharing of losses is not essential but follows the profit-sharing ratio unless agreed otherwise.
  • A private company must have a minimum of 2 members and directors, and maximum of 200 members; an OPC has a single member (Section 2(62)).
  • The MoA defines the company's objects and powers; acts beyond the MoA are ultra vires and void ab initio — shareholders cannot ratify them.
  • Under the Consumer Protection Act 2019, the pecuniary jurisdiction is: District Commission up to ₹50 lakhs; State Commission ₹50 lakhs to ₹2 crores; National Commission above ₹2 crores.
  • A cheque is always payable on demand and drawn only on a banker — this distinguishes it from a bill of exchange which may be drawn on any person.
  • Non-verbal communication includes body language, facial expressions, paralanguage, proxemics, and haptics; inconsistency between verbal and non-verbal cues confuses receivers.
  • The grapevine is an informal communication network within organisations; it spreads information quickly but is prone to distortion and rumours.
  • Feedback completes the communication loop; without feedback, communication remains a one-way process and its effectiveness cannot be verified.
  • The 7 Cs of effective communication are: Clear, Concise, Correct, Complete, Coherent, Concrete, and Courteous.
