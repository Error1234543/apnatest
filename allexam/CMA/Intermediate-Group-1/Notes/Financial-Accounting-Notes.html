1. Accounting Standards (AS) — Framework, Scope and Key Standards

Accounting Standards are authoritative pronouncements issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) that prescribe specific accounting treatments for transactions, events, and conditions to ensure uniformity and comparability in financial reporting. The Standard Setting Board of ICAI is responsible for formulating and issuing Accounting Standards, which are issued as mandatory requirements under the Companies Act and relevant regulatory frameworks.

Scope and Applicability:
• AS 1 (Disclosure of Accounting Policies): Requires disclosure of significant accounting policies adopted in preparing financial statements. Policies should be consistently applied across periods unless a change is required by an Accounting Standard or results in more appropriate presentation. Changes must be disclosed with effects quantified where practicable.
• AS 2 (Valuation of Inventories): Inventories are valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV). Cost includes all costs of purchase, conversion, and other costs incurred in bringing inventories to their present location and condition. NRV is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less estimated costs of completion and costs necessary to make the sale. Methods of costing include FIFO, weighted average cost; LIFO is not permitted. Provision for obsolescence and deterioration is made when required.
• AS 3 (Cash Flow Statements): Requires preparation and presentation of a cash flow statement classifying cash flows into operating, investing, and financing activities. The indirect method starts from profit before tax and adjusts for non-cash items, working capital changes, and non-operating items. The direct method presents gross receipts and payments. Significant non-cash transactions are disclosed separately.
• AS 4 (Contingencies and Events After the Reporting Period): Contingent liabilities are possible obligations whose existence is confirmed by uncertain future events. They are not recognised but disclosed in notes unless the possibility of outflow is remote. Events after the reporting period that provide evidence of conditions existing at the balance sheet date require adjustment; those indicating conditions arising after the balance sheet date require disclosure only if material.
• AS 5 (Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items and Changes in Accounting Policies): Defines prior period items as income or expenses arising in the current period relating to previous periods. These are recognised in the current period's profit and loss account, not retrospectively. Changes in accounting policies are accounted for retrospectively with comparative figures restated.
• AS 7 (Construction Contracts): Construction contracts are accounted for using the percentage-of-completion method. Contract revenue is recognised in proportion to work completed. Expected losses on entire contracts are recognised immediately in the profit and loss account, not deferred.
• AS 9 (Revenue Recognition): Revenue from sale of goods is recognised when significant risks and rewards of ownership are transferred to the buyer. For services, revenue is recognised based on the proportion of services completed. Interest, dividends, and royalties are recognised on a time-proportion basis unless another method is more appropriate.
• AS 10 (Accounting for Fixed Assets): Fixed assets are initially recognised at cost, comprising purchase price, import duties, non-refundable taxes, and directly attributable costs. Subsequent expenditure is capitalised only if it enhances future economic benefits beyond original standards. Revaluation model is permitted under AS 10, with increases credited to revaluation reserve.
• AS 11 (The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates): Foreign currency transactions are recorded at the spot exchange rate on the date of transaction. Monetary items at year-end are translated at the closing rate; non-monetary items at historical rate. Exchange differences are recognised in profit and loss except for qualifying borrowing differences which may be capitalised.
• AS 15 (Employee Benefits): Covers short-term employee benefits, post-employment benefits (provision and gratuity), and other long-term employee benefits. Defined benefit plans require actuarial valuation. Current service cost, interest cost, and past service cost are recognised in profit and loss. Actuarial gains and losses may be recognised in other comprehensive income or amortised over the expected working life of employees.
• AS 16 (Borrowing Costs): Borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition, construction, or production of a qualifying asset are capitalised as part of the cost of that asset. A qualifying asset is one that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale. Capitalisation begins when expenditures and borrowing costs are incurred and activities are underway; it ceases when substantially all activities are complete.
• AS 17 (Segment Reporting): Requires disclosure of information about operating segments based on the management approach. Reportable segments are identified based on internal reporting structure. Segment revenue, results, assets, liabilities, and reconciliations to consolidated figures must be disclosed.
• AS 18 (Related Party Disclosures): Requires identification and disclosure of related party relationships and transactions. Related parties include parents, subsidiaries, associates, joint ventures, key management personnel, and their close family members. Transactions with related parties must be disclosed including nature of relationship, description of transactions, and amounts involved.
• AS 19 (Leases): Leases are classified as finance leases or operating leases. Finance leases transfer substantially all risks and rewards incidental to ownership; operating leases do not. In finance leases, the lessee recognises an asset and liability at the lower of fair value and present value of minimum lease payments. Lease payments under operating leases are expensed on a straight-line basis.
• AS 20 (Goodwill and Negative Goodwill on Consolidation): On consolidation, goodwill arises when consideration exceeds the fair value of net identifiable assets acquired. Negative goodwill arises when consideration is less than the fair value of net assets. Negative goodwill is recognised immediately in the profit and loss account after reassessing asset valuations and identifying unrecorded liabilities.
• AS 21 (Consolidated Financial Statements): Requires consolidation of parent and subsidiary financial statements. Subsidiaries are entities controlled by the parent (more than 50% voting power or de facto control). Intercompany transactions, unrealised profits, and balances are eliminated in full. Minority interest is shown separately in equity.
• AS 22 (Accounting for Taxes on Income): Current tax is the amount of income taxes payable/recoverable in respect of the taxable profit/loss for the period. Deferred tax is recognised for timing differences between accounting and tax bases using the liability method. Deferred tax assets are recognised only when there is reasonable certainty of sufficient future taxable income.
• AS 23 (Accounting for Investments in Associate Companies): Associates are entities over which the investor has significant influence but not control. Equity method of accounting is used — investment is initially recognised at cost and subsequently adjusted for the investor's share of post-acquisition profits or losses.
• AS 24 (Discontinued Operations): Discontinued operations are components of an entity that have been disposed of or are classified as held for sale. Results of discontinued operations are presented separately in the profit and loss account.
• AS 26 (Intangible Assets): Intangible assets are identifiable non-monetary assets without physical substance. Initially recognised at cost. Subsequently carried at cost less accumulated amortisation and impairment. Useful life is assessed as finite or indefinite. Finite-life intangibles are amortised over their useful life; indefinite-life intangibles are not amortised but tested annually for impairment.
• AS 27 (Financial Reporting of Interests in Joint Ventures): Joint ventures are classified as structural joint ventures, incorporated joint ventures, or contractually agreed joint ventures. Interests in jointly controlled entities are accounted for using proportional consolidation or equity method.

2. Financial Statements — Format, Preparation and Presentation

Financial statements are the formal records of the financial activities and position of a business. Under the Companies Act, 2013, read with Section 129 and Schedule III, companies are required to prepare a Balance Sheet and Statement of Profit and Loss as the primary financial statements, along with accompanying notes and, where applicable, cash flow statements and segment reports.

Format of Balance Sheet (Schedule III): The Balance Sheet presents assets, liabilities, and equity in a structured format. Non-current assets include fixed assets (tangible and intangible), long-term investments, deferred tax assets (net), and other non-current assets. Current assets include inventory, trade receivables, cash and cash equivalents, short-term loans and advances, and other current assets. Non-current liabilities include long-term borrowings, deferred tax liabilities (net), long-term provisions, and other non-current liabilities. Current liabilities include short-term borrowings, trade payables, short-term provisions, and other current liabilities. Shareholders' funds include share capital, reserves and surplus, and money received against share warrants.

Format of Statement of Profit and Loss (Schedule III): The statement presents revenue from operations, other income, finance costs, depreciation and amortisation, and extraordinary items. Items of income and expenditure are disclosed based on either nature or function of expense. Earnings per share (basic and diluted) must be disclosed for listed companies.

Notes to Accounts: Notes form an integral part of financial statements and disclose accounting policies, detailed breakdowns of line items, commitments and contingencies, related party disclosures, and segment information. They provide essential context and explanation for figures reported in the primary statements.

Preparation Requirements:
• Accrual basis of accounting is the foundation unless cash basis is more appropriate for certain items.
• Going concern assumption is presumed unless management intends or is compelled to liquidate.
• Consistency in accounting policies across periods with disclosure of any changes.
• Materiality threshold — omissions or misstatements that could influence decisions are treated as material.
• Matching concept ensures revenues and expenses of a period are matched.
• Every item in the balance sheet and P&L should have a corresponding note explaining the basis of recognition, measurement, and any changes.

Common Adjustments at Year-End:
• Closing stock valuation at lower of cost and NRV.
• Depreciation on fixed assets calculated as per applicable AS and company policy.
• Provisions for bad and doubtful debts based on ageing analysis.
• Outstanding expenses and prepaid expenses adjusted.
• Income received in advance and accrued income recognised.
• Interest on capital and drawings of partners adjusted in partner's capital accounts.
• Rectification of errors discovered during preparation.

Qualities of Good Financial Statements:
• Faithful representation: Complete, neutral, and free from error.
• Relevant: Capable of making a difference in user decisions.
• Comparability: Consistent application enables comparison across periods and entities.
• Verifiability: Different knowledgeable observers could reach consensus.
• Timeliness: Information available before it loses capacity to influence decisions.
• Understandability: Classified and presented clearly for users with reasonable business knowledge.

Statement of Changes in Equity: For companies with complex equity structures, a statement showing changes in each component of equity (share capital, reserves, retained earnings) during the period is prepared as part of the financial statements.

3. Ratio Analysis — Formulas, Classification and Interpretation

Ratio analysis is the process of calculating and interpreting financial ratios derived from financial statements to evaluate a company's performance, financial position, and operational efficiency. Ratios are classified into several categories:

Liquidity Ratios (Short-term solvency):
• Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities. A ratio of 2:1 is traditionally considered satisfactory, though this varies by industry.
• Quick Ratio (Acid Test) = (Current Assets - Inventory - Prepaid Expenses) / Current Liabilities. A ratio of 1:1 is ideal. It is a stricter measure than the current ratio.
• Absolute Liquid Ratio = (Cash + Bank + Marketable Securities) / Current Liabilities. The most conservative liquidity measure.
• Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities.
• Operating Ratio = (Operating Cost / Operating Revenue) × 100. Operating cost excludes non-operating items like interest, tax, and extraordinary items.
• Proprietary Ratio = Shareholders' Funds / Total Assets. Measures the proportion of total assets financed by owners' funds.

Solvency Ratios (Long-term financial position):
• Debt-to-Equity Ratio = Long-term Debt / Shareholders' Funds. A ratio of 2:1 is generally acceptable.
• Debt Service Coverage Ratio = PBDIT / (Interest + Principal Repayment). Measures ability to service debt.
• Interest Coverage Ratio = PBDIT / Interest Charges. Indicates ability to meet interest obligations.
• Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio = (PBDIT - Preference Dividend) / (Interest + Preferred Dividend). After-tax version adjusts for tax shield.
• Net Worth to Total Debt = Shareholders' Funds / Total Debt.

Profitability Ratios:
• Gross Profit Ratio = (Gross Profit / Revenue from Operations) × 100.
• Net Profit Ratio = (Net Profit / Revenue from Operations) × 100.
• Operating Profit Ratio = (Operating Profit / Revenue from Operations) × 100.
• Operating Ratio = (Operating Cost / Revenue from Operations) × 100. Lower is better.
• Return on Capital Employed (ROCE) = (PBDIT / Capital Employed) × 100. Capital employed can be calculated as Shareholders' Funds + Long-term Debt or Total Assets - Current Liabilities.
• Return on Equity (ROE) = (Net Profit after Tax / Shareholders' Funds) × 100.
• Return on Total Assets (ROTA) = (PBDIT / Total Assets) × 100.
• Return on Net Worth = (Net Profit after Tax and Preference Dividend / Net Worth) × 100.
• Earnings Per Share (EPS) = (Net Profit after Tax - Preference Dividend) / Number of Equity Shares.

Activity / Turnover Ratios:
• Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory. Higher indicates efficient inventory management.
• Debtors Turnover Ratio = Credit Sales / Average Debtors. Shows frequency of collection.
• Creditors Turnover Ratio = Credit Purchases / Average Creditors. Shows frequency of payment.
• Working Capital Turnover = Revenue from Operations / Average Working Capital.
• Fixed Assets Turnover = Revenue from Operations / Average Net Fixed Assets.
• Total Assets Turnover = Revenue from Operations / Average Total Assets.
• Stock Turnover Ratio = (Opening Stock + Purchases - Closing Stock) / Average Stock.
• Ramalingam Ratio = Cost of Goods Sold / (Average Stock + Working Capital).

Coverage Ratios:
• Dividend Payout Ratio = Dividend per Share / Earnings per Share.
• Dividend Pay-out Ratio = Total Dividend / Net Profit after Tax.
• Retention Ratio = (Net Profit - Dividend) / Net Profit.
• Corporate Survival Ratio = (Net Profit after Tax + Depreciation) / Total Debt.

Du Pont Analysis (Return on Investment):
• ROI = Net Profit / Capital Employed = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover.
• ROE = Net Profit after Tax / Shareholders' Funds = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier.
• The Du Pont formula decomposes ROE into profitability, efficiency, and leverage components for deeper analysis.

Limitations of Ratio Analysis:
• Based on historical data and may not reflect current conditions.
• Ignored by window dressing and creative accounting practices.
• Different accounting policies across companies limit comparability.
• Qualitative factors are not captured.
• Price level changes can distort ratio interpretation.
• Industry norms vary significantly; cross-industry comparison is often meaningless.

4. Cash Flow Statement — Preparation and Analysis

A Cash Flow Statement is a statement that shows the movement of cash and cash equivalents during an accounting period, classified into operating, investing, and financing activities. It is governed by AS 3 and provides insights into a company's ability to generate cash and meet its obligations.

Classification of Cash Flows:

Operating Activities: These are the principal revenue-producing activities of the entity. Cash inflows include cash received from customers, interest and dividends received, and refunds of income tax. Cash outflows include cash paid to suppliers and employees, interest paid, income tax paid, and payments for goods and services.

Investing Activities: These involve acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents. Cash inflows include proceeds from sale of fixed assets, sale of investments, and recovery of loans. Cash outflows include purchase of fixed assets, purchase of investments, and grants of loans.

Financing Activities: These result in changes in the size and composition of contributed equity and borrowings. Cash inflows include proceeds from issue of shares, debentures, and long-term borrowings. Cash outflows include repayment of borrowings, redemption of shares/debentures, and payment of dividends.

Methods of Presentation:
• Direct Method: Shows major classes of gross cash receipts and payments. Provides more information about cash flows but is rarely used because it requires detailed cash transaction records.
• Indirect Method: Starts from profit before tax and adjusts for non-cash items, non-operating items, and changes in working capital. This is the commonly used method.

Steps for Preparation (Indirect Method):
1. Start with Profit Before Tax (PBT).
2. Add back non-cash charges: depreciation, amortisation, provision for doubtful debts, provision for warranties, loss on sale of assets.
3. Deduct non-cash incomes: gain on sale of assets, dividend income, interest income.
4. Adjust for non-operating items: add losses and deduct gains from sale of fixed assets/investments.
5. Adjust for changes in working capital: increase in current assets (other than cash) is subtracted; decrease is added. Increase in current liabilities is added; decrease is subtracted.
6. Deduct interest paid and income tax paid (if not already adjusted).
7. The result is Net Cash from Operating Activities.

Cash from Investing Activities:
• Proceeds from sale/purchase of fixed assets (at actual cash received/paid).
• Proceeds from sale/purchase of investments.
• Loans granted and recovered.

Cash from Financing Activities:
• Proceeds from issue of shares/debentures/borrowings.
• Repayment of borrowings.
• Redemption of shares/debentures.
• Dividends paid.

Changes in Cash and Cash Equivalents:
Net increase/decrease = Cash from Operations + Cash from Investing + Cash from Financing. Opening cash balance plus this net change equals closing cash balance.

Cash and Cash Equivalents: Includes cash on hand, demand deposits with banks, and short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible to known amounts of cash and subject to insignificant risk of changes in value. Maturity of three months or less from date of investment qualifies.

Important Adjustments:
• Increase in creditors means more purchases on credit; cash saved is added to operating cash flow.
• Decrease in debtors means more cash collected; added to operating cash flow.
• Increase in inventory means more cash spent; subtracted from operating cash flow.
• Decrease in prepaid expenses means less cash paid; added to operating cash flow.
• Non-cash transactions (like issue of shares for assets) are disclosed as notes, not included in cash flow.

Limitations:
• Based on historical cash movements.
• Does not account for non-cash financing and investing activities directly.
• May not predict future cash flows accurately.
• Subject to manipulation through timing of cash receipts and payments.

5. Accounting for Share Capital — Issue, Forfeiture, Surrender and Buy-back

Share capital represents the portion of a company's capital raised by issuing shares to shareholders. The Companies Act, 2013, and relevant Accounting Standards govern the accounting treatment for various aspects of share capital.

Classification of Share Capital:
• Authorized Capital: Maximum amount of share capital that a company is authorised to issue as per its memorandum of association.
• Issued Capital: Portion of authorized capital actually offered to the public for subscription.
• Subscription Capital: Portion of issued capital that shareholders have actually subscribed to.
• Called-up Capital: Portion of subscribed capital that the company has called for payment from shareholders.
• Paid-up Capital: Amount actually paid by shareholders on called-up capital.
• Reserve Capital: Portion of uncalled capital that the company has resolved to call up only in the event of winding up.

Issue of Shares at Par:
When shares are issued at face value, the journal entries are straightforward. On application, amount received is credited to Share Application Account. On allotment, the amount due on allotment is debited to Share Allotment Account and credited to Share Capital. Calls in arrears are shown as a deduction from share capital in the balance sheet.

Issue of Shares at Premium:
Under Section 52 of the Companies Act, 2013, the premium received on issue of shares is credited to Securities Premium Account. This account can be used for: writing off preliminary expenses, writing off commission/discount on shares/debentures, providing for premium on redemption of shares/debentures, issue of fully paid bonus shares, and buying back of shares. It cannot be used for cash dividends.

Issue of Shares at Discount:
Shares cannot be issued at a discount under the Companies Act, 2013. If issued at discount, the discount amount is shown as a separate deduction from share capital.

Forfeiture of Shares:
When a shareholder fails to pay calls due, the board may forfeit the shares after following proper procedure. On forfeiture:
• Share Capital is debited with the amount called up on forfeited shares.
• Forfeiture Account is credited with amount actually received on those shares.
• The difference (called-up amount minus amount received) is credited to Forfeiture Account as profit.
This profit is shown under Reserves and Surplus in the balance sheet until the shares are reissued.

Re-issue of Forfeited Shares:
On reissue, cash is received and Share Forfeiture Account is debited. The reissue price may be at par, at a discount, or at a premium. If reissued at a discount, the discount is debited to Forfeiture Account to the extent of profit available thereon. Any remaining profit from Forfeiture Account is transferred to Capital Reserve.

Surrender of Shares:
Voluntary surrender occurs when shareholders return shares to the company, usually during a depression to reduce capital. The accounting treatment is similar to forfeiture but initiated voluntarily by shareholders.

Buy-back of Shares:
Under Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013, a company may buy back its own shares from 10% to 25% of total paid-up capital and free reserves in a financial year. Sources of funds for buy-back include free reserves, securities premium, and proceeds from shares/debentures. On buy-back, the amount paid is debited to the relevant reserve and the shares are cancelled. The buy-back amount cannot be deducted from share capital; instead, it reduces free reserves and securities premium.

Bonus Issue (Rights Issue):
Bonus shares are issued freely to existing shareholders by capitalising reserves. The company transfers an equivalent amount from reserves to share capital. Types of reserves that can be capitalised include securities premium, general reserve, profit and loss account balance, and revaluation reserve (only to the extent of realised profits).

Redemption of Preferences Shares:
Preference shares can be redeemed out of: (a) free profits available for dividend, (b) proceeds of a fresh issue of shares, or (c) the company's capital reserves. When redeemed out of fresh issue proceeds, a Provision for Redemption of Preference Shares is created equal to the fresh issue amount. If redeemed out of profits alone, a Capital Redemption Reserve is created equal to the amount redeemed.

6. Accounting for Debentures — Issue, Redemption and Transfer

Debentures are instruments that acknowledge debt owed by a company to the debenture holders. They represent a loan from the public at a fixed rate of interest. Accounting for debentures involves various aspects including issue, discount/premium, redemption, and conversion.

Issue of Debentures:
• At Par: Amount received on application is credited to Debenture Application Account. On allotment, debentures are allotted and the amount is transferred to Debenture Account.
• At Premium: Under Section 53 of the Companies Act, 2013, debentures can be issued at premium. The premium is credited to Securities Premium Account and can be used for premium on redemption.
• At Discount: Debentures can be issued at discount. The discount is shown as a separate item under non-current liabilities (as a deduction from the carrying amount of debentures) and written off over the period of debentures.
• As Collateral Security: When debentures are issued as security for a loan, no journal entry is passed in the books. A note is given in the balance sheet disclosing the amount of debentures issued as security.

Discount on Issue of Debentures:
The discount represents additional cost of borrowing. It is written off over the tenure of debentures. Two methods are used:
• Sinking Fund Method: A separate fund is created by setting aside equal annual amounts. The fund is invested in specified securities. At maturity, the accumulated fund plus interest is used to redeem debentures.
• Annual Provision Method: An equal annual charge is made to P&L account without creating a separate fund. The provision accumulates in a Debenture Redemption Reserve or similar account.

Redemption of Debentures:
• Out of Profits: When redeemed out of profits, a Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) must be created equal to the amount of redemption. Under the Companies Act, 2013, at least 25% of the debenture maturity amount must be transferred to DRR before redemption. The DRR is shown under Reserves and Surplus and can be used only for redemption purposes.
• Out of Fresh Issue: When new debentures are issued to raise funds for redemption, a Provision for Redemption of Debentures is created. The proceeds from the new issue are invested in authorized securities.
• By Conversion: Existing debenture holders convert their debentures into equity shares or new debentures. The old debenture liability is extinguished and replaced with the new liability/equity.
• By Purchase in Open Market: The company buys back its own debentures from the stock exchange. Gains or losses on purchase are transferred to DRR or P&L.
• By Draw of Lottery: Debentures bearing prize coupons are drawn periodically. Prize money is accounted for as a separate expense.

Transfer to DRR:
• Mandatory under Companies Act for certain classes of companies.
• Minimum 25% of debenture amount must be transferred before redemption.
• DRR can be utilised only for redemption of debentures.

Accounting for Premium on Redemption:
Premium payable on redemption is shown as a liability and written off over the remaining life of debentures. It can be met from Securities Premium Account to the extent available.

Journal Entries for Key Transactions:
• Issue at discount: Bank A/c Dr. to Debenture A/c (at nominal); Discount A/c Dr. to Bank A/c (balance);
• Written off discount: P&L A/c Dr. to Discount on Issue of Debentures A/c (annually);
• Creating DRR: P&L A/c Dr. to DRR A/c;
• Investment of DRR: Investment A/c Dr. to Bank A/c;
• On redemption: Debenture A/c Dr. to Bank A/c;
• Transfer from DRR: DRR A/c Dr. to Debenture A/c (on redemption).

Important facts

  • Under AS 15, employee benefits include short-term, post-employment, and other long-term benefits.
  • Current service cost is always recognised in profit or loss.
  • Actuarial gains and losses can be recognised in OCI or amortised over employees' expected working life.
  • Under AS 16, borrowing costs directly attributable to qualifying assets are capitalised.
  • Qualifying assets take a substantial period to get ready for intended use.
  • Interest on long-term borrowings is a borrowing cost eligible for capitalisation.
  • Under AS 19, finance leases transfer substantially all risks and rewards of ownership.
  • Operating lease payments are expensed on a straight-line basis over the lease term.
  • Under AS 22, deferred tax assets are recognised only when there is reasonable certainty of future taxable income.
  • Timing differences give rise to deferred tax; permanent differences do not.

Common mistakes

  • Capitalising borrowing costs for assets that do not qualify (e.g., inventories produced quickly).
  • Recognising deferred tax assets without assessing probability of future taxable profits.
  • Classifying operating leases as finance leases or vice versa.
  • Using closing balances instead of average balances in ratio analysis.
  • Forgetting to adjust for prior period items in the current period P&L.
