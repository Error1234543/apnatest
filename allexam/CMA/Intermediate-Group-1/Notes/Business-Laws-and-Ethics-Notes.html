1. Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Essentials of a Valid Contract and Classes of Contracts

A contract is defined under Section 2(h) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 as 'an agreement enforceable by law.' The foundational equation is: Agreement + Enforceability = Contract. Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract.

Essential Elements of a Valid Contract (Sections 10–25):
• Offer and Acceptance (Sections 2(a), 2(b)): A valid offer must be definite, communicated, and capable of acceptance. Acceptance must be absolute and unconditional (Section 2(b)), communicated in a prescribed manner, and must match the terms of the offer exactly — this is the 'mirror image' rule. A counter-offer terminates the original offer (Section 2(d)).
• Lawful Consideration (Section 2(d)): Consideration means 'quid pro quo' — something in return. It must be real, lawful, and move at the desire of the promisor. Nominal or illusory consideration is insufficient. Past consideration is generally invalid unless it falls within exceptions.
• Competency of Parties (Section 11): Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority (18 years per Majority Act, 1875 — 20 for Guardians and Wards Act matters), of sound mind, and not disqualified from contracting by any law.
• Free Consent (Sections 12–22): Consent is free when not caused by coercion (Section 15 — including acts punishable under IPC), undue influence (Section 16 — fiduciary relationships such as attorney-client, parent-child, doctor-patient), fraud (Section 17 — false representation with intent to deceive), misrepresentation (Section 18 — innocent false statement), or mistake (Section 20 — both parties under mistake of fact; Section 21 — mistake of law renders contract voidable, not void, per later amendment).
• Lawful Object (Section 23): The object must not be forbidden by law, fraudulent, injurious to another's person or property, or deemed immoral/opposed to public policy.
• Intention to Create Legal Relations: There must be a clear intention to create legally binding obligations. Social/domestic agreements generally lack this intent (Balfour v. Balfour principle).
• Certainty and Possibility of Performance (Sections 29, 56): Terms must be certain and not vague. Agreements with impossible performance are void.
• Compliance with Legal Formalities: Written registration where mandated by law.

Classes of Contracts:
• Express Contract: Terms explicitly stated in words (written or oral).
• Implied Contract: Inferred from conduct or circumstances (e.g., parking a car in a paid garage).
• Quasi-Contract (Sections 68–72): Also called 'implied-in-law' contracts. Where one person has enjoyed the benefit of another's goods or services without a formal contract, the law implies a promise to reimburse. Specific provisions cover necessaries supplied to incapable persons (Sec. 68), payment of money due (Sec. 69), finder of goods (Sec. 71), and non-gratuitous acts (Sec. 72).
• Void Agreement (Section 2(g)): An agreement not enforceable by law — e.g., agreement with a minor (Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose — minor's agreement is void ab initio), agreement in restraint of marriage (Sec. 26), restraint of trade (Sec. 27 — with exceptions), restraint of proceedings (Sec. 28), uncertain agreements (Sec. 29), wagering agreements (Sec. 30).
• Voidable Contract (Section 2(i)): Enforceable at the option of the aggrieved party — e.g., consent obtained through coercion or undue influence.
• Unenforceable Contract: Valid but cannot be enforced due to some technical defect (e.g., unstamped document).
• Illegal Agreement: One whose object or consideration is unlawful; it is void ab initio and has greater consequences than a void agreement.

2. Indian Contract Act — Doctrine of Privity, Discharge, and Remedies for Breach

Doctrine of Privity of Contract: Only a party to a contract can sue or be sued upon it. However, recognized exceptions include: (a) Trust — a beneficiary may enforce a contract made for his benefit (b) Assignment of monetary claims (c) Collateral contracts (d) Family arrangements (e) Contracts constituted as running covenants affecting land (f) Negligence — third-party tort claims in Donoghue v. Stevenson spirit.

Discharge of Contract (when contractual obligations cease):
• Discharge by Performance — actual performance or attempted performance (tender) where the promisor offers to perform but the promisee refuses.
• Discharge by Mutual Agreement/Novation (Section 62): When parties agree to replace the old contract with a new one, rescind it, or redirect performance to a new party. The original contract is extinguished.
• Discharge by Impossibility (Section 56): When performance becomes impossible or unlawful after the contract is made. Agreements to do impossible things are void. If impossibility arises after formation (supervening impossibility), the contract becomes void. Frustration doctrine applies.
• Discharge by Breach: Actual breach (failure to perform on due date) or anticipatory breach (intimation before due date).
• Discharge by Operation of Law: Death (personal service contracts), merger (higher security substituted), or lapse of time (limitation).
• Discharge by Material Alteration (Section 63): Any material alteration to a written instrument without consent discharges the party whose instrument is altered.

Remedies for Breach of Contract (Sections 73–75):
• Damages — compensatory in nature, not penal. The leading case is Hadley v. Baxendale. Two rules: (1) Those damages naturally arising in the usual course of things; (2) Those reasonably contemplated by both parties at the time of contract as likely to result from breach. Damages for loss of profit, reputation, physical suffering, and mental anguish may be claimed if foreseeable.
• Specific Performance: Court-ordered performance where damages are inadequate (applies where contract involves unique goods or land).
• Injunction: Restraint order preventing a party from doing what they have agreed not to do.
• Quantum Meruit: 'As much as earned' — recovery for partial or incomplete performance when the other party has enjoyed the benefit.
• Restitution: Restoration of any benefit conferred on the breaching party.

3. Law of Indemnity, Guarantee, and Bailment

Indemnity (Sections 124–125):
• Definition: A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused by the conduct of the promisor himself or of any third person.
• Essential Features: There are two parties — the indemnifier (who promises) and the indemnified (who is protected). The obligation arises only when the indemnified suffers actual loss. No consideration is expressly required but is presumed.
• Rights of Indemnitee (Section 125): The person promised to be indemnified is entitled to recover costs incurred in defending proceedings, sums paid under compromise that are not unreasonable, and the actual loss itself.
• Notable Case: Chikkammasamma v. Somaswamy — an agreement to indemnify against criminal liability is not enforceable if it contravenes public policy.

Guarantee (Sections 126–148):
• Structure: Three parties — Surety (guarantor), Principal Debtor (main borrower), and Creditor (lender). The surety undertakes to perform the promise or discharge the liability of a third person.
• Essential Features: At least two parties in substance; the main contract between principal debtor and creditor is primary, and the guarantee is secondary.
• Revocation (Section 130): A continuing guarantee may be revoked by notice, by death of the surety, or by any variation in terms by mutual consent.
• Discharge of Surety (Sections 134–141):
— Discharged by variance in terms (Sec. 133)
— Discharged by release or discharge of principal debtor (Sec. 134)
— Discharged by creditor's act or omission impairing surety's eventual recourse (Sec. 139)
— Discharged by guarantee contract being usurious (Sec. 140)
• Right of Surety: Upon discharge, the surety stands in the shoes of the creditor and may enforce against the principal debtor (Sec. 140).

Bailment and Pledge (Sections 148–181):
• Bailment (Sec. 148): Delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. Key points: goods must be delivered, purpose must be specified, and return/disposal is mandatory. A pawnee's right of retention (Sec. 170) allows holding goods until charges are paid even after the debt is discharged if there are subsequent charges.
• Pledge (Sec. 172): A subset of bailment where goods are delivered as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise. The pawnor and pawnee are styled accordingly. Special rules apply to rights of rightful owners of pledged goods (Sec. 178A).
• Duties of Bailee/Pawnee: To take reasonable care of goods, not to make unauthorized use, to return goods, and to deliver any increase/profit from goods.

4. Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Conditions, Warranties, and Transfer of Property

Sale of Goods Act governs the transfer of property in goods from seller to buyer for a price. Goods include every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money (Sec. 2(7)).

Key Distinctions:
• Sale vs. Agreement to Sell: A sale is an executed contract where property passes immediately; an agreement to sell is executory where property passes at a future date or subject to conditions.
• Contract of Sale (Sec. 4): Contains three elements — seller, buyer, and price. Goods may be existing, future, or contingent.

Conditions and Warranties (Sections 12–17):
• Condition (Sec. 12(1)): A stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract. Breach gives right to repudiate the contract and claim damages.
• Warranty (Sec. 12(2)): A stipulation collateral to the main purpose. Breach only allows damages, not rejection.
• Types of Conditions: Express (clearly stated), Implied (read into contract by law or custom), Condition precedent/subsequent.
• Implied Conditions (Sec. 14–17):
— Condition of title (Sec. 14): Seller must have right to sell.
— Condition of quiet possession (Sec. 14(b)).
— Condition that goods are of merchantable quality (Sec. 15, now ' satisfactory quality').
— Condition as to fitness for buyer's purpose (Sec. 16 — caveat emptor subject to exceptions).
— Sale by sample (Sec. 17).
• Sale by Description: Where goods are sold by description, there is an implied condition that the goods correspond with the description.

Transfer of Property (Sections 20–25):
• Specific/ascertained goods: Property passes when intended by the parties (Sec. 20).
• Rules for ascertaining intention (Sec. 21–22): Unascertained goods pass when delivered unconditionally; conditional sale requires fulfillment of condition.
• Reservation of right of disposal (Sec. 23): Seller may reserve right until payment.
• Risk passes with property (Sec. 22, Rule 1) — 'perish prima domino' (risk follows ownership).
• Unsold goods and unpaid seller's rights (Sec. 45–54):
— Right of lien (Sec. 47): Retain goods until price is paid.
— Right of stoppage in transit (Sec. 48–52): Stop goods while in transit if buyer is insolvent.
— Right of resale (Sec. 54).

Auction Sales (Sec. 64): Auctioneer may withdraw goods before sale; bidder may retract bid before acceptance; auction sale complete when hammer falls.

5. Partnership Act, 1932 — Formation, Rights, Duties, and Dissolution

Definition (Sec. 4): Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Key features: (1) Two or more persons; (2) Agreement; (3) Sharing of profits (profit-sharing is prima facie evidence but not conclusive proof); (4) Mutual agency — every partner is both agent and principal.

Partnership Deed: A written agreement recording terms; though oral partnerships are valid, written deed avoids disputes.

Registration: Not compulsory but unregistered firms face severe disabilities — cannot sue third parties or partners; claims above Rs. 100 are barred (Sec. 69).

Kind of Partners:
• Active/Working Partner: Acts in business.
• Sleeping/Dormant Partner: Shares profits but does not participate in management.
• Nominal Partner: Lends name without ownership; liable to outsiders as if actual partner.
• Partner by Estoppel/Holding Out: Represents himself or is represented as partner; liable to third parties.
• Minor Partner: Cannot be a full partner but may be admitted to benefits with unanimous consent of all partners (Sec. 30).

Rights and Duties of Partners (Secs. 12–13):
• Rights: Right to take part in management, right to be consulted, right to share profits equally (if no agreement), right to interest on capital (if agreed, max 6%), right to remuneration for extra services, right to indemnity, right to use partnership property only for partnership purposes, right of inspection of books.
• Duties: Duty to render true accounts and full information, duty to indemnify for loss caused by fraud, duty to act in good faith, duty to carry on business to the greatest common advantage, duty to share losses equally (not proportionally unless agreed).

Dissolution of Partnership (Secs. 40–44):
• Dissolution of Partnership (changing relationship) vs. Dissolution of Firm (ending relationship entirely).
• Dissolution by agreement, compulsory dissolution on adjudication of insolvency, dissolution on happening of certain contingencies (expiry, completion, illegality).
• Dissolution by notice (where partnership is at will).
• Notice of dissolution must be published in the Official Gazette and known to those who had knowledge of the partnership.
• Adherence to firm name by retired partner continues liability.
• Rights and duties of outgoing partners and persons representing them continue until notice is given.

Admisson and Retirement: Admission requires unanimity unless otherwise agreed; retirement similarly requires consent of all. Rights and obligations of outgoing partners are governed by Sec. 32.

6. Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) Act, 2008 — Key Provisions

The LLP Act, 2008 introduced a new form of business organization in India blending features of partnership and company. An LLP is a body corporate and a legal entity separate from its partners, with unlimited liability of partners as provided in the LLP agreement (Sec. 3).

Key Features:
• Separate Legal Entity: LLP has perpetual succession and can own property, sue, and be sued in its own name.
• Limited Liability: Partners' liability is limited to their agreed contribution; personal assets are protected.
• Minimum Two Designated Partners: At least two designated partners, out of which one must be an individual who is a body corporate and at least one designated partner must be a body corporate.
• Incorporation: Filed with Registrar through Form 2. A Digital Signature Certificate and DIN/DSC are required.
• LLP Agreement (Sec. 11): Can be oral or written; governs mutual rights, duties, profit-sharing ratio, capital contribution.
• Compliance Requirements: Maintenance of books of account, filing annual returns (Form 11) and statement of accounts (Form 8), audit thresholds (turnover > Rs. 40 lakhs or contribution > Rs. 25 lakhs requires statutory audit).
• Conversion: Existing partnership firms and companies can convert into LLPs subject to conditions.
• Differences from Partnership: No unlimited joint liability, separate legal personality, perpetual succession, lower compliance burden than companies.
• Differences from Company: Lower compliance, flexible internal governance, no requirement for board meetings orAGM, but fewer disclosure requirements.
• Offences (Chapter XII): Penalties for default in filing, false statements, conversion to partnership or company without compliance.

7. Companies Act, 2013 — Formation, Share Capital, Directors, and Meetings

Formation and Incorporation:
• A company is an artificial person created by law, having a separate legal entity, perpetual succession, and a common seal. It can own property, incur liability, sue, and be sued.
• Types: Public company (minimum 7 members, minimum 3 directors), Private company (minimum 2 members, maximum 200, minimum 2 directors), One Person Company (OPC — minimum 1 member, 1 director), Producer Company, Section 8 Company (non-profit).
• Incorporation process: Promotion → Name approval (RUN/RSP) → Drafting MOA & AOA → Subscription to shares → Filing with Registrar → Certificate of Incorporation (Sec. 7) → Commencement of Business (Sec. 11 — for public companies).

Memorandum of Association (MOA) — Sec. 4:
• Six clauses: Name clause, Registered office clause, Objects clause (main + ancillary), Liability clause, Capital clause, Subscription clause.
• Ultra Vires Doctrine: Acts beyond the objects clause are void ab initio and cannot be ratified even by unanimous shareholders.

Articles of Association (AOA) — Sec. 5:
• Internal rules governing conduct of company's affairs. Subservient to MOA and the Act. Default provisions in Tables F, G, H apply unless altered.

Share Capital (Sec. 43, 44):
• Equity shares: Carry voting rights and residual claim on dividends and assets.
• Preference shares: Right to preferential payment of dividend and capital repayment; may be cumulative/non-cumulative, redeemable/irredeemable, convertible/non-convertible.
• Rights of shareholders: Right to vote, receive dividends, inspect books, participate in management, pre-emptive rights on further issues.

Directors (Sec. 149–252):
• Board composition: Minimum 3 for public, 2 for private, 1 for OPC. At least one woman director and one resident director in India (182 days).
• Independent directors (Sec. 149(6)): Requirement for listed and specified public companies — at least 1/3 of total directors, independence criteria defined in Sec. 149(3).
• KMP (Key Managerial Personnel): Managing Director, Whole-Time Director, CEO, CFO, Company Secretary.
• Appointment: First directors by subscribers; subsequent by shareholders. Holding office subject to retirement by rotation (except MD/WTD/Independent).
•董事's duties (Sec. 166): Good faith, proper care and diligence, avoid conflict of interest, not achieve undue gain, act within powers, promote company objects.
• Liabilities: Penal and civil liabilities for contravention, disqualification (Sec. 164).

Meetings:
• Board Meetings: Minimum 4 in a year, gap not exceeding 120 days.
• AGM: Within 6 months of financial year-end, gap not exceeding 15 months.
• EGM: For special business.
• Members' Meeting: Quorum requirements, proxy rights, voting procedures (poll/vote by show of hands).
• Class Rights Meetings: For variation of class rights (Sec. 48–59).

8. Companies Act — Corporate Governance, CSR, Minority Protection, and Winding Up

Corporate Governance:
• Board responsibilities include strategic direction, oversight of management, risk management, and accountability to shareholders.
• Audit Committee (Sec. 177): Mandatory for listed and prescribed companies — financial reporting, internal controls, auditor appointment, whistleblower mechanism.
• Nomination and Remuneration Committee (Sec. 178): Recommends appointment, remuneration, and evaluation of directors and KMP.
• Stakeholder Relationship Committee (Sec. 178(2)).
• Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) — Sec. 135: Applicable to companies meeting any of three thresholds — net worth ≥ ₹500 crore, OR turnover ≥ ₹1,000 crore, OR net profit ≥ ₹5 crore during any financial year. Must constitute a CSR Committee, frame a CSR Policy, and spend at least 2% of average net profits of the preceding three years on CSR activities listed in Schedule VII. Unspent CSR must be transferred to a fund specified in Schedule VII unless the project is suspended/abandoned.

Protection of Minorities:
• Oppression and Mismanagement (Sec. 241–242): Members can petition Tribunal if affairs are conducted in a manner oppressive or prejudicial to public interest or members' interests. Remedy includes regulation of conduct, purchase of shares, alteration of MOA/AOA, and winding up.
• Class Action Suits (Sec. 245): Shareholders (minimum 100 or 1/10th of members) can file suits for grievance against company/promoters/directors for fraudulent or wrongful acts.
• Investigation (Sec. 210–217): Central Government may order investigation.

Winding Up (Sec. 270–322):
• Modes: Compulsory winding up by Tribunal (Sec. 271 — grounds include inability to pay debts, special resolution, just and equitable ground), Voluntary winding up (members' voluntary — solvency declaration required; creditors' voluntary — no solvency declaration), Supervised winding up.
• Consequences: Company ceases to carry on business except for beneficial winding up; property vests in Liquidator; all proceedings stay; company dissolves after 3 months from distribution of assets.

9. Consumer Protection Act, 2019 — Rights, Authorities, and Redressal Machinery

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 replaced the 1986 Act and introduced significant reforms:

Definition of Consumer (Sec. 2(7)): Any person who hires services for consideration, including any beneficiary with consent, and includes a member of a cooperative society. Does not include a person who obtains goods/services for commercial purpose (more than 50% self-use).

Consumer Rights (Sec. 17):
• Right to be protected against marketing of hazardous goods and services.
• Right to be informed about quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard, and price.
• Right to be heard and to seek redressal.
• Right to seek consumer education.
• Right to choose — access to a variety of goods/services at competitive prices.
• Right to safety — protection against goods/services dangerous to life and property.

Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) — Sec. 10:
• Statutory authority empowered to inspect, investigate, and enforce; issue recall orders, penalize misleading advertisements, and classify products based on risk. It can suo motu take action or on referral/complaint.

Product Liability (Sec. 2(28), 25–28):
• Manufacturer or service provider liable to compensate for harm caused by defective product or deficient service. Strict liability applies.

E-filing and Mediation (Sec. 34, 74):
• Settlement possible through mediation centers; mediation reports filed before the District Commission, State Commission, or National Commission.

Redressal Machinery (Three-Tier): (Note: pecuniary jurisdiction amounts as per latest amendment, verify current figures.)
• District Commission: Up to ₹50 lakh (value of goods/services + compensation claimed).
• State Commission: ₹50 lakh to ₹2 crore.
• National Commission: Above ₹2 crore and appellate jurisdiction.

Penalties: For deficiency in service or defect in goods, compensation, stoppage of practice, recall of goods, and imprisonment/fine for offenses.

10. Competition Act, 2002 — Anti-Competitive Agreements, Abuse of Dominance, and Mergers

The Competition Act, 2002 (amended in 2007) was enacted to prevent practices having adverse effect on competition, protect consumer interests, and promote free trade.

Competition Commission of India (CCI) — Sec. 7:
• Statutory body responsible for implementing the Act. Has powers of a civil court, can regulate its own procedure, appoint experts, and impose penalties.

Anti-Competitive Agreements (Sec. 3):
• Horizontal Agreements: Between competitors at same level (cartels, price-fixing, bid-rigging, market allocation, output restriction) — presumed to have appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC).
• Vertical Agreements: Between enterprises at different levels (exclusive supply, tie-in, refusal to deal, exclusive distribution, resale price maintenance).
• Presumption of AAEC applies to horizontal agreements; vertical agreements require proof of AAEC.
• Exceptions: Joint ventures (Sec. 3(5)), reasonable quality improvement, export cartels (with intimation to CCI), rationalization, modernization.

Abuse of Dominant Position (Sec. 4):
• Dominant position — position of strength enabling enterprise to operate independently of competitive forces.
• Abuse includes: imposition of unfair conditions/prices, limiting production or markets, denying market access, imposing supplementary obligations, using dominant position in one market to enter another.
• Remedies: Cease and desist orders, modification of agreements, imposition of penalty (up to 10% of average turnover for 3 preceding financial years), divestiture in certain cases.

Combination Regulation (Sec. 5–6):
• Mergers, acquisitions, and amalgamations meeting asset turnover thresholds require prior notification to CCI.
• CCI examines whether combination causes or is likely to cause AAEC.
• Penalties for implementing combination without approval.

Natural Resources (Sec. 19): CCI considers availability of substitute goods/services, consumer welfare, and market power while examining dominance.

11. Business Ethics — Principles, Frameworks, and Corporate Ethics Integration

Business Ethics definition: The study of appropriate business policies and practices regarding potentially controversial subjects including corporate governance, insider trading, bribery, discrimination, corporate social responsibility, and fiduciary responsibilities.

Theoretical Foundations:
• Utilitarianism: Actions are right if they maximize overall happiness/benefits.
• Deontology (Kantian): Actions are judged by rules/duties, not consequences — moral absolutes exist.
• Virtue Ethics: Focuses on character and virtues of the moral agent.
• Justice Theory (Rawls): Fairness in distribution of benefits and burdens.
• Rights Theory: Respect for fundamental human rights in business decisions.

Ethical Decision-Making Framework:
1. Identify the ethical issue
2. Gather facts — who are stakeholders?
3. Evaluate alternative actions using ethical theories
4. Make a decision and test it (publicity test: would you want it published?)
5. Act and reflect on outcomes

Types of Ethical Issues in Business:
• Ethical dilemmas in hiring and firing
• Discrimination and harassment
• Confidentiality and privacy
• Environmental ethics
• Marketing ethics — truth in advertising, pricing ethics, predatory behavior
• Insider trading and market manipulation
• Corporate culture and tone at the top

Importance of Ethics in Business:
• Builds trust with stakeholders, reduces litigation, improves brand reputation, enhances employee morale, ensures long-term sustainability, prevents regulatory overreach, supports societal license to operate.

12. Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR), ESG, and Professional Ethics for CMAs

CSR Beyond Section 135:
• CSR has evolved from philanthropy to strategic engagement. Global reporting includes UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), B Corp certification concepts, and stakeholder capitalism models.
• Triple Bottom Line: People, Planet, Profit — measuring success across social, environmental, and economic dimensions.
• Materiality Assessment: Identifying which ESG factors are most relevant to the business and its stakeholders.

ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance):
• Environmental: Carbon footprint, waste management, water usage, renewable energy transition, climate risk disclosure.
• Social: Diversity and inclusion, labor standards, human rights in supply chain, community engagement, data privacy.
• Governance: Board independence, executive compensation, anti-corruption measures, shareholder rights, transparency in reporting.
• SEBI BRSR (Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report): Mandatory for top 1,000 listed companies — aligns with National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct.

Professional Ethics for Cost and Management Accountants:
• Code of Ethics issued by ICMAI is based on IESBA International Code — five fundamental principles:
— Integrity: Be straightforward and honest in professional and business relationships.
— Objectivity: Do not allow bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence to override professional judgments.
— Professional Competence and Due Care: Maintain professional knowledge and skill, act diligently.
— Confidentiality: Respect confidentiality of information acquired, disclose only with authority or legal requirement.
— Professional Behavior: Comply with laws and regulations, avoid discrediting the profession.
• Threats to Compliance: Self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation threats — and safeguards to counter them.
• Whistleblower Mechanism (Sec. 177(9) Companies Act): Every company must establish a vigil mechanism for directors and employees to report genuine concerns.

13. Key Judicial Precedents and Important Case Laws

Critical case laws frequently tested in CMA Intermediate:

Indian Contract Act:
• Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903): Minor's agreement is void ab initio — not merely voidable.
• Bolanos v. Indian Oil Corp: Impossibility of performance — supervening difficulty does not necessarily frustrate contract unless it makes performance truly impossible.
• Chandora Textiles Ltd. v. National Insurance Co.: Insurable interest must exist at time of loss.
• Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co.: Doctrine of frustration — contract contains an express or implied term that it is to be performed only if the contemplated situation persists.
• Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.: Offer to the world at large can be accepted by anyone performing the condition.

Sale of Goods Act:
• Roberson v. Rochester folding Bag Co.: Implied warranty of fitness for purpose — buyer must rely on seller's skill and judgment.
• Lee v. Griffin: Contract of sale of goods vs. contract for work and materials — test is whether the substance is the article produced or the skill/labor.

Partnership Act:
• Badridas Jaitamal v. CIT: Partnership is not a legal entity distinct from partners for tax purposes (now modified by taxation law).
• K.P. Varghese v. ITO: Sale of partnership assets by retiring partner is a capital transaction, not revenue.
• Kalyanaswami Mills Co. Ltd. v. CIT: De facto dissolution when partnership deed was not actually carried out.

Company Law:
• Salomon v. Salomon & Co.: Company is a separate legal entity distinct from its members — even a one-member company. Established the veil of incorporation principle.
• Macaura v. Northern Assurance: Policyholder must have insurable interest in the property insured.
• Foss v. Harbottle: Proper plaintiff rule — only the company can sue for wrong done to it; minority shareholders cannot ordinarily sue for wrong to the company.
• Dum Dumdum Co. v. Banwari Lal: Company's existence can only be ended by law (winding up).
• E.D. Sassoon & Co. v. ITO: Corporate veil can be pierced in tax evasion cases.
• Venkatachala Mudaliar v. Pydi Lakshmi: Company is a person but cannot be said to be a citizen.

Consumer Protection:
• Indian Hotels Co. Ltd. v. State of Kerala: Service deficiency — failure to maintain standards in hotel services.
• D.D.A. v. Trilok Singh Choudhary: Deficiency in service — delay and denial of possession of flat.

Competition Act:
• M/S Entertainment Network (India) Ltd. v. CCI: CCI has jurisdiction to examine abuse of dominant position even if market definition is contested.

14. Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 — Promissory Note, Bill of Exchange, and Cheque

Negotiable Instruments are governed by Sections 1–138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881:

Definitions:
• Negotiable Instrument (Sec. 13): Promissory note, bill of exchange, or cheque payable to bearer or order.
• Promissory Note (Sec. 4): An instrument containing an unconditional undertaking signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money only to or to the order of a specified person or to bearer.
• Bill of Exchange (Sec. 5): An instrument containing an unconditional order signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money to or to the order of a specified person or bearer.
• Cheque (Sec. 6): A bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and payable on demand.

Essential Features:
• Must be in writing and signed.
• Unconditional promise/order.
• Certain parties, certain sum, certain time.
• Payable to order or bearer.

Holder vs. Holder in Due Course:
• Holder (Sec. 8): Any person entitled in his own name to possess the instrument.
• Holder in Due Course (Sec. 9): One who holds for value, in good faith, with reasonable certainty of title, before maturity, without notice of defects.
• Advantages: Free from defects in title of prior parties, can sue all prior parties.

Dishonor of Negotiable Instruments (Sec. 100–108):
• Dishonor by non-acceptance or non-payment.
• Notice of dishonor must be given; protest where required.

Cheque Bouncing (Sec. 138):
• If a cheque is returned unpaid due to insufficient funds or exceeds arrangement, the drawer is liable. Criminal liability requires: (1) cheque presented within 3 months of issue, (2) notice within 30 days of dishonor, (3) payment not made within 15 days of receiving notice.
• Penalty: Imprisonment up to 2 years or fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.

15. Legal Formalities, Documentation, and Regulatory Compliance in Business

Statutory Compliance Checklist for Businesses:
• Company Law Compliance: Annual filings (MGT-7, AOC-4), board meeting minutes, auditor appointments, register maintenance (members, directors, charge, etc.), ROC fees.
• Income Tax Compliance: TDS/TCS deposit and returns, GST returns (GSTR-1, GSTR-3B), income tax advance tax and returns.
• Labour Law Compliance: EPFO, ESIC, PF, Gratuity, Bonus Act, Payment of Wages, Minimum Wages, Shops & Establishments Act compliance.
• Environment Compliance: Consent to establish and operate under EPA, 1986; industrial waste disposal norms.
• FEMA Compliance: Foreign exchange transactions under RBI guidelines.

Documentary Formalities:
• MOA/AOA: Constitute the charter and rules of the company — must be stamped and filed with ROC.
• Prospectus/Shelf Prospectus: Public issue documentation — truthfulness of statements critical (Sec. 34 offences).
• Debenture Trust Deed: For secured debentures, registered with ROC.
• Charges Register (Charges): Creation, modification, and satisfaction of charges must be registered with ROC within prescribed time.
• Board Resolutions: Ordinary and special resolutions — ordinary (simple majority), special (75% majority, with 21-day notice for EGM).
• Minutes: Recorded within 30 days of meeting — evidence of due process.
• Stamp Duty: Various instruments require stamp duty — undervaluation attracts penalties.

Penalties and Offences:
• Every Act prescribes penalties ranging from fines to imprisonment. For CMA Intermediate, focus on knowing the categories: minor defaults (compounding), fraud (Sec. 447 Companies Act — punishment: 1 to 10 years imprisonment + fine), misstatement in prospectus, etc.
• Sec. 447 Fraud: Includes any act, omission, concealment of material fact, or abusive act designed to gain undue advantage. Punishable with imprisonment and fine.

Important facts

  • A contract requires agreement + enforceability; minor's agreement is void ab initio (Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose)
  • Consideration must be real, lawful, and move at the desire of the promisor; past consideration is generally invalid
  • Free consent includes absence of coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, and mistake of fact
  • Quasi-contracts (Sections 68–72) are implied obligations to prevent unjust enrichment
  • Damages under Section 73 are compensatory only and governed by the foreseeability rule from Hadley v. Baxendale
  • Indemnity involves two parties; guarantee involves three parties with secondary liability
  • Bailment requires delivery of goods for a purpose with obligation to return; pledge is bailment as security for debt
  • Under Sale of Goods Act, property in goods passes when intended by parties; risk follows property
  • Unregistered partnership faces disability under Section 69 — cannot enforce contracts
  • LLP is a separate legal entity with limited liability — partners' personal assets are protected
  • Companies Act 2013 mandates at least one woman director and one resident director in India
  • Independent directors must comprise at least one-third of the board for prescribed companies
  • Section 166 imposes seven statutory duties on directors including good faith, due diligence, and avoiding conflicts
  • CSR applies to companies meeting thresholds and mandates 2% spending of average net profits
  • Consumer Protection Act 2019 establishes CCPA and provides for product liability and e-filing
  • Competition Act prohibits anti-competitive agreements and abuse of dominant position; combinations above thresholds need CCI approval
  • Negotiable Instruments Act Section 138 makes cheque bounce a criminal offence with specific procedural requirements
  • Salomon v. Salomon established that a company is a separate legal entity from its members
  • Ultra vires acts are void ab initio and cannot be ratified
  • Oppression and mismanagement remedy is available under Sections 241–242 of the Companies Act

Common mistakes

  • Confusing void agreement with voidable contract: void agreements are unenforceable from the beginning; voidable contracts are enforceable until repudiated by the aggrieved party
  • Assuming all agreements are contracts: Only agreements enforceable by law are contracts; social and domestic agreements generally are not
  • Believing partnership registration is mandatory: It is not mandatory but non-registration attracts serious disabilities under Section 69
  • Thinking a minor's agreement is voidable: It is void ab initio (Mohori Bibi case) — the minor cannot ratify upon attaining majority
  • Confusing Conditions with Warranties in Sale of Goods: Breach of condition allows rejection; breach of warranty only allows damages
  • Assuming all directors retire by rotation: MD, WTD, and Independent directors are exempt from rotation
  • Forgetting that promoter's pre-incorporation contracts are personal liabilities of promoters, not the company (Ruben v. Great Fingall Consolidated)
  • Not distinguishing between 'share capital' and 'authorized capital': Authorized is maximum; issued is what has been offered to shareholders
  • Assuming CCPA under CPA 2019 can only act on complaints: It can take suo motu action on its own motion or on referral
  • Forgetting the three-step notice procedure for Section 138 NI Act: Presentation within 3 months → notice within 30 days → payment within 15 days
  • Mistaking CSR spending from 'net profit' calculation: It is based on average net profits of the immediately preceding three financial years
  • Overlooking that ultra vires acts cannot be ratified even unanimously by shareholders
  • Not recognizing that in a partnership, every partner is an agent of the firm and other partners (mutual agency)
  • Confusing LLP with partnership on liability: In LLP, liability is limited; in partnership, partners have unlimited joint liability
  • Assuming Company is a citizen: It is a legal person but not a citizen (Venkatachala Mudaliar case)
