1. Cost Classification, Terminology and Fundamentals

Cost accounting is the process of classifying, recording, and controlling costs to assist management in decision-making. Under the CMA Intermediate syllabus, mastery of cost terminology and classification forms the foundation for every subsequent topic.

Cost Classification by Nature:
- Materials: Raw materials, packaging materials, consumable stores
- Labour: Direct labour (traceable to product), indirect labour (cannot be traced)
- Expenses: Direct expenses (charges directly to cost unit, e.g., hire charges, design costs), indirect expenses (overheads)
- Prime Cost = Direct Materials + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses
- Works/Factory Cost = Prime Cost + Factory Overheads (indirect material, indirect labour, factory rent, power, depreciation)
- Cost of Production = Works Cost + Administration Overheads
- Cost of Sales/Total Cost = Cost of Production + Selling & Distribution Overheads

Classification by Behaviour:
- Fixed Costs: Do not vary with output level in the short run (rent, salaries, insurance)
- Variable Costs: Vary proportionately with output (raw materials, direct labour, power for production)
- Semi-variable/Mixed Costs: Contain both fixed and variable components (telephone bills, salaries with commission)
- Step-fixed Costs: Fixed within a range but jump at certain output levels (supervisor salary added at certain capacity threshold)

Classification by Normality:
- Normal Cost: Expected under normal operating conditions; charged to cost of production
- Abnormal Cost: Unexpected, non-recurring; treated as period cost and excluded from valuation of inventory

Key Cost Concepts:
- Opportunity Cost: Value of the next best alternative foregone (not recorded in accounts but critical for decision-making)
- Imputed/Notional Cost: Hypothetical cost where no actual expenditure occurs (e.g., imputed interest on own capital, rental value of owned building)
- Out-of-pocket Cost: Actual cash expenditure incurred
- Sunk Cost: Past cost already incurred, irrelevant for future decisions
- Avoidable Cost: Can be eliminated if a particular activity is discontinued
- Unavoidable Cost: Will continue regardless of the decision
- Relevant Cost: Future cost that differs among alternatives; used in decision-making
- Marginal Cost: Variable cost of producing one additional unit
- Lifecycle Cost: Total cost from R&D through production to disposal

Cost Units and Cost Centres:
- Cost Unit: Unit of product/service to which costs are ascribed (unit, batch, set, dozen, kilometre, tonne, etc.)
- Cost Centre: Location, person, equipment, or department where costs are accumulated before allocation

ABC Classification of Inventory (for Materials):
- A items: High value, low quantity — tight control, frequent review, 70-80% of value
- B items: Moderate value and quantity — moderate control, 15-20% of value
- C items: Low value, high quantity — loose control, 5-10% of value

2. Material Cost Management

Material often constitutes 50-60% of total cost in manufacturing concerns. Efficient material cost management covers procurement, storage, issue, and inventory control.

Purchase Price Determination Methods:
- Economic Order Quantity (EOQ): The lot size that minimises total ordering and carrying costs.
EOQ = √(2 × D × S / C)
Where: D = Annual demand (units), S = Ordering cost per order, C = Carrying/holding cost per unit per annum
At EOQ, total ordering cost equals total carrying cost.

- Total Annual Cost at EOQ = Purchase cost + Ordering cost + Carrying cost
= D × P + (D/EOQ) × S + (EOQ/2) × C

Lead Time and Reorder Level:
- Reorder Level (ROL) = Maximum Usage × Maximum Lead Time
- Minimum Stock Level = ROL − (Normal Usage × Normal Lead Time)
- Maximum Stock Level = ROL + EOQ − (Minimum Usage × Minimum Lead Time)
Or: Maximum Stock Level = Reorder Level + EOQ − (Normal Consumption × Normal Lead Time)
- Danger Stock Level = Minimum Stock Level − (Average Usage × Average Lead Time)
- Average Stock Level = (Min Stock + Max Stock)/2 or (Min Stock + EOQ/2)

Inventory Valuation Methods:
- FIFO (First-In-First-Out): Opening stock and earliest purchases issued first; closing stock valued at latest prices; results in higher profit during inflation
- LIFO (Last-In-First-Out): Latest purchases issued first; closing stock valued at oldest prices
- Weighted Average Cost: Total cost of materials available ÷ Total units available
- Specific Identification Method: Used for unique/high-value items
- Standard Costing Approach: All issues valued at pre-determined standard price

Discounts on Purchases:
- Trade Discount: Not recorded in accounts; purchase price is net of trade discount
- Cash/Sales Discount: Treated as finance income or deducted from purchase cost depending on accounting policy
- Quantity Discount: May justify larger orders even if it raises carrying costs — evaluate using incremental analysis

Material Loss Handling:
- Normal Waste/Loss: Incidental to production; increase unit cost of good output; included in cost of production
- Abnormal Waste/Loss: Beyond normal expectation; excluded from cost of production; written off to Costing P&L
- Yield: Actual output obtained from given input; Yield % = (Actual output/Input) × 100
- Loss on abnormal waste = Cost per unit of normal yield × Abnormal loss quantity

Control Techniques:
- ABC Analysis (already covered in Section 1)
- VED Analysis: Vital, Essential, Desirable — based on criticality
- FSN Analysis: Fast-moving, Slow-moving, Non-moving
- HML Analysis: High, Medium, Low value
- SDE Analysis: Scarce, Difficult to obtain, Easily available
- Inspection: Incoming inspection, In-process inspection, Final inspection

3. Labour Cost Management

Labour costing involves measurement, allocation, and control of personnel costs. Under CMA Intermediate, the focus is on calculation of labour cost, incentive schemes, and idle/over-time treatment.

Remuneration Systems:
- Time Rate System: Wages paid based on time worked; simple but does not incentivise productivity
- Piece Rate System: Wages proportional to output produced
Types: Straight piece rate, Taylor's differential piece rate (two rates: 80% for below standard, 120% for above), Rowan Plan, Halsey Plan, Halsey-Weir Plan

Halsey Plan:
Wage = (Time taken × Rate per hour) + 50% × (Time saved × Rate per hour)
= Time Rate + 0.5 × Time Saved × Hourly Rate

Rowan Plan:
Wage = (Time taken × Rate per hour) + (Time saved/Standard time) × (Time taken × Rate per hour)
= Time Rate + (Time Saved / Standard Time) × Time Taken × Rate

Comparison: Rowan Plan provides a gradual increase in wages as efficiency rises, whereas Halsey gives a flat 50% bonus. Both reward savings but cap the bonus differently.

Incentive Scheme Evaluation:
Always calculate earnings under each scheme for given data and determine which yields highest worker remuneration at different efficiency levels. Key question type in CMA exams.

Calculation of Labour Cost Components:
- Gross Wages = Basic wages + Dearness allowance + Overtime wages + Bonus
- Less: Deductions (PF, ESI, Income tax, Deductions for idle time, etc.)
- Net Wages/Gross Earnings = payable amount
- Cost to firm = Gross wages + Employer's PF contribution + Employer's ESI contribution + Gratuity provision + Other statutory benefits

Overtime Handling:
- Overtime Premium treated as overhead (not charged directly to job unless specifically directed)
- If overtime is incurred due to specific customer order: charged directly to that order
- If overtime is due to general increased workload: treated as factory/production overhead
- Ordinary overtime wages (regular work within normal time) = direct labour cost

Idle Time and Idle Time Cost:
- Idle Time = Period when payment is made but no work is done (power failure, strike, weather, machine breakdown)
- Normal Idle Time: Included in overheads (charged to all jobs proportionally)
- Abnormal Idle Time: Charged to Costing P&L Account
- Idle Time Cost = Hours × Rate per hour
- Rate of Idle Time = (Idle Time / Total Time Worked) × 100 or = (Idle Time Cost / Total Labour Cost) × 100

Labour Turnover:
- Cause: Personal, Domestic, Organisational, Economic, Accidental factors
- Methods of Calculation:
(a) Separation Method: (Number of separations during period / Average number of workers) × 100
(b) Replacement Method: (Number of replacements / Average number of workers) × 100
(c) Flux Method: ((Separations + Exits) / 2 × Average number of workers) × 100
Or equivalently: (Separations + Exits) / (2 × Average workers) × 100
- Cost of Turnover = Cost of hiring + Cost of training + Cost of separation + Cost of lost production + Cost of reduced productivity

Absence of Records — Retrospective Calculation:
- When time records are missing, allocate overheads proportionally or use standard percentages from previous periods.
- For overtime: apportion between direct jobs and overhead based on causative factors.

4. Direct Expenses and Overhead Management

Overhead management is central to cost accounting. The CMA Intermediate level requires understanding of classification, allocation, apportionment, absorption, and under/over-absorption.

Classification of Overheads:
- Factory/Works Overheads: Indirect materials, indirect labour, factory rent, power, depreciation of plant, maintenance
- Administration Overheads: Office rent, salaries, stationery, auditing fees, depreciation of office assets
- Selling Overheads: Advertising, sales commissions, delivery van expenses, trade subscriptions
- Distribution Overheads: Warehouse rent, carriage outwards, loading/unloading, packing for dispatch
- Research & Development Overheads: R&D personnel, experimentation costs

Overhead Recovery Basis Selection:
- Material Cost: % on Material Cost (suitable when material is dominant cost element)
- Material Consumed: % on Material Consumed (more accurate than on purchase)
- Direct Labour Cost: % on Direct Labour Cost (suitable when labour-intensive)
- Direct Labour Hours: Per hour rate (when labour is homogeneous)
- Machine Hours: Per machine hour (when machine-intensive — most appropriate for automated processes)
- Prime Cost: % on Prime Cost
- Works Output: Per unit of output
- Sales Value: % on Sales Value (for selling/distribution overhead recovery)

Allocation vs Apportionment:
- Allocation: Directly assigning entire cost item to one cost centre (e.g., salary of factory supervisor allocated to factory)
- Apportionment: Distributing common cost among multiple cost centres on equitable basis
Common apportionment bases:
- Rent/Rates/Insurance: Floor area, cubic content
- Power: Horsepower of machines, meter readings
- Lighting: Number of lamps, floor area
- Depreciation: Book value of assets, machine hours
- Supervision: Number of workers, labour cost
- Plant & Equipment: Value of assets, machine hours
- Canteen/Transport: Number of employees
- Interest on Capital: Value of assets employed

Multi-Stage Overhead Recovery:
When service departments provide support to production departments, overheads must be reapportioned:
- Direct Method: Allocate service department costs directly to production departments (ignores inter-service department usage)
- Step-Down (Sequential) Method: Allocate service dept costs sequentially starting from the dept providing maximum service to others
- Reciprocal Method (Simultaneous Equations): Accounts for mutual services between service departments

Absorption Rate Calculation:
- Traditional/Simple Absorption Rate = Estimated Overheads / Estimated Absorption Base
- ABC Rate = Estimated Overhead for Activity / Estimated Activity Driver Units
- Machine Hour Rate includes: Depreciation, Power, Supervisor salary, Repair & maintenance per hour of machine use
Machine Hour Rate = (Total Machine-related Overheads) / (Total Machine Hours)

Under and Over Absorption:
- Under-absorption = Actual Overheads − Absorbed Overheads (when absorbed < actual)
- Over-absorption = Absorbed Overheads − Actual Overheads (when absorbed > actual)
- Causes: Incorrect estimation of overheads, change in activity level, seasonal variations, abnormal idle time
- Treatment:
- Small amount: Transfer to Costing P&L Account
- Large amount due to normal causes: Prorate to WIP, Work-in-Process, Finished Goods, and Cost of Goods Sold
- Large amount due to abnormal causes: Transfer only the abnormal portion to Costing P&L

Cost to Completion % Method:
For work-in-progress valuation:
Equivalent units in WIP = Physical units × % of completion
For material, conversion, and overhead may have different completion percentages.

5. Cost Sheet Preparation and Cost Audit

A Cost Sheet is a statement showing the composition and ascertainment of cost of products. Cost Audit examines the accuracy of cost records and compliance with cost accounting standards.

Structure of Cost Sheet (as per Cost Accounting Standards and ICMAI format):
1. Prime Cost
+ Factory/Works Overheads
= Works/Factory Cost
+ Administrative Overheads
= Cost of Production
+ Selling & Distribution Overheads
= Cost of Sales
+ Profit (added to arrive at Sales)
= Sales

Detailed Sub-headings Required:
- Opening Stock of Raw Materials
- Purchases of Raw Materials (less: returns to suppliers, discount)
- Carriage Inwards, Import Duties, Freight & Clearance
- Opening Stock of Work-in-Progress
- Closing Stock of Raw Materials
- Closing Stock of Work-in-Progress
- Opening & Closing Stock of Finished Goods
- Direct Charges, Hire Charges, Design Charges
- Consumption of Raw Materials = Opening RM + Purchases − Closing RM
- Component/Part Stock adjustments where applicable
- Wages (Gross, less: overtime premium if separated)
- Royalties on production/sales
- Contract site expenses

Reconciliation of Cost and Financial Accounts:
Profits under cost accounts and financial accounts differ due to:
- Items in financial accounts but not in cost accounts: Rent received, interest earned, profit on sale of assets, dividends, bad debt written off, depreciation differences
- Items in cost accounts but not in financial accounts: Over/under-absorption of overheads, depreciation as per cost accounting standards, interest on capital, goodwill amortisation
- Different valuation of stock (FIFO vs weighted average)
- Different depreciation methods/policies
- Abnormal losses handled differently
- Personal expenses included in financial accounts

Treatment: Start with one profit figure and adjust to arrive at the other. Debit/Credit adjustments follow the rule: if item increases financial profit but not cost profit, subtract from financial profit to get cost profit.

Cost Audit — Key Provisions (Companies Act, 2013 and Rules):
- Cost Audit required for specified industries/companies as per Rule 6 of Companies (Cost Records and Audit) Rules
- Cost Audit Report: Form CAR-3, filed within 30 days of appointment of cost auditor
- Cost Auditor's Report: Form CAR-4
- Eligibility: Only practising cost accountants in practice
- Remuneration: As approved by Board/General Meeting
- Key areas examined:
(a) Maintenance of cost records as required
(b) Accuracy of cost records and cost accounting
(c) Compliance with Cost Accounting Standards (CAS)
(d) Internal control systems
(e) Efficiency of operations from cost perspective
(f) Proper allocation of costs

Cost Accounting Standards (CAS): Issued by ICMAI, mandatory compliance. Key CAS applicable:
- CAS 1: Classification of Cost
- CAS 2: Capacity Preparation and Utilization
- CAS 3: Capital Cost
- CAS 4: Project Evaluation
- CAS 5: Capacity Costs
- CAS 6: Product Cost
- CAS 8: Computation of Working Capital Requirements
- CAS 10: Valuation of Inventories
- CAS 11: Valuation of Work-in-Progress
- CAS 12: Valuation of Scrap, Spoilage, Rework, Sale of By-products
- CAS 14: Depreciation and Depletion
- CAS 15: Employee Benefits
- CAS 20: Measurement of Environmental Costs
- CAS 21: Carbon Emissions

6. Job, Batch and Contract Costing

Job, batch and contract costing are used for heterogeneous products/services produced to customer specifications.

Job Costing:
- Used when work is executed against specific customer orders/jobs
- Each job is treated as a separate cost unit
- Job Cost Card is maintained for each job
- All direct costs (material, labour, expenses) are charged directly; overheads are applied on an appropriate basis
- When a job is incomplete at year-end: WIP is valued at actual cost or cost plus profit depending on the degree of completion
- Escrow/Retention Money: A percentage withheld by the buyer; should be disclosed appropriately
- Profit Reservation: For incomplete contracts nearing completion, only a portion of profit may be transferred to P&L (as per Companies Act provisions)

Batch Costing:
- Used when identical items are produced in batches/lots
- Cost per batch ÷ Number of units in batch = Cost per unit
- Applicable in pharmaceuticals, biscuits, spare parts, etc.

Contract Costing (Long-term Contracts):
- Used for construction contracts (buildings, roads, bridges, ships) spanning more than one accounting period
- Work Certified vs Work Uncertified:
- Work Certified: Approved by the engineer/client; valued at contract price (cost plus agreed profit) or lower of cost and market value
- Work Uncertified: Completed but not yet approved; valued at cost only (conservative approach — no profit recognition)
- Material on Site: Should be valued at cost or market price, whichever is lower
- Calculation of Profit to Date:
- Contract Account is opened for each contract
- All costs incurred (direct and indirect) are debited; revenue recognized is credited
- For incomplete contracts, profit computation depends on stage of completion and regulatory requirements

Profit Reservation on Incomplete Contracts (Companies Act, 2013 — Section 198 and Rule 2(1)(ca)):
When work certified is less than 25% of contract price: No profit should be taken to P&L; full estimated profit should be kept in reserve.
When work certified is 25% or more but contract incomplete:
Profit to Reserve = Total Estimated Profit × (Work Certified / Contract Price) × (CWF / CWF + WUC + Mat on Site)
Where CWF = Cost of Work Certified, WUC = Cost of Work Uncertified, Mat on Site = Material on site
Alternatively: Profit to P&L = Total Estimated Profit − Profit to Reserve
- Loss on Contract: If estimated loss is anticipated, full loss must be provided immediately regardless of stage of completion.
- Contract Completed in Full: Full profit is recognized; balance in Contract Account transferred to P&L.

7. Process Costing

Process costing is used where production involves continuous steps/processes and output is homogeneous. Each process is treated as a separate cost unit.

Basic Features:
- Production goes through successive processes
- Each process produces an output which may be further processed or sold
- Average cost per unit is computed for each process
- Equivalent production concept applies when WIP exists
- Joint products, by-products, and waste need specific treatment

Equivalent Production (Method of Valuation):
- Material: Usually added at the beginning of the process; hence 100% complete for material even if physically incomplete
- Conversion Cost (Labour + Overheads): Added uniformly; based on % completion
- Two methods:
(a) Opening WIP Included in Equivalent Units: Equivalent Units = Units Completed + Closing WIP (% complete) + Opening WIP (100%)
(b) Opening WIP Excluded: Equivalent Units = Units Completed + Closing WIP (% complete) − Opening WIP (from previous period, already accounted for)
- FIFO Method: Assumption that opening WIP is completed first; separates current period costs from prior period costs
Current Period Equivalent Units = (Opening WIP × % remaining) + Units Started and Completed + (Closing WIP × % complete)
- Weighted Average Method: Combines opening WIP with current production; simpler but less accurate for cost control

Output Categories:
- Normal Loss: Inevitable loss; no cost charged; reduces output; increases cost per unit of good output
Cost per unit = (Total Process Cost − Scrap Value of Normal Loss) / Normal Output
- Abnormal Loss: Unexpected loss; valued at full cost per unit; transferred to Costing P&L
Units of Abnormal Loss = Actual Output − Normal Output
- Abnormal Gain: When actual output exceeds normal output; valued at cost per unit; transferred to Costing P&L as gain
- Spoilage: Corrective action depends on whether normal or abnormal
- Scrap Value of Normal Loss is deducted from process cost; scrap value of Abnormal Loss is credited separately

Joint Products, By-Products and Waste:
- Joint Products: Two or more products of substantial value arising from same process; common costs apportioned using:
(a) Physical Units Method: Based on quantity/weight/volume
(b) Sales Value at Split-off Method: Based on individual sales values at the point of separation
(c) Net Realisable Value (NRV) Method: Sales value less post-split-off processing costs — most preferred
(d) Contribution Method
(e) Average Unit Cost Method
- By-Products: Incidental products with relatively low value; method of treatment:
(a) Sales Value Method: By-product sales value deducted from joint cost; balance apportioned to main products
(b) NRV Method: Similar but using NRVs
(c) Average Unit Cost Method: By-product valued at average cost of main products
- Waste: Usually has minimal scrap value; deducted from process cost

Inter-process Profits:
When transfer between processes includes profit, elimination of inter-process profit is required for correct valuing of closing WIP and finished goods. Two methods:
- Pro-forma Method (Virtual Process Method)
- Adjustment in Closing WIP Method

8. Marginal Costing and Break-even Analysis

Marginal Costing is based on the distinction between fixed and variable costs. It shows the effect on profit of changes in volume of output.

Fundamental Concepts:
- Contribution = Sales − Variable Cost
- Also: Contribution = Fixed Cost + Profit
- P/V Ratio (Profit-Volume Ratio) = (Contribution / Sales) × 100
Or: (Change in Contribution / Change in Sales) × 100
Or: (Fixed Cost + Profit) / Sales × 100
- Break-even Point (BEP) = Fixed Cost / P/V Ratio (in value)
BEP (in units) = Fixed Cost / Contribution per unit
- Margin of Safety (MOS) = Actual Sales − Break-even Sales
MOS (%) = (MOS / Actual Sales) × 100
- Target Profit Sales = (Fixed Cost + Target Profit) / P/V Ratio
- Target Profit Units = (Fixed Cost + Target Profit) / Contribution per unit

Assumptions of Marginal Costing:
- Costs can be strictly classified as fixed or variable
- Variable cost per unit remains constant; total variable cost varies proportionately
- Fixed cost remains constant within relevant range
- Selling price remains constant
- Volume is the sole factor affecting costs and revenues
- Production equals sales (no change in inventory)

Limitations:
- Difficulty in strict classification of semi-variable costs
- Fixed costs may not remain constant over long periods
- Ignores time value of money
- Not suitable for long-term decisions
- Assumes linear relationship between cost and volume

Applications:
- Make or Buy Decision: Compare cost of making (variable + avoidable fixed) vs purchase price; consider qualitative factors
- Shut Down Decision: Continue only if Contribution > Avoidable Fixed Costs (i.e., contribution covers some fixed costs)
- Product Mix Decision: When a limiting factor exists, rank products by contribution per unit of limiting factor
- Drop/Continue a Product Line: Compare contribution lost with fixed costs saved
- Acceptance of Special Order: Accept if special price > variable cost (assuming excess capacity and no effect on regular sales)
- Price Determination: Minimum price = Variable Cost + Avoidable Fixed Cost per unit; any price above this contributes to fixed costs

Break-even Chart and Profit-Volume Graph:
- BEP Chart: Shows sales, total cost, fixed cost lines intersecting at BEP; area below BEP is loss, above is profit
- P-V Chart: More useful; directly shows profit/loss at different activity levels; slope = P/V ratio
Profit = (Contribution per unit × Units) − Fixed Cost

9. Standard Costing and Variance Analysis

Standard costing involves pre-determining costs and comparing them with actual costs to compute variances for managerial control.

Nature and Objectives:
- Standards are scientifically set pre-costs for items of cost
- Objectives: Cost control, cost reduction, performance evaluation, budgetary control, motivator for improvement
- Difference between Standard Costing and Budgetary Control: Budget covers total planned cost for a period; standard cost focuses on unit cost with variance analysis for control

Types of Standards:
- Ideal Standard: Best possible performance under perfect conditions; no allowance for waste, breakdowns
- Normal Standard: Based on normal conditions, average efficiency; allows for normal wastage
- Current Standard: Set for current period conditions; reflects current prices and efficiency
- Basic/Constant Standard: Long-term standard used as base; not updated frequently

Setting Up Standard Costs:
- Material Standard: Standard price per unit × Standard quantity per unit of output
- Labour Standard: Standard rate per hour × Standard time per unit
- Overhead Standard: Based on normal capacity or budgeted activity

Material Variances:
- Material Cost Variance (MCV) = (Standard Cost of Actual Output) − (Actual Cost)
MCV = SQ × SP − AQ × AP = (SQ − AQ) × SP + AQ × (SP − AP)
- Material Price Variance (MPV) = AQ × (SP − AP)
AQ = Actual Quantity purchased or consumed; compute at purchase or consumption point consistently
If at purchase: MPV = (SP − AP) × Quantity Purchased
If at consumption: MPV = (SP − AP) × Quantity Consumed
- Material Usage Variance (MUV) = SP × (SQ − AQ consumed)
- Material Yield Variance (MYV) = SP × (Actual Yield − Standard Yield from Actual Input)
Or: MYV = Standard Cost of Actual Output − Standard Cost of Actual Input
- Material Mix Variance (MMV) = Standard Cost of Actual Mix − Standard Cost of Ideal Mix
MMV = Σ[Actual Mix Quantity × (Standard Mix Price − Blended Standard Price)]

Labour Variances:
- Labour Cost Variance (LCV) = (Standard Cost of Actual Output) − (Actual Cost)
- Labour Rate Variance (LRV) = AH × (SR − AR)
- Labour Efficiency Variance (LEV) = SR × (SH − AH)
- Labour Mix Variance (LMV) = Standard Cost of Actual Mix − Standard Cost of Ideal Mix
- Labour Yield Variance (LYV) = Standard Rate × (Actual Output − Standard Output from Actual Input)
- Idle Time Variance = Standard Rate × Idle Hours

Overhead Variances:
- Total Overhead Variance = (Standard Overhead for Actual Output) − (Actual Overhead)
- For Variable Overhead:
VOH Cost Variance = VOH Efficiency Variance + VOH Spend Variance
VOH Efficiency Variance = SR × (SH − AH)
VOH Spend Variance = AH × (SR − AR) or = Actual VOH − (AH × SR)
- For Fixed Overhead:
FOH Cost Variance = FOH Volume Variance + FOH Spend Variance
FOH Volume Variance = FOH Capacity Variance + FOH Utilization Variance + FOH Efficiency Variance
FOH Volume Variance = Standard FOH Rate × (Actual Output − Budgeted Output)
FOH Capacity Variance = Standard FOH Rate × (Actual Hours − Budgeted Hours)
FOH Efficiency Variance = Standard FOH Rate × (Standard Hours for Actual Output − Actual Hours)
FOH Spend Variance = Budgeted FOH − Actual FOH

Sales Variance:
- Sales Value Variance = (Standard Selling Price − Actual Selling Price) × Actual Quantity + Standard Price × (Budgeted Quantity − Actual Quantity)
- Sales Price Variance = Actual Quantity × (Standard Price − Actual Price)
- Sales Volume Variance = Standard Profit per Unit × (Budgeted Units − Actual Units)

10. Activity Based Costing (ABC)

Activity Based Costing (ABC) is a method that assigns overhead costs to products based on the activities they require, rather than using a single plant-wide overhead rate.

Core Principle: Products consume activities, and activities consume resources. ABC traces costs from resources → activities → cost objects.

Steps in ABC:
1. Identify major activities performed in the organization (e.g., machine setup, quality inspection, material handling, order processing, procurement)
2. Assign resource costs to each activity to create cost pools
3. Identify cost drivers for each activity (the factor that causes the cost to change)
4. Calculate Activity Rate = Total Cost of Activity Pool / Total Quantity of Cost Driver
5. Assign costs to products: Product Cost = Σ(Activity Rate × Activity Consumption by Product)

Advantages of ABC:
- More accurate product costing, especially in diverse product lines
- Identifies non-value-adding activities
- Helps in pricing, product mix, and outsourcing decisions
- Reveals true profitability of products/customers
- Better overhead absorption than traditional methods

Limitations of ABC:
- Complex and costly to implement and maintain
- Subjective selection of activities and cost drivers
- May not be justified for simple product ranges
- Time-consuming data collection
- Not widely accepted for external financial reporting under IFRS/Ind AS

Comparison with Traditional Costing:
| Aspect | Traditional | ABC |
|---|---|---|
| Cost allocation | Single/plural volume-based rate | Multiple activity rates |
| Cost driver | Direct labour hours, machine hours | Various: setups, inspections, orders |
| Accuracy | Less accurate for diverse products | More accurate |
| Complexity | Simple | Complex |
| Product cost | Often over/under-costed | Relatively accurate |

Critical Analysis for Decision-Making:
ABC typically reveals that high-volume, simple products are over-costed under traditional methods while low-volume, complex products are under-costed. This reversal has significant implications for pricing, product discontinuation, and customer profitability analysis.

Cost Driver Selection Criteria:
- Causal relationship with cost incurrence
- Measurability and availability of data
- Cost-benefit feasibility
- Reasonableness and understandability by management

11. Budgetary Control and Flexible Budgeting

Budgetary control is the establishment of budgets relating to the responsibilities of executives and the rendering of comparative results to determine propriety and efficacy of executive duties.

Definitions:
- Budget: A quantitative expression of a plan for a defined period of time
- Budgetary Control: The process of creating budgets, coordinating activities, comparing actual performance with budgeted targets, and taking corrective action
- Fixed Budget: Prepared for a single level of activity; does not change with actual output
- Flexible Budget: Adjusted to different levels of activity; distinguishes between fixed and variable elements

Types of Budgets:
- Operating Budgets: Sales budget, Production budget, Material budget, Labour budget, Overhead budget, ending finished goods budget
- Financial Budgets: Cash budget, Capital budget, Budgeted Balance Sheet
- Standing Budget: Continues to operate throughout the year without revision
- Self-imposed (Participative) Budget: Prepared with participation of those who will be responsible for achieving it
- Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB): Every expense must be justified from scratch; no reference to previous budget
- Rolling Budget: Continuously updated by adding a new period as the current period expires
- Master Budget: Comprehensive budget incorporating all operating and financial budgets
- Incremental Budget: Prepared by adjusting the previous period's budget for expected changes

Cash Budget — Methods of Preparation:
1. Receipts and Payments Method (Direct Method):
Closing Balance = Opening Balance + Total Receipts − Total Payments
Useful for short periods and cash flow forecasting
2. Adjusted Net Income Method (Indirect Method):
Cash Flow from Operations = Net Income + Non-cash Expenses − Non-cash Income ± Changes in Working Capital
Plus Cash Flow from Investing Activities and Financing Activities

Cash Budget Components:
Receipts: Cash sales, Credit collections, Debtors collection schedule, Receipts from loans, Dividend/income, Sale of assets, Tax refunds
Payments: Cash purchases, Credit payments to creditors, Wages, Overheads, Tax payments, Dividend paid, Loan repayments, Purchase of assets, Capital expenditure

Flexible Budget Formula:
Flexible Budget Cost = Fixed Cost + (Variable Cost per Unit × Actual Output)
Or if variable cost for standard output is known:
Flexible Budget at Actual Output = Fixed Cost + (Variable Cost for Standard Output / Standard Output) × Actual Output

Variance Analysis under Budgetary Control:
- Budget Variance = Actual Cost − Budgeted Cost (at actual level of activity)
- For flexible budgeting, compare actual costs with flexible budgeted costs for the actual output level
- The key difference from standard costing is that budgetary control focuses on overall budget adherence while standard costing focuses on unit cost variances

12. Cost Audit Procedures and Reporting

Cost audit is an independent examination of cost accounting records and systems to verify correctness, completeness, and compliance with cost accounting standards and relevant regulations.

Scope and Procedure of Cost Audit:
- Verification of cost accounting records: Check that records are maintained as prescribed under the Companies (Cost Records and Audit) Rules
- Verification of cost statements: Cross-check cost sheet figures with financial accounts and reconciliation statements
- Verification of physical existence: Stock verification, WIP verification, fixed asset verification
- Verification of valuation methods: Consistency in inventory valuation, depreciation, WIP valuation
- Verification of allocation and apportionment bases: Reasonableness of bases used
- Verification of overhead absorption rates: Appropriateness and consistency
- Verification of standard costs and variance analysis: Adequacy of standards, reasonableness of variances
- Review of internal controls: Adequacy of cost control systems, documentation, authorizations
- Verification of compliance with Cost Accounting Standards (CAS)
- Review of sustainability/environmental cost disclosures if applicable

Checklist for Cost Auditor:
1. Confirmation of appointment and acceptance
2. Understanding of business and industry
3. Assessment of risk and materiality
4. Review of cost accounting policies
5. Testing of transactions and balances
6. Verification of stock and WIP
7. Confirmation of payables and receivables
8. Review of contracts and agreements
9. Testing of overhead allocation
10. Review of related party transactions
11. Verification of statutory compliance
12. Documentation of findings and conclusions

Cost Audit Report Format (CAR-3):
The report should contain:
- Part A: General Information about the company
- Part B: Compliance with Cost Accounting Standards
- Part C: Specific observations and recommendations
- Annexures for detailed findings

Key Areas of Examination:
- Proper maintenance of cost records
- Correct determination of cost of production/sales
- Adequacy of internal financial controls
- Correct treatment of joint products, by-products, waste, scrap
- Correct computation of depreciation as per CAS-14
- Correct treatment of employee benefits as per CAS-15
- Proper disclosure of environmental costs as per CAS-20
- Correct valuation of inventories as per CAS-10

Difference between Cost Audit and Financial Audit:
| Aspect | Cost Audit | Financial Audit |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Cost records and cost accounting | Financial statements |
| Objective | Verify cost data accuracy, compliance with CAS | True and fair view of financial position |
| Standard followed | CAS, Cost Audit Rules | GAAP, Ind AS, Auditing Standards |
| Scope | Cost-specific aspects | Entire financial system |
| Report to | Board + ROC + Central Govt | Shareholders |
| Auditor | Practicing Cost Accountant | Chartered Accountant |

Important facts

  • Prime Cost = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses; Works Cost adds Factory Overheads; Cost of Production adds Admin Overheads; Cost of Sales adds S&D Overheads.
  • EOQ = √(2DS/C) minimizes total ordering and carrying costs; at EOQ, ordering cost equals carrying cost.
  • ROL = Maximum Usage × Maximum Lead Time; Minimum Stock = ROL − Normal Usage × Normal Lead Time.
  • Halsey Plan: Wage = Time Rate + 50% × Time Saved × Rate; Rowan Plan: Wage = Time Rate + (Time Saved/Std Time) × Time Taken × Rate.
  • Abnormal loss is excluded from cost of production and written off to Costing P&L; normal loss increases unit cost of good output.
  • Under/Over-absorption = Actual Overheads − Applied Overheads; small amounts go to Costing P&L, large amounts may be prorated.
  • For incomplete contracts: if work certified < 25%, retain full estimated profit; if ≥ 25%, apply reserve formula.
  • Loss on contract: full estimated loss must be provided immediately regardless of stage of completion.
  • Equivalent production (FIFO): includes work remaining on opening WIP + started & completed + work done on closing WIP.
  • Joint products cost apportionment: NRV method is generally preferred over physical units or sales value methods.
  • Contribution = Sales − Variable Cost; P/V Ratio = Contribution/Sales × 100; BEP (units) = FC/CPV; BEP (value) = FC/PV Ratio.
  • Margin of Safety = Actual Sales − BEP Sales; MOS (%) = MOS/Actual Sales × 100.
  • Material Cost Variance = SQ×SP − AQ×AP; split into Price Variance (AQ×(SP−AP)) and Usage Variance (SP×(SQ−AQ)).
  • Labour Cost Variance = SH×SR − AH×AR; split into Rate Variance (AH×(SR−AR)) and Efficiency Variance (SR×(SH−AH)).
  • FOH Volume Variance = Std Rate × (Actual Output − Budgeted Output); FOH Spend Variance = Budgeted FOH − Actual FOH.
  • ABC assigns costs via Activity Cost Pool → Activity Rate → Cost Object; more accurate for diverse product lines.
  • Flexible Budget = Fixed Cost + (Variable Cost per Unit × Actual Output); adapts budget to actual activity level.
  • CAS 10 covers inventory valuation, CAS 14 covers depreciation, CAS 15 covers employee benefits, CAS 20 covers environmental costs.
  • Cost Audit under Companies Act: CAR-3 is the report format; CAR-4 for appointment; only practicing CAs can conduct cost audit.
  • Reciprocal method for inter-service department cost allocation uses simultaneous equations; Step-down allocates sequentially; Direct ignores inter-service.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing MPV at purchase vs consumption point — must be consistent; if purchase quantity ≠ consumption, state clearly which is used.
  • Treating overtime premium as direct labour always — only charge directly when specifically caused by a particular job/order; otherwise treat as overhead.
  • Including abnormal loss in cost of production — abnormal loss must be excluded from cost sheets and written off to Costing P&L.
  • Taking profit on incomplete contract without applying reserve formula — for work certified < 25%, NO profit to P&L; profit reservation formula applies otherwise.
  • Providing full loss on estimated contract loss only when contract is complete — estimated total loss must be provided immediately in the period it is identified.
  • Using total overheads instead of fixed overheads when computing FOH variances — separate variable and fixed components carefully.
  • Calculating P/V Ratio using total cost instead of contribution — P/V Ratio = Contribution/Sales, NOT (Sales−Variable Cost)/Sales with wrong variable cost identification.
  • Applying equivalent production for material as same as conversion — material is often 100% complete even when WIP is only partially complete for conversion.
  • Ignoring scrap value of normal loss in process costing — it reduces the cost to be distributed over good output.
  • Computing flexed budget using only variable cost — must add fixed cost (which remains unchanged) to get total flexible budget.
  • Confusing Labour Rate Variance sign convention — positive = adverse when AR > SR; negative = favourable when AR < SR. State explicitly.
  • Apportioning joint costs by physical units when products have vastly different values — NRV method is more appropriate when market prices differ significantly.
  • Treating imputed cost as actual cost in financial accounts — imputed/notional costs are not recorded in financial accounts but are relevant for cost accounts and decision-making.
  • For inter-process profit elimination: forgetting to eliminate unrealized profit in closing WIP — only profit on certified/sold portion is realized; unrealized portion must be removed.
  • Calculating Material Mix Variance without considering ideal mix — must compare actual proportions against standard/budgeted proportions.
  • In Cash Budget: ignoring opening balance or forgetting that closing balance of one month becomes opening of next — sequential linking is essential.
