Definitions

  • Contract: An agreement enforceable by law. Every agreement becomes a contract only when it satisfies the essentials of a valid contract under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.
  • Void Agreement: An agreement not enforceable by law. It may be void from the beginning (nullity) or become void subsequently due to supervening events or statutory prohibitions.
  • Contingent Contract: A contract to do or not to do something, if some event collateral to the contract and uncertain happens or does not happen.
  • Bailment: Delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them.
  • Agreement vs Contract: All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts. An agreement becomes a contract only when enforceable by law after satisfying all legal essentials.
  • Undertaking: A forward-looking statement of fact or assurance given by a director or promoter regarding the fairness of prospectus statements and compliance with legal requirements.

Important facts

  • Limitation period for filing suit on a contract is three years from when the contract becomes enforceable. — High-yield numerical fact for exam application.
  • Notice of dishonour need not be given when acceptance or payment is expressly waived, acceptor is drawee bound to accept, drawer is liable party, or party has waived notice expressly or impliedly. — Commonly tested exception in instrument law.
  • Cheque dated is valid only for three months from date of issue; thereafter bank must refuse payment. — Standard banking practice frequently examined.
  • Conditional sale where property passes only on fulfillment of condition treats transaction as hire-purchase until condition met. — Distinction critical for GST and accounting treatment.
  • Minor's agreement is void ab initio; no estoppel, restitution, or ratification applies against minor. — Absolute principle consistently examined.
  • Doctrine of Ulterior Purpose cannot be used to circumvent statutory prohibition on agreements. — Advanced contract law concept tested at CMA level.
  • Insolvency of customer qualifies as insurable interest for insurance of receivables under contract principles. — Intersection of contract and insurance law.
  • Factor holding goods by reason of previous sale may sell back to buyer only if buyer has no notice of prior disposition. — Protection of innocent third parties in sales.

Memory aids

  • Use COFOLEC to recall contract essentials: Capacity, Offer/Acceptance, Free Consent, Objective/Lawful Consideration, Lawful Object, Legal Formalities, Enforceability.
  • Remember VOID categories as MART: Marriage restraint, Agreement without consideration, Restraint of trade, Uncertain terms.
  • Negotiable instruments triad: Note (promise), Bill (order to pay), Cheque (demand instrument).
  • Limitation periods mnemonic: 'Three Years Contract, Twelve Months Cheque' — suit on contract 3 years, cheque dishonour suit 2 years but cheque validity 3 months.
  • Co-extensive liability means surety stands in shoes of principal debtor — creditor can sue either or both simultaneously.
  • Ultra Vires acts are void not merely irregular — compare intra vides which are merely procedural defects.

Exam tips

  • Always distinguish between condition and warranty in problem-solving — read whether the stipulation goes to root of contract or is merely collateral.
  • When dealing with guarantee questions, identify the three parties and trace liability sequence before calculating quantum.
  • In limitation problems, calculate from date when right to sue accrues, not from date of contract itself.
  • For agency questions, check whether agent had actual authority, apparent authority, or ratification basis before concluding on third-party rights.
  • Ethics questions require professional attitude answers grounded in code of conduct rather than general moral opinions.
  • In company law questions, apply ultra vires doctrine strictly — memorandum defines corporate capacity, not just objects clause.
  • Remember that minor's incapacity is absolute — no restitution even if minor obtained goods by fraud.
  • For negotiable instruments, focus on who can enforce, what defences are available, and notice requirements.

Common traps

  • Confusing void and voidable contracts — void agreements are never enforceable by either party, while voidable contracts remain enforceable unless avoided by aggrieved party. — Void = nullity ab initio. Voidable = valid until rescinded by party with option to avoid.
  • Treating all agreements with family members as valid — consideration requirement applies equally; natural love and affection exception requires closeness and written registration. — Familial agreements without consideration remain void despite relationship.
  • Assuming condition always permits rejection — once buyer accepts goods or acts inconsistently with seller's ownership, condition breaches reduce to warranty claims only. — Election of remedy matters; conduct after discovery can convert condition breach to warranty claim.
  • Forgetting that holder in due course protects only for negotiable instruments, not ordinary contracts. — Holder in due course doctrine is unique to negotiable instruments and provides better title than transferor possessed.
  • Applying limitation periods uniformly — different contracts have different periods starting from different events (discovery of fraud, continuing breaches, etc.). — Identify cause of action date carefully; discovery rule applies in fraud cases.
