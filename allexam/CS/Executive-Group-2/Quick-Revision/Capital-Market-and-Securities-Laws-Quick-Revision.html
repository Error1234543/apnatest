Quick Revision: Capital Market and Securities Laws — CS Executive Group 2

CSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Capital Market: The segment of the financial market where long-term debt or equity-backed securities are bought and sold. It comprises the primary market (new issues) and the secondary market (trading of existing securities).
  • Primary Market: Also called the new-issue market; it is the market in which companies raise fresh capital by issuing securities for the first time or through further issues. Transactions here directly benefit the issuer.
  • Secondary Market: The market where already-issued securities are traded among investors. Transactions here do not involve the issuing company and do not raise fresh capital for it. Stock exchanges are the principal venue.
  • Prospectus: A document issued by a company inviting offers from the public for subscription/purchase of its shares or debentures, or for the purpose of raising money. It must disclose all material facts that a reasonable investor would consider in deciding whether to invest.
  • Offer Document: A disclosure document required to be filed with SEBI when a company makes a rights issue or a book-building issue. It contains information substantially equivalent to a prospectus but tailored to the specific type of issue.
  • Listing: The admission of securities of a company to trading on a recognised stock exchange. Only listed securities can be freely traded on the exchange.
  • DEMAT (Dematerialisation): The process of converting physical share certificates into electronic form, held with a depository. It eliminates risks of loss, theft, forgery and enables faster settlement.
  • Depository: An institution that holds securities in electronic (demat) form and facilitates their transfer. In India, NSDL (National Securities Depository Ltd.) and CDSL (Central Depository Services Ltd.) are the two depositories.
  • SEBI: Securities and Exchange Board of India — the statutory regulator for the securities market in India, established in 1988 and conferred statutory powers through the SEBI Act, 1992.
  • Merchant Banker: An intermediary registered with SEBI under the SEBI (Merchant Bankers) Regulations, 1992, who undertakes issue management, portfolio management, advisory services and other financial services related to the securities market.

Important facts

  • SEBI established: 1988; Statutory status: 1992 (SEBI Act came into force in January 1992).
  • SEBI Act, 1992 — Sections 11, 11B, 11A confer objects, powers and functions.
  • Depositories Act enacted: 1996; came into force: September 1996.
  • NSDL established: 1996; CDSL established: 1998.
  • ISE (Interscholarsecurities market) is governed by the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 (SCRA).
  • SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015 replaced the older Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements framework with consolidated norms.
  • SEBI (ICDR) Regulations, 2018 consolidates regulations on issue of capital and disclosure requirements (merged earlier Separate ICDR Regulations).
  • Mandatory open offer trigger threshold under SAST: 25% of voting rights.
  • Top 1,000 listed entities required to file BRSR (Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report) annually.
  • Shares must be transferred in demat form for listed companies since April 1, 2019 (all trading must be in demat form).
  • Grey Market Premium: not legally recognised but widely referenced in IPO context.
  • Preferential allotment needs: (a) shareholder approval by special resolution, (b) valuation report from SEBI-registered valuer, (c) compliance with Section 62(1)(c) Companies Act, 2013.

Memory aids

  • SEBI Functions Mnemonic — "PRD": Protective, Regulatory, Developmental.
  • SAST Trigger – "25 Open" : At 25% threshold, mandatory Open Offer of 26% follows.
  • Depository Acts – "Deposities Act 1996 → Demat made possible", NSDL (N=Novel first, N=National), CDSL (C=Central, C=Commodity-like service).
  • ICDR vs LODR: ICDR = Issue Capital Disclosure Requirements (front end — getting listed); LODR = Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements (back end — keeping listed).
  • BRSR: Remember "Big 1000 Reported Sustainably and Responsibly" — top 1000 listed entities must file.
  • Merchant Banker Categories – "I, II, III" : Cat I does everything, Cat III only broking. Count backwards (III is narrowest scope).

Exam tips

  • Memorise key dates and thresholds: SEBI Act 1992, Depositories Act 1996, demat mandate date (April 1, 2019), open offer trigger at 25%, BRSR applicability at top 1000.
  • Know the difference between ICDR and LODR regulations by function — ICDR covers issuance and prospectus requirements; LODR covers post-listing obligations.
  • Practice differentiation questions: e.g., distinguish between FPI and FII, primary vs secondary market, dematerialisation vs custodianship, merchant banker categories.
  • For descriptive answers, always cite the relevant regulation name (e.g., SEBI (SAST) Regulations, 2011; SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015) along with key provisions.
  • Draw simple flowcharts for issue processes (IPO steps, preferential allotment steps) in your answer sheet to gain presentation marks.
  • Remember that SEBI has adjudicatory authority — any penalty questions should reference SEBI's power under Section 15A–15T of the SEBI Act.
  • In the CS Executive exam, questions on insider trading, open offer triggers, and depository mechanisms are perennially high-yield.

Common traps

  • Confusing Primary Market with Money Market: Primary market raises fresh capital; money market deals with short-term instruments. Never mix them.
  • Open Offer quantity confusion: Many students mistakenly remember the open offer percentage as 25%. It is actually 26% (to push acquirer above the trigger threshold of 25%).
  • SEBI vs RBI jurisdiction: RBI regulates banking institutions and money market instruments; SEBI regulates securities market. Mutual funds fall under SEBI despite having banking links.
  • Demat conversion deadline trap: Since April 1, 2019, all share transfers in listed companies must be in demat form — this is frequently tested as a date-based fact.
  • Sweat equity lock-in: Confusing the 1-year lock-in for sweat equity shares with the 3-year lock-in for ESOPs. They differ.
  • Book-building vs Fixed Price: Students often confuse anchor investor eligibility — anchors are permitted only in book-built issues, not fixed-price issues.
  • LODR deadline misreading: Quarterly results due within 45 days of quarter-end for listed entities, not 30 days. Annual financial statements are also within 60 days of AGM.
  • Preferential allotment: Must have valuation by SEBI-registered valuer — not just any CA; the distinction is frequently tested.
