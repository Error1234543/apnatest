Definitions

  • Consumer: Any person who buys goods or hires services for consideration; includes any user of such goods/services with the buyer's approval. Excludes a person who re-sells the goods for commercial purposes.
  • Anti-competitive agreement: An agreement between enterprises or persons that causes or is likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC) in India. Includes cartel agreements (horizontal) and vertical restraints.
  • Dominant position: A position of strength enjoyed by an enterprise in the relevant market, which enables it to operate independently of competitive forces or affect its competitors or consumers in its favor.
  • Abuse of dominant position: Conduct by a dominant enterprise that disproportionately restricts market access or exploits consumers — e.g., imposing unfair/discriminatory prices, conditions, refusal to deal, tying, or limiting technical development.
  • Combination: Acquisition, amalgamation, or merger of enterprises exceeding notified thresholds, requiring prior approval of the Competition Commission of India (CCI).
  • Holder in due course: A person who became a holder of a negotiable instrument for valuable consideration, in good faith, with reasonable care, and before maturity — enjoys a pristine title even if a prior party had a defect.
  • Consideration: Something in return (quid pro quo). A promise enforceable under contract law must be supported by lawful consideration unless falling within specific exceptions.
  • Material default (Cheque bounce): Dishonour of a cheque for insufficiency of funds or exceeding arrangement with the bank; the drawer is liable under Section 138 after statutory demand notice.
  • Digital signature: Authentication of any electronic record by a subscriber using an electronic key pair (private key + public key), as defined under the IT Act.
  • PEPSI (Personal Data / Sensitive Personal Data or Information): Under IT Act rules, special categories of data — password, financial info, health condition, sexual orientation, biometric, etc. — that require stricter consent and protection.
  • Lien: A right retained by a bailee, pledgee, carrier, or attorney over goods/documents till payment for services rendered. Two kinds: general lien (right to retain any goods for balance of account) and particular lien (retention only for charges relating to the specific goods). Only certain classes — factor, wharfinger, attorney, banker, policy broker, pawnee — have a general lien.

Key concepts

  • CONSUMER PROTECTION ACT, 2019: Consumer Rights — (i) Right to safety against hazardous goods, (ii) Right to be informed about quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard, price, (iii) Right to choose (access to variety at competitive prices), (iv) Right to be heard/represented in consumer forums, (v) Right to seek redressal, (vi) Right to consumer education. Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions: District (pecuniary up to ₹50 lakh), State (₹50 lakh to ₹2 crore), National (above ₹2 crore). Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) has power to investigate violations, recall products, impose penalties, and issue safety notices. Class action is now permitted. Mediation is a mandatory pre-litigation step in certain disputes. E-filing of complaints is enabled.
  • COMPETITION ACT, 2002: Three pillars — (i) Anti-competitive Agreements (Section 3): Horizontal agreements (cartels — price fixing, market sharing, bid-rigging, output restriction) are presumed to cause AAEC. Vertical agreements (exclusive supply, exclusive distribution, tie-in, resale price maintenance) are judged on rule of reason/test of AAEC. Positive list of exemptions exists for certain tech transfer, standards, export cartels. (ii) Abuse of Dominant Position (Section 4): Dominance itself is not illegal; abuse is. Factors for dominance include market share, economic power, barriers to entry, countervailing buyer power, dependency of consumers. (iii) Combinations (Sections 5 & 6): Thresholds based on assets/turnover (India + global/double of India). Combination not effective until confirmed by CCI. Penalties on parties for consummating before approval. CCI is the adjudicatory authority; Penal Consequences are separate.
  • NEGO T IABLE INSTRUMENTS ACT, 1881: Key instruments — Promissory Note (Sec 4(1): maker promises to pay), Bill of Exchange (drawer orders drawee to pay), Cheque (Sec 6: drawee is always a banker; payable on demand). Sections 85 & 91 (cheque protection): banker who pays in due course is discharged. Holder in due course (Sec 9): must hold for consideration, in good faith, without enough cause to believe a defect exists — enjoys the best possible title. Crossing of cheques (Sec 123–131): general crossing (two parallel lines) vs. special crossing (name of a banker). 'Not negotiable' crossing prevents transfer of better title. Accrual of liability is on issuance of note/bill; consideration may be past, present, or future. Part payment (Sec 58) must be endorsed. Loss of promissory note/bill entitles holder to sue on evidence. Blank vs. filled incomplete instruments: maker must complete within reasonable time; if filled beyond, original signatory is discharged unless in possession as holder in due course. Stamp duty and admissibility: unstamped instruments are inadmissible in evidence but can be stamped later.
  • CHEQUE BOUNCE — SECTION 138, NI ACT: Ingredients: (1) cheque drawn by person on account maintained with banker, (2) for discharge wholly/partially of a debt/other liability, (3) returned unpaid by banker (insufficiency or exceeding arrangement), (4) payee gives written demand notice within 30 days of dishonour, (5) drawer fails to pay within 15 days of receipt of notice, (6) complaint filed within 30 days of cause of action ( Sec 142). Time limits are mandatory and strictly construed. Jurisdiction: magistrate where drawee branch is situated. Compoundability: offences under Sec 138 are compoundable. Section 143A: interim compensation of up to 20% of cheque amount may be awarded on application during pendency of appeal — recoverable if accused is acquitted, or refundable if conviction upheld. Recent amendments (2018) widened scope to allow interim compensation. Supreme Court has held that the 3-month limitation for filing complaint runs from the day following expiry of 15-day notice period; also clarified that subsequent payments/negotiations do not reset the limitation automatically. In case of a partnership firm, partners can be proceeded against (Sec 141). The proviso requires the cheque to be issued in discharge of a legally enforceable debt/liability — however, civil courts routinely take plaintiff's contention as prima facie established in criminal proceedings.
  • INDEMNITY AND GUARANTEE (Contract Act, 1872): Indemnity (Sec 124): contract by one party to save the other from loss caused by conduct of the promisor himself or a third person. Only two parties. Example: insurance contract. Guarantee (Sec 126): three-party contract — surety guarantees to creditor the debtor's performance. Surety has right of subrogation (Sec 140) upon payment: steps into shoes of creditor including all securities. Right of compensation (Sec 145): to claim expense incurred in discharging liability. Right to compel payment (Sec 146): if consideration is two-fold (creditor + another beneficiary), surety can compel both to contribute. Bank's right of set-off and combined account treatment are judicial developments on guarantee. Continuing guarantee (Sec 129–130): extends to a series of transactions; revocable by notice. Revocation happens automatically on death (unless contracted otherwise), on variation of terms without surety's consent (Sec 133), on creditor's act disabling surety's remedy (Sec 134), or on discharge of principal debtor by composition/accord.
  • INSURANCE CONTRACTS — PRINCIPLES: Utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei) — all material facts must be disclosed (Sec 4 of Insurance Act + IRDAI regulations). Materiality: anything influencing the mind of a prudent insurer in fixing premium or deciding to accept risk. Broking: disclosure by broker = disclosure by assured. Representation must be substantially true. Warranties must be exactly complied with; breach discharges insurer. Principle of indemnity (life insurance excepted — life contracts are valued policies; marine/fire/property are indemnity-based). Insurable interest must exist at time of loss (marine) or at inception (other contracts). Subrogation: insurer steps into insured's shoes after paying claim — cannot recover more than the actual loss. Contribution: where two or more policies cover same risk, insurers share the loss proportionately. Proximate cause: the dominant/effective cause of loss must be covered peril. Loss adjustment: depreciation claimed subject to sum insured.
  • FEMA, 1999 — OBJECTIVES & FRAMEWORK: Replaced FERA to liberalize forex management. Objective: facilitate external trade and payments, promote orderly development of foreign exchange market. Residents defined broadly — Indian citizen, NRI, foreign national resident in India, body corporate incorporated in India, branch/office in India of foreign entity. Schedule I (current account transactions) — freely permissible: trade, travel, gifts, maintenance abroad, dividends, etc. Schedule II (capital account transactions) — regulated/restricted: overseas investment, borrowing, immovable property acquisition by NRIs (permitted under specified conditions). RBI/Reserve Bank permissions needed for capital account liberalization beyond auto-route. ODA (Overseas Direct Investment) / ODI (Overseas Portfolio Investment) routes governed by FEMA/Notifications. ECB (External Commercial Borrowings) — floor rate (CD swap rate + 200 bps) and end-use restrictions apply. FEMA contravention: penalty up to 3× sum involved, up to ₹2 lakh where amount not quantifiable; Adjudicating Officer decides; Appeal to Appellate Tribunal (FEMA); Prosecution requires prior written permission of Director. Compounding of offences by authorized officer is possible.
  • IT ACT, 2000 — KEY PROVISIONS: Legal recognition of electronic records (Sec 4) and digital signatures (Sec 5). Section 6A governs authentication by digital signature. Section 6B covers certification of digital signatures. Section 7–9 cover use of electronic records where law requires handwriting/signature. Section 10A validates contracts concluded through electronic means. Section 11: communication of electronic records — deemed received when it enters recipient's server. Section 12: attribution — electronic record sent by a person is attributable to him if sent by system operated by him or authorized. Section 13: invokation — deemed sent from place where sender has establishment; deemed received at place of addressee's principal place of business. Section 14: definition of 'electronic signature'. Section 15: secure electronic signature = digital signature created by secure key generation facility + hashing algorithm. Section 16–31 deal with Digital Signature Certificates and Certifying Authorities (CA), registration, obligations, audit, supervision by Controller. Section 37: Designated Government. Section 43A: compensation for failure to protect sensitive personal data — notified under SPTA Rules 2011. Section 48: Data protection authorities. Section 65: Tampering with computer source code — punishable. Section 66: Computer-related offences (hacking, virus, data theft). Section 66C: Identity theft. Section 66D: Cheating by personation. Section 66E: Violation of privacy (capturing/publishing images). Section 67: Obscene electronic content; 67A: Publishing/transmitting sexually explicit material; 67B: Child pornography. Section 70: Protected systems. Section 72A: Disclosure pursuant to legal obligation. Section 79: Intermediary liability exemption — intermediaries are not liable for third-party content if they observe due process and do not initiate/modify transmission, and expeditiously remove upon actual knowledge or court/govt order.
  • LIEN, PLEDGE, HYPOTHESIS — Pledge vs. Hypothecation: Pledge (Sec 172): bailment of goods as security for payment/performance; pledgee has right to retain but not to sell without due process (after reasonable notice). Possession is essential. Hypothecation (Sec 172 implied but not named): security without possession — hypothecatee gets no right of retention but has equitable charge; commonly used in vehicle/car loans. Mortgage/charge over moveables is not pledge. Lien (Sec 114–123, Sale of Goods Act + Contract Act): right to retain possession till payment; general vs. particular lien. Seller's lien (Sec 45): unpaid seller retains goods if insolvent or credit expired. Carrier's lien (Sec 62): for charges. Artisan's lien (Sec 83): for work done on specific article. Pawnee's lien (Sec 176): pledgee may retain pledged goods till payment.
  • SELLER & BUYER RIGHTS — SALE OF GOODS ACT, 1930: Conditions (essential terms going to root of contract) vs. warranties (collateral terms). Breach of condition may be treated as breach of warranty. No-condition clauses ('as is') do not absolve seller of latent defects known to him. Nemo dat quod non habet (Sec 27): seller cannot convey better title than he possesses. Exceptions (Sec 27–30): seller in possession after sale, buyer in possession, mercantile agent, sale under voidable title, sale by seller under reserve, sale by co-owner. Sale by non-owner (auctioneer) — bona fide purchasers are protected under certain conditions. Right of unpaid seller: (i) Lien (Sec 45–49), (ii) Right of stoppage in transit (Sec 50–59) — stops delivery while goods are with carrier before buyer takes delivery, (iii) Right of resale (Sec 54). Unpaid seller's lien lost on delivery to carrier, waiver, buyer's lawful possession, or sub-sale. Transit ends when buyer/agent takes delivery from carrier or carrier acknowledges holding on buyer's behalf. Stopping in transit operates by giving notice to carrier; carrier must redeliver to seller at seller's expense. Damages for non-acceptance (Sec 73) — ordinary rules of remoteness; loss of profit. Installment contracts (Sec 32): partial delivery accepted = waiver of breach; rejection of one installment may repudiate whole.
  • AGENCY — CONTRACT ACT, 1872: Agent (Sec 182): person employed to do acts for principal or represent principal in dealings with third persons. Principal: person on whose behalf agent acts. Creation: express (written/oral), implied (from conduct, relation, ratification). Ratification (Sec 196–201): approving unauthorized act retrospectively; requires lawful act, capacity, full disclosure, acceptance of whole transaction, no third-party right acquired in interim. Duties of agent: conduct business according to directions, skill and diligence, communicate, render accounts, hand over properties, not to deal on own account, not to take secret profits. Authority (Sec 186–193): express vs. implied (usual/customary). Apparent authority (Sec 188): ostensible authority creates estoppel against principal if principal represents third party that agent has authority. Revocation (Sec 201–209): by act of parties, operation of law. Revocation must be communicated to agent and to third parties who dealt with agent. Agent's lien (Sec 217): right to retain principal's goods/money for commission/account. Principal's liability to third parties: disclosed principal — agent is conduit; undisclosed principal — agent can be sued personally and principal may be joined if proved. Broker vs. factor: factor in possession of goods; broker merely arranges deals without possession.

Important facts

  • Consumer Protection Act, 2019: Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) — enforcement via recall, penalty, class action. Complaints can be filed online. E-commerce entities are now explicitly covered as 'goods sellers.' Product liability action can be initiated against manufacturer, brand owner, seller, or service provider.
  • Competition Act, 2002: CCI was constituted in 2009 (originally intended earlier). Enterprise threshold notifications updated periodically. Section 4 prohibits abuse of dominance; no per se prohibition unlike Sec 3 horizontal agreements. Leniency regime exists for cartels (first informant gets full immunity). Competition Commission vs. Supreme Court jurisprudence is extensive on 'rule of reason' vs 'per se' for verticals.
  • Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881: 26 sections amended in 2018 (introducing Sec 143A interim compensation, amendment to Sec 138 limitation). Supreme Court in Surendra Trading Co. v. Joga Raju (1994) laid down principles on holder in due course. S.S. Telegu Desam v. CBI (and later Rajesh Agrawal) clarified jurisdiction for cheque bounce cases. The 'immediate former holder' rule under Sec 142(a) has been interpreted restrictively recently.
  • Cheque bounce limitation: 3 months from date of cause of action (i.e., after 15-day notice period expires) as per amended Sec 142(b) and Supreme Court decisions. The 15-day period is a statutory grant; not extendable by court discretion except in fit cases for condonation of delay under Sec 142(b). Recently, the Supreme Court in N. Hari Narayanan v. M. Sundaram (2023) reaffirmed strict adherence to limitation timelines.
  • FEMA: Reserve Bank of India is the primary regulator. Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) allows an individual to remit USD 250,000 per financial year for any current or capital account transaction (not for trading in securities or currency). ECB policy stack updated over time; floor rate is dynamic. FEMA contravention is a compoundable offence with limited prosecution route (requires Central Government's consent).
  • IT Act, 2000: Sec 43A placed responsibility on bodies corporate handling sensitive personal data; rules framed under SPTA 2000 mandate consent, purpose limitation, and security safeguards. Intermediary guidelines (IT Rules 2021) expanded compliance duties — grievance officers, traceability constraints, and due diligence. Certifying Authorities licensed by Controller of Certifying Authorities (CCA); RAUL (Registered Verifying Officer) framework. Digital Evidence rules require forensic examination for admissibility in certain cases.
  • Insurance Act, 1938: IRDAI (Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority) was established in 1999 as statutory body; regulations cover solvency margins, policyholder protection, disclosure norms. Life Insurance Corporation (LIC) nationalized in 1956; General Insurance Corporation (GIC) restructured into four general insurance companies in 2002. New insurance entities (Life/General) now permitted under modified regulatory framework withprudential norms.
  • Indemnity & Guarantee: Indemnity involves two parties; guarantee involves three. A surety's right of subrogation arises only after the surety has paid the debt fully. If the creditor releases the principal debtor without preserving securities, the surety is discharged (Sec 139). If the creditor's inaction prejudices the surety's eventual recourse (Sec 139), surety is discharged.
  • Seller's remedies: Under Sec 54 (Sale of Goods Act), an unpaid seller who resells the goods after exercising lien or stoppage in transit can recover the difference between sale price and contract price as damages. Stoppage in transit requires notice to the carrier and is effective only if carrier still has possession. Once buyer takes delivery from carrier, transit ends.
  • Agency: An agent acting within actual or apparent authority binds the principal. If agent exceeds authority but principal ratifies, ratification relates back to the date of the act. An undisclosed principal can be sued once discovered; however, if the third party chose to contract with the agent personally, the principal cannot later intervene.
  • Key difference: Lien retains possession without transferring ownership or right to sell (only right to withhold); Pledge gives pledgee right to sell after notice upon default; Hypothecation carries neither possession nor immediate sale right — only a charge and right to take possession via court/seller intervention; Mortgage is over immovable property with creation of charge.

Memory aids

  • CCPA — remember as 'Central Consumer Protection Authority' with powers to recall, penalize, and bring class actions. The three-tier system: District → State → National, ascending pecuniary limits: ₹50 Lakh → ₹2 Crore → above ₹2 Crore.
  • CARDS for Competition Act pillars: Cartel (anti-competitive agreements), Abuse (of dominant position), Combinations (mergers/acquisitions needing CCI approval). Cartel members are per se guilty of AAEC; vertical restraints judged on rule of reason.
  • HIC for Holder in Due Course: Holder, In good faith, Consideration. Memorize: 'He Gives Coins'.
  • N.I. Act Sec 138 checklist (remember as NOTICE): Notice given within 30 days → 15-day demand → Complaint within 30 days of cause of action → Jurisdiction at drawee's branch → Tripple test of debt, liability, dishonour.
  • RAT for ratification: Recall (act was unauthorized), Accept (principal accepts all consequences), Termination not already complete (third party hasn't acquired rights in interim). Ratification cannot relate back if it prejudices a bona fide third party.
  • FEMA acronym for Residents: Foreigners, Entrants, Merchants — all can be 'residents' under FEMA depending on duration. Auto-route means prior approval not needed; Route is automatic. ODI/ODA: Overseas Direct/Portfolio Investment.
  • INTERMEDIARY defence under IT Act Sec 79: Remember 'Notice and Remove' — intermediary must observe due diligence, must not initiate/modify transmission, must expeditiously remove upon actual knowledge. Safe harbour applies only if these three conditions met.
  • P-L-A-N for Agency creation: Power (express/implied), Likeness (holding out/apparent), Agreement (contractual), Neces­sity (emergencies). For agent duties: remember CARE — Conduct, Account, Communicate, Right (not to deal on own account).
  • LPH mnemonic for security rights: Lien (possessory right, no sale), Pledge (bailment, right to sell after notice), Hypothecation (no possession, equitable charge). Think of a shopkeeper (hypothecation — car loan), pawnbroker (pledge — gold kept in vault), repairman (lien — retaining phone till bill paid).
  • Sell/Buy: For Sale of Goods Act remedies, remember 'SLAP': Stoppage, Lien, Against price (damages), Repudiation/Resale.

Exam tips

  • In CS Executive descriptive papers, always cite the relevant section number along with the principle. Examiners expect precise statutory references (e.g., 'Section 138, Negotiable Instruments Act' rather than just 'cheque bounce offence').
  • For Competition Act questions, lead with the distinction between per se and rule of reason — this is a high-weightage conceptual area. Use case examples like cartel price-fixing (per se) vs. exclusive distribution agreements (rule of reason) to illustrate.
  • When answering Consumer Protection Act questions, structure your answer by: (a) definition of consumer, (b) rights available, (c) forum with pecuniary jurisdiction, (d) procedure, (e) remedy. CCPA's role under the 2019 Act should be highlighted separately since it is a new departure.
  • For NI Act questions, create a timeline diagram in your answer sheet showing the 30+15+30 day cycle — this visual aid scores well and avoids mechanical errors in limitation computation.
  • In FEMA questions, distinguish between current account and capital account transactions with specific examples from Schedules I and II. Mention the LRS limit (USD 250,000 p.a.) and that trading in foreign exchange is prohibited — this shows practical awareness.
  • For IT Act questions on intermediary liability, use the three-part test from Sec 79: (1) function limited to providing access/communication, (2) did not initiate/modify transmission, (3) removed upon actual knowledge. Reference the Shreya Singhal judgment principles if relevant to free speech vs. takedown.
  • In indemnity/guarantee questions, always draw a tripartite diagram for guarantee (creditor-debtor-surety) and bipartite for indemnity. List the surety's rights under Sec 140–142 separately with section numbers — this earns full marks even if the substantive answer is brief.
  • For Stoppage in Transit questions, remember: transit begins when goods are delivered to a carrier for transmission to buyer, and ends when buyer or agent takes delivery from carrier. Intermittent transit or carrier acknowledging holding for buyer terminates transit. Cite Sec 50–59.
  • Practice drawing comparison tables for: (a) Condition vs. Warranty, (b) Pledge vs. Hypothecation vs. Lien, (c) Indemnity vs. Guarantee, (d) General Lien vs. Particular Lien, (e) Current Account vs. Capital Account under FEMA. These are perennially asked in comparative form.
  • In recent years, ICSI has asked integrated questions combining two topics (e.g., Consumer rights + competition law, or FEMA + IT Act). Practice writing answers that connect cross-cutting themes like 'protection of stakeholders' across multiple Acts.
  • Always mention the date of enactment/amendment when relevant — e.g., Competition Act 2002 (amended 2007, 2017, 2022), CPA 2019 (replacing 1986 Act), NI Act 1881 (amended 2018). Showing awareness of legislative evolution distinguishes a good answer.
  • For cheque bounce problems, check three things before concluding: (i) Was there a legally enforceable debt/liability? (ii) Was the demand notice properly served and within time? (iii) Was the complaint filed within limitation? Any flaw defeats the case — write this analytical checklist in your answer.

Common traps

  • Trap: Thinking competition law treats all anti-competitive agreements equally. Horizontal (cartel) agreements are presumed to cause AAEC (Sec 3(3)); vertical agreements are tested on rule of reason (Sec 3(4)). This distinction is frequently examined.
  • Trap: Confusing Indemnity (two parties — indemnifier + indemnified) with Guarantee (three parties — creditor + principal debtor + surety). In indemnity, liability arises only when loss actually occurs; in guarantee, liability is co-extensive with principal debtor's from the start (Sec 128).
  • Trap: Assuming a banker has a general lien on all customers' securities. Only specified classes (factor, wharfinger, attorney, banker, policy broker, pawnee) enjoy general lien. A banker's general lien is over securities deposited in course of business, but not over title deeds of immovable property unless specifically pledged as such.
  • Trap: Mixing up limitation periods under NI Act. Notice must be served within 30 days of dishonour (not 60 days). Drawer gets 15 days to pay after notice receipt. Complaint must be filed within 30 days of cause of action arising — i.e., after 15-day notice period expires. These three periods are distinct and frequently confused.
  • Trap: Assuming the IT Act provides absolute immunity to intermediaries. Sec 79 immunity is conditional on observing due diligence and expeditious removal upon actual knowledge. The Intermediary Guidelines Rules (2021) have layered additional obligations.
  • Trap: Treating 'not negotiable' crossing as making the cheque non-transferable. It does not prevent transfer; it only prevents the transferee from obtaining a better title than the transferor had. 'To a.c.b. payee only' crossing makes it non-negotiable in fact.
  • Trap: Forgetting that a holder in due course must acquire the instrument before maturity. A holder who acquires a bill after maturity cannot claim HDC status (Sec 9).
  • Trap: Assuming an unpaid seller can always resell goods. Right of resale arises only after exercising lien or stoppage in transit and giving notice of intention to resale (Sec 54). Resale without satisfying these conditions may give buyer a claim for damages.
  • Trap: Believing a surety can sue the principal debtor before paying the debt. Under Sec 140, subrogation rights arise only after the surety has paid the debt in full. Before that, the surety has no independent remedy against the debtor.
  • Trap: Forgetting that ratification requires the act to be lawful. An unauthorized act which is illegal or void ab initio cannot be ratified (Sec 200). Also, ratification cannot be partial — the principal must adopt the whole transaction.
  • Trap: Confusing general lien with particular lien. Particular lien attaches only to the specific goods for charges relating to those goods (e.g., solicitor's lien on documents). General lien permits retention of any goods for the overall balance of account. Most liens are particular, not general.
  • Trap: Misunderstanding when insurer is discharged under utmost good faith. Non-disclosure must be material. Insurer must prove that disclosure would have affected the mind of a prudent insurer. 'But for' causation test is NOT required — it suffices that the fact was material to the risk.
