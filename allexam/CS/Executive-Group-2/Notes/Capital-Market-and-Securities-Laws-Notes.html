Structure of Capital Market: Primary, Secondary and Derivatives Markets

['The Indian capital market comprises institutions, markets and instruments that facilitate the mobilization of long-term savings. It is broadly divided into two segments: the Primary Market and the Secondary Market, with a distinct derivatives segment operating alongside both.', 'Primary Market (Issue Market): This is where securities are issued for the first time and capital is raised by companies. It includes Initial Public Offers (IPOs), further issues such as rights issues, preferential/allotted issues, private placements, bonus issues, and buy-back of securities. The primary market connects issuers with investors directly, though intermediaries — merchant bankers, registrars, legal counsel, and underwriters — facilitate the process. Key statutory anchors are Section 39 of the Companies Act, 2013 (prohibition on public offers without an effective prospectus/Red Herring Prospectus), Section 42 (private placement conditions), and SEBI ICDR Regulations 2018 (as amended). Pricing mechanisms include fixed price, book-building (with anchor, QIB, retail and NII categories), and offers for sale versus offers of new securities.', 'Secondary Market (Stock Market): Here, already-issued securities are bought and sold among investors on recognized stock exchanges or through alternative trading systems. Stock exchanges (NSE, BSE, etc.) provide a regulated platform ensuring transparency, fair price discovery, and investor protection. SEBI supervises exchanges under the SCRA, 1956 and SEBI Act, 1992. Trading can occur through screen-based trading systems (SBTs), order-driven or trade-matching mechanisms, or through designated multi-commodity exchanges for commodity derivatives.', 'Derivatives Market: Derivatives derive value from an underlying asset — equities, indices, commodities, currencies, or interest rates. In India, equity and index derivatives are traded on recognized stock exchanges under SEBI (ICDR / SME & Derivatives) framework. The four principal types are forwards, futures, options, and swaps. Key risk-management tools include hedge positions, spread trades, and arbitrage. SEBI and the respective exchange must approve the contract specs, margining methodology, circuit filters, and position limits.', 'Money Market vs Capital Market: The money market handles short-term funds (up to one year) through instruments such as Treasury Bills, Commercial Paper, Certificates of Deposit, Call/Notice money, and Commercial Bills. The capital market deals with long-term funds (over one year) through equities, debentures, bonds, and derivative contracts. Both markets are interlinked because liquidity in money markets influences cost of capital in equity/debt markets. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) supervises money-market instruments; SEBI supervises capital-market securities.']

SEBI: Objectives, Functions, Powers and Regulatory Architecture

["Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) was established in 1988 as a non-statutory body and granted statutory powers through the SEBI Act, 1992 (effective April 1992). Its headquarters are in Mumbai, and it operates through multiple regional offices across India. SEBI is the premier regulator of India's securities and capital markets.", "Objectives (Section 11 of the SEBI Act): The three core objectives are: (i) to protect the interests of investors in securities; (ii) to promote the development of the securities market; and (iii) to regulate the securities market. Over the decades, SEBI's mandate has expanded to cover mutual funds, debentures, securitized debt instruments, listing agreements, takeovers, insider trading, collective investment schemes, portfolio management services, and infrastructure financing.", 'Powers and Functions (Section 11A and subsequent provisions): SEBI can (a) register and regulate intermediaries — brokers, sub-brokers, merchant bankers, underwriters, portfolio managers, investment advisers, registrars, transfer agents, credit rating agencies, custodians, depository participants, foreign portfolio investors, and venture capital funds; (b) register and regulate mutual funds and collective investment schemes; (c) prohibit fraudulent and unfair trade practices; (d) regulate substantial acquisition of shares and takeovers; (e) regulate insider trading; (f) call for information, undertake inspections, conduct inquiries and investigations; (g) issue directions, circulars, and regulations; (h) levy fees and penalties; (i) supervise stock exchanges and self-regulatory organizations (SROs); and (j) perform functions under the SCRA, 1956, the Depositories Act, 1996, and the Companies Act, 2013 (in relation to securities regulation).', "Regulatory instruments and hierarchy: SEBI makes Regulations (having the force of law, enforceable against entities covered), Circulars/Guidance Notes (interpretative or procedural direction), and Press Releases/Advisories (informational guidance). For dispute resolution, SEBI has the Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT) as the appellate authority under Section 15K of the SEBI Act. Civil courts are excluded from matters within SAT's purview. SAT decisions can be appealed to the Supreme Court on a substantial question of law under Section 15T.", "Recent structural developments: SEBI has undertaken significant consolidation and simplification of its regulatory framework, notably merging the older ICDR, Issue and Listing, and SME Listing regulations into unified frameworks, harmonizing obligations across different market segments. SEBI has also strengthened enforcement through the Market Abuse Surveillance System (MASS), enhanced disclosure norms for related-party transactions, and tighter governance expectations for listed entities. Students should note SEBI's emphasis on ESG disclosures and governance improvements for listed companies in recent years."]

Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 — Key Provisions

['The SCRA, 1956 is the foundational statute governing securities contracts, stock exchanges, and clearing corporations in India. It provides the legal recognition and framework for exchange-based trading and regulates the validity and enforceability of securities contracts.', "Definition of 'Securities' (Section 2(h)): Includes shares, securities, stock exchange nil-bearing receipts, government securities, derivative, investment units issued by mutual funds, securitized debt instrument, security receipt, voting right security, equity share, preference share, debenture, bond, deposit receipt, certificate or instrument evidencing participation in an issue of any such security, and right or interest in insurance policies or annuity contracts. This is an inclusive definition, capturing traditional and newer instruments.", 'Recognized Stock Exchanges (Chapter V-A, Sections 4–6): A stock exchange must obtain recognition from SEBI under Section 4. The application is examined on parameters including financial soundness, infrastructure, managerial competence, adequate bye-laws/rules, investor protection mechanisms, and ability to ensure orderly functioning. Recognition may be suspended or revoked under Section 6 if conditions are not met or if the exchange fails to comply with SEBI directions. Trading on non-recognized exchanges is generally unlawful unless specifically exempted.', "Regulation of Securities Contracts (Sections 1–3): A 'securities contract' means a contract for buying, selling, or dealing in securities that is recognized by a recognized stock exchange. Such contracts are neither void nor voidable merely because they are not executed on a recognized stock exchange, but enforceability and regulatory scrutiny differ. The SCRA gives SEBI power to regulate the business of stock exchanges and to prescribe standards for fair dealing.", 'Prohibition of Manipulative Practices (Section 12B): No person shall indulge in any manipulative or fraudulent device or scheme in connection with a securities contract. This includes creating artificial prices, wash sales, matched orders, and circulating false or misleading information. Violations attract penalties and prosecution.', "Listing Agreements: Under the SCRA framework, listed companies enter into listing agreements with stock exchanges, which contain covenants regarding periodic disclosure, corporate governance, shareholder communication, and compliance with SEBI guidelines. While listing regulations have been largely absorbed into SEBI's direct regulatory framework, the conceptual requirement of exchange-level compliance remains relevant for Company Secretaries.", 'Clearing Corporations: The SCRA was amended to provide for recognition of clearing corporations, which act as central counterparties to mitigate counterparty risk. They guarantee settlement and operate clearing funds, margining systems, and default-fund arrangements. The National Securities Clearing Corporation Limited (NSCCL, now NSDL Clearing Limited) and the Multi Commodity Exchange Clearing Corporation Limited are prominent examples.']

SEBI Regulations on Public Issues and Listing Obligations

['SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018 (ICDR Regulations) regulate public offers of securities, including IPOs, rights issues, bonus issues, offers for sale, and qualified institutions placements (QIPs). These regulations consolidate earlier separate frameworks and apply uniformly based on the nature of the issue rather than the segment alone.', 'Eligibility criteria for main-board public issues: Issuers must satisfy financial criteria (such as minimum net worth and profitability thresholds, adjusted for sector and size), corporate governance standards, and absence of material adverse events. SME entities are governed by the SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018 Part C and associated SME Listing Regulations, which have relaxed eligibility norms relative to the main board. Recent reforms have created distinct listing categories for SMEs on dedicated platforms like the BSE SME and NSE Emerge.', 'Prospectus/Red Herring Prospectus (RHP): For book-built issues, an RHP is filed first, followed by the final prospectus upon price discovery. For fixed-price issues, a prospectus is filed directly. Key contents include risk factors, object of the issue, use of proceeds, detailed financial statements (audited for three years plus interim financials), management discussion and analysis, corporate governance report, grievance redressal mechanism, and particulars of directors/promoters. Material information must be disclosed; omissions or misstatements render the issuer liable.', 'Book-building process: The lead manager determines the price band. Anchor investors (typically QIBs) subscribe ahead of the book opening to provide confidence. The book is opened for a minimum of three working days. Price discovery occurs through bids received from QIBs, corporate applicants, and retail/non-institutional investors. Allocation to QIBs must be at least 50% of the net issue (subject to reservations); retail portion is reserved for individuals up to ₹2 lakhs per application; and NII investors receive the balance. Minimum lot sizes are prescribed, and cut-off time for bid submission is strictly enforced.', "Further issues and corporate actions: Rights issues require proportionate allocation to existing shareholders with a specified subscription period. Bonus issues require fully paid-up capital and adherence to the Companies Act's capital conversion rules. Buy-back of securities is permitted under Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013, subject to limits (maximum 25% of paid-up capital in a year), funding from free reserves or share premium, and SEBI buy-back regulations (disclosure, open-interval mechanics, and completion timelines).", 'Listing obligations (post-ICDR alignment): Listed entities must comply with continuous disclosure norms, including quarterly financial results, material event disclosures within the stipulated timeframe, annual reports, shareholder communications, and a clearly documented investor grievance mechanism. Related-party transactions above thresholds require approval and disclosure. SEBI has mandated stricter related-party transaction norms and enhanced board committee effectiveness, especially for audit and stakeholder relationship committees.']

Takeover Code: SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011

['The SEBI SAST Regulations, 2011 govern acquisitions of shares and voting rights that trigger disclosure, open offer, or control-change obligations on listed companies and their acquirers. The regime aims to ensure equitable treatment of all shareholders when control shifts.', "Trigger points: An acquirer (including persons acting in concert) crossing 25% of voting rights triggers a mandatory disclosure (Form VIA). Crossing 50% triggers disclosure and, if the threshold is acquired along with further acquisition or control is obtained, may trigger an open-offer obligation. Reaching 75% or more requires a full open offer to remaining shareholders. The precise obligation depends on whether the increase is deemed a 'relevant acquisition' and whether it results in change of control.", 'Open Offer requirement: When an acquirer crosses the 25% threshold and subsequently acquires additional voting rights beyond a prescribed threshold (currently exceeding 5% in a financial year for certain categories, or when there is a change of control), an open offer must be made to the public shareholders of the target company. The open offer must be for a minimum of 26% of voting rights (or such percentage as prescribed), at a ceiling price determined by formula (based on consideration paid by the acquirer in preceding fourteen months and the highest price paid in the preceding six months, plus adjustments for bonuses, dividends, etc.).', 'Exemptions from open offer: SEBI grants exemptions under Chapters II and III of the regulations for specific scenarios — intra-group transfers, merger/amalgamation/demerger transactions approved by NCLT, buy-back of securities by the company, reduction of capital, off-market acquisitions with SEBI approval, transfers pursuant to debt restructuring or SEBI orders, and certain FPI/FII transfers (subject to conditions). Each exemption carries explicit conditions and filing requirements.', 'Persons Acting in Concert (PAC): PAC is a vital concept — persons who, pursuant to an agreement or understanding (formal or informal), act in concert in acquiring, disposing of, or exercising voting rights in securities of a listed company. The definition is wide and encompasses promoters, group companies, relatives, advisors, financiers, and any entity sharing common economic interest. PAC aggregation significantly affects trigger analysis and open-offer calculations.', 'Disclosure formats and timelines: Form VIA (acquisition between 25% and 50%), Form VIIA (additional acquisition between 50% and 75%), and Form VIIB (open offer announcement and compliance) must be filed with the exchange and SEBI within specified timeframes. Timeliness is critical — delays can attract penalty proceedings. The acquirer must also publish a statement of objections/rights to dissenting shareholders and ensure the open offer escrow arrangement complies with SEBI guidelines.']

Insider Trading Regulations: SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015 (as amended)

["The SEBI PIT Regulations, 2015 (as substantially amended in 2018) regulate the misuse of unpublished price-sensitive information (UPSI) in securities transactions. The regime applies to all 'insiders' connected with listed companies and their intermediaries.", 'Unpublished Price-Sensitive Information (UPSI): UPSI is information that, if published, is likely to materially affect the price of securities. Examples include financial results, dividends, mergers, acquisitions, buy-backs, changes in directorship or management, major orders or contracts, significant investments, changes in capital structure, and regulatory actions. The regulation adopts an objective test: information is UPSI if a reasonable investor would likely consider it important in making an investment decision.', 'Insiders — Categories: (a) Officials — directors, key managerial persons, and employees who have access to UPSI by virtue of their position; (b) Connected persons — professionals (lawyers, chartered accountants, merchant bankers), consultants, banks, and relatives who have or had access to UPSI through business or professional relationships; (c) Communicators — anyone who knowingly communicates UPSI to others; (d) Receivers — anyone who receives UPSI and trades or causes to be traded, or further communicates it.', 'Prohibitions: No insider shall (i) trade on the basis of UPSI; (ii) communicate or counsel/provoke another to trade on the basis of UPSI; (iii) procure, do or abet the act prohibited above. The prohibition extends to pre-planned trades using anticipated UPSI and trades undertaken after becoming aware of UPSI, even if the trade execution occurs before formal publication.', 'Compliance mechanisms required of listed entities: (1) Designated Officer — a senior officer responsible for PIT compliance, acting as the single point of contact with the exchange and SEBI; (2) Insider Register — maintaining a register of all insiders and their dealings; (3) Code of Conduct — framing a code regulating dealing in securities, including closed periods, trading windows, and pre-clearance procedures; (4) Pre-clearance of trades — insiders must obtain prior approval before trading during permitted windows; (5) Blackout/closed periods — restrictions on trading around financial result announcements and other material events; (6) Training and sensitization — periodic training for insiders; (7) Quarterly reporting — submission of a compliance report to the exchange within specified timelines.', 'Penalties and enforcement: SEBI can impose penalties for contravention of the regulations, including disgorgement of profits and monetary penalties up to ten times the profit made or loss avoided. SEBI may also initiate action against intermediaries for failure to maintain adequate internal controls. SAT and criminal jurisdiction (under Section 24 of the SEBI Act) can also be invoked for serious violations.']

Depositories, Dematerialisation and Related Infrastructure

['A depository holds securities in electronic (dematerialized) form, analogous to a bank holding money in accounts. India has two depositories: National Securities Depository Limited (NSDL), established in 1996, and Central Depository Services (India) Limited (CDSL), established in 1999. Both are regulated by SEBI under the Depositories Act, 1996 and SEBI (Depositories and Participants) Regulations.', 'Dematerialisation (DEMAT): Dematerialisation converts physical securities into electronic form, eliminating risks of theft, forgery, loss of certificates, and delays in transfer. Process includes: (i) delivery of physical certificates to the depository participant (DP); (ii) verification by the DP; (iii) credit of demat account with equivalent electronic entries. Re-materialisation is also permitted on request. All listed equity shares are compulsorily dematerialized in India, and many debt and gilt securities have also been dematerialized.', 'Participants in the depository system: Depositories themselves, Depository Participants (DPs — banks, financial institutions, brokerage firms registered as DPs), Issuer/RTA (Registrar and Transfer Agent), and investors. DPs act as the interface between investors and the depository, providing account opening, trading settlement, and demat services.', "Rights and obligations: Beneficial owners have the right to receive all benefits (dividends, bonuses, rights, interest) flowing from securities held in demat form. Transfers are effected by instructions to the DP/depository. The depository maintains records of beneficial ownership and ensures settlement through electronic book-entry operations. Disputes regarding ownership are resolved through the depository's dispute-resolution mechanism and, if necessary, through SAT or civil courts.", 'Risk mitigation and investor protection: Depositories maintain segregation of client assets, maintain insurance or equivalent protection, and maintain a grievance-redressal mechanism. SEBI conducts periodic inspections of depositories and DPs. For systemic safety, RTAs and DPs are subject to fit-and-proper criteria, capital adequacy norms, and continuous compliance monitoring. Investors are advised to verify DP registration status and report fraudulent activities to SEBI.', "Recent developments: SEBI has promoted centralized depository operations, introduced e-voting and e-delivery instructions to reduce paperwork, and mandated stricter KYC norms for DP account openings. The integration of depository data with SEBI's surveillance systems has improved tracking of unusual trading patterns and concentration of holdings."]

Mutual Funds, Collective Investment Schemes and Portfolio Management Services

["Mutual Funds (MFs) are collective investment vehicles regulated by SEBI under the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996 (as amended). An MF pools money from investors and invests in securities (equity, debt, money market, hybrid) according to a stated investment objective. The structure involves: (a) the Sponsor, who promotes the MF; (b) the Asset Management Company (AMC), which manages the fund's portfolio and is registered with SEBI; (c) the Trustee, who holds the assets in trust for unit holders; and (d) the Custodian, who safeguards the securities. A Registrar and Transfer Agent (RTA) handles investor onboarding and record-keeping.", 'Key features of MF regulation: Every scheme must disclose its Objective, Risk-O-meter, Expense Ratio, and Performance Indicators. The AMC must maintain a minimum Net Worth (₹5 crore as base, increasing with AUM), sufficient independent directors on the Trustee board, and segregation of portfolios. Related-party transactions are tightly regulated, and conflicts of interest must be disclosed. Investor complaints are addressed through the grievance-redressal mechanism, and SEBI mandates periodic reporting on portfolio composition and valuation.', "Classification of schemes: Equity schemes (large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap, multi-cap, sectoral/thematic, ELSS), Debt schemes (liquid, ultra-short, short-duration, medium/long duration, corporate bond, banking & PSU, G-Sec, floater), Hybrid schemes (aggressive hybrid, balanced hybrid, conservative hybrid), Solution-oriented schemes (children's fund, pension fund), and Index/ETF schemes. Each category has distinct investment criteria and risk profiles that must be communicated clearly to investors.", 'Collective Investment Schemes (CIS): Regulated under SEBI (Collective Investment Schemes) Regulations, 1999 (as amended). A CIS pools money from multiple investors for collective investment in securities or other assets, promising returns to participants. Key differences from MFs include: CIS operators must be registered with SEBI, projects must be identifiable, and returns must be derived from profits of the project or appreciation of assets. Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) and Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs) are specialized CIS-like structures governed by separate SEBI regulations.', "Portfolio Management Services (PMS): Regulated by SEBI (Portfolio Managers) Regulations, 2020 (which consolidated and replaced earlier regulations). PMS involves discretionary or non-discretionary management of a client's securities portfolio for a fee. Minimum net-worth investment is prescribed (generally ₹50 lakhs, with partial pooling allowed under certain conditions). PMS entities must be registered, maintain adequate capital, disclose conflicts, maintain client portfolios separately, and file periodic reports. SEBI has tightened PMS regulations to improve transparency, reduce fee opacity, and enhance investor protection."]

Merchant Banking, Intermediaries and Market Infrastructure Institutions

['Merchant Banking: Merchant bankers are financial intermediaries that assist companies in raising capital through the issue process. They undertake activities including issue management (acting as lead manager/co-manager), portfolio management, advisory services (mergers, restructuring, buy-backs), and investor relations. SEBI regulates them under the SEBI (Merchant Bankers) Regulations, 1992. Category I, II, and III classifications were abolished and replaced by a unified registration framework; however, the scope of permissible activities still varies based on the registration category and capital adequacy.', 'Key merchant banking functions: Structuring the issue (public/further issue/private placement), preparing the prospectus/RHP, pricing the issue, conducting roadshows and marketing, managing the bidding process (for book-built issues), allotment and listing coordination, and post-issue compliance monitoring. Merchant bankers are accountable for due diligence and must certify that the prospectus contains true, correct, and complete information. SEBI can suspend or cancel registration for failures in due diligence or regulatory breaches.', "Other regulated intermediaries: Brokers and sub-brokers (SEBI (Stock Brokers and Sub-Brokers) Regulations, 1992), Underwriters (regulated through ICDR and inter se agreements), Credit Rating Agencies (SEBI (CRAs) Regulations, 1999 — CARE, CRISIL, ICRA, MOODY'S India, etc.), Portfolio Managers (SEBI (PMS) Regulations, 2020), Research Analysts (SEBI (RA) Regulations, 2014), and Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs — regulated under SEBI (FPI) Regulations, 2014, replaced FII/FPI dual regime). Each category has capital, fit-and-proper, compliance, and reporting requirements.", 'Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs): Beyond depositories, MIIs include stock exchanges (NSE, BSE, MSE, IEX, MCX), clearing corporations (NSCCL, ICCL, etc.), and central counterparties. SEBI regulates MIIs to ensure operational efficiency, risk management, and investor protection. MIIs must maintain adequate infrastructure, adopt robust technology platforms, implement circuit breakers, position limits, and margining systems, and provide transparent fee structures and grievance redressal.', 'Corporate Governance of intermediaries: SEBI requires intermediaries to have independent directors, audit committees, compliance officers, risk management frameworks, and periodic reporting. Fit-and-proper criteria include integrity, professional competence, financial soundness, and absence of regulatory/sanction history. Intermediaries must maintain adequate net worth, professional indemnity insurance, and segregation of client funds from corporate funds.']

Companies Act, 2013 Provisions on Share Capital, Debentures and Investor Protection

['While SEBI governs market conduct and intermediaries, the Companies Act, 2013 provides the corporate-law framework for share capital, debenture issuance, and investor protection that intersects with capital market activities.', 'Share Capital and Issue Procedures: Section 39 prohibits any offer to the public without a prospectus duly filed with the Registrar. Section 42 permits private placement subject to compliance with conditions (PAS form, allotment within 60 days, pledge restrictions). Section 43 defines different classes of shares — equity shares with voting rights, equity shares with differential voting rights (EVR), and preference shares. Section 47 prohibits differential voting rights except as permitted, and Section 48 restricts transferability of EVR shares during the existence of such rights.', 'Debentures and Secured Debt: Section 71 allows companies to issue debentures subject to articles and board resolution. Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) requirements have been modified — listed companies may create DRR only for non-convertible debentures, while unlisted companies face different conditions. Security creation on assets requires registration of charges with the ROC under Sections 77–78. Debenture Trustees are mandated for public issues of secured redeemable debentures under Section 72, with SEBI and Companies Act requirements converging for listed issues.', 'Buy-back of Securities: Section 68 permits buy-back out of free reserves, securities premium, or proceeds of shares/debentures, subject to a maximum of 25% of paid-up capital and financial ratio tests (debt-equity ratio not exceeding 2:1 post buy-back unless higher historically). The board must pass a special resolution, and buy-back must be completed within one year. SEBI buy-back regulations govern open-market and tender-offer routes for listed companies.', 'Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF): Established under Sections 125 and 124 read with IEPF Rules, 2016. Unclaimed dividends, matured deposits/debentures, refund amounts, and surplus from liquidation are transferred to IEPF after seven years. Claims for refunds are filed on IEPF portal by shareholders/depositors. The IEPF Authority (comprising Union Finance Minister and other members) oversees utilization for investor education and protection. Company Secretaries must ensure timely transfer and maintain records of unclaimed amounts.', 'Related Party Transactions and Audit Committee: Sections 188 and 177 require related-party transactions to be approved by the board/shareholders as applicable, and the audit committee to review and recommend. Listed companies must additionally comply with SEBI LODR norms. These provisions protect minority investors from tunneling and self-dealing by controlling shareholders or promoters.']

Regulation of Foreign Portfolio Investors, Custodians and Cross-Border Capital Flows

["Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs): Governed by SEBI (FPI) Regulations, 2014, which unified the earlier FII/FPI classification. FPIs are entities established or incorporated outside India that participate in India's securities market. Registration categories include Category I (qualified institutional buyers, sovereign wealth funds, multilateral agencies), Category II ( hedge funds, private equity, family offices, smaller institutions), and Category III (special situations investors). Key compliances include PAN linkage, KYC through registering entities (REs), annual certification, disclosure of beneficial ownership, adherence to FDI/securities caps (sectoral caps and overall 24% promoter + FPI combined limit in certain sectors), and reporting of changes in holdings.", 'Custodians: Regulated by SEBI (Custodians of Securities) Regulations, 2001. Custodians hold securities in safe custody, settle trades, collect dividends and interest, and provide ancillary services to FPIs and domestic institutions. They must be registered with SEBI, maintain minimum net worth, segregate client assets, and comply with reporting and inspection requirements. Custodians play a critical role in cross-border capital flows by enabling safe-holding and corporate action processing for foreign investors.', 'Cross-border capital flow architecture: RBI regulates foreign investment under the FEMA framework and external commercial borrowings (ECBs). SEBI regulates market conduct, listing, and investor protection. For ECBs, the Prudential Framework (rbi.org) sets eligibility, end-use, and maturity norms. FPI investment is tracked through the FPI registration system and daily reporting to RBI/SEBI. Portfolio investment limits (overall and sectoral) are jointly managed by SEBI, RBI, and Government of India policy.', "Reporting and surveillance obligations: FPIs and custodians must file daily/weekly/monthly holdings reports, disclose large stakes, and report any breach of limits promptly. SEBI's surveillance systems monitor unusual FPI trading, manipulation, and systemic risk. Cross-border transactions must comply with SEBI's sub-account structure, RE oversight, and lock-in/tax deductibility rules where applicable.", "Recent regulatory trends: SEBI has streamlined FPI registration and simplified KYC processes to enhance ease of doing business while tightening beneficial ownership disclosure. Custodianship norms have been aligned with global best practices (IOSCO principles). India's integration with global indices (MSCI, FTSE, S&P) has increased FPI inflows, making robust regulatory oversight essential for market stability."]

Risk Management, Compliance Obligations and Recent Regulatory Developments

['Risk Management for Listed Entities and Intermediaries: SEBI mandates that listed companies and intermediaries establish risk management frameworks. For listed entities, the Board must approve a risk management policy covering identification, measurement, monitoring, and mitigation of risks (financial, operational, strategic, reputational). Listed top-1000 entities by market capitalization must constitute a Risk Management Committee of the Board. Intermediaries (brokers, AMCs, PMS, DPs, etc.) must maintain dedicated risk management functions, stress-testing capabilities, capital adequacy buffers, and business continuity plans.', 'Corporate Governance and Disclosure Enhancements: SEBI has continuously upgraded listing obligations under LODR regulations. Recent emphases include: enhanced board composition with independent directors; mandatory rotation of auditors and audit firms; stricter related-party transaction scrutiny; ESG and sustainability reporting for top 1000 listed entities; disclosure of executive remuneration and key managerial personnel compensation; and improved investor communication through designated investor web portals. Company Secretaries are expected to drive compliance across these areas.', 'ESG and Sustainability Reporting: SEBI has mandated Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) for the top 1000 listed entities, aligning with global ESG disclosure standards. BRSR covers environmental, social, and governance metrics, requiring assurance readiness in future phases. This reflects the convergence of capital market regulation with sustainable finance principles and investor demand for ESG data.', 'Surveillance and Enforcement: SEBI employs technology-driven surveillance (MASS — Market Abuse Surveillance System) to detect manipulation, insider trading, and unusual trading activity in real time. Penalties include disgorgement, monetary penalties, debarment from market activities, and referral for prosecution. SEBI also uses enforcement adjudication proceedings under the SEBI Act, with SAT as appellate authority. Cooperation with international regulators (IOSCO-multilateral memorandum of understanding) enhances cross-border enforcement.', 'Emerging Developments (up to mid-2026): SEBI has been pushing for deeper retail participation through simplified mutual fund investments, enhanced demat accessibility, and stronger grievance redressal via SEBI SCORES. Regulatory focus has shifted toward market integrity, data analytics, investor education, and reducing information asymmetry. The ICSI syllabus expects candidates to track SEBI circulars, notifications, and consultation papers, as these reflect evolving practice. Candidates should note that while this chapter covers core statutory and regulatory provisions, examination questions frequently test recent circulars, specific numerical thresholds, and application-based scenarios rather than mere rote memorization.']

Important facts

  • SEBI was granted statutory powers through the SEBI Act, 1992, with headquarters in Mumbai and regional offices across India.
  • SCRA, 1956 provides the legal basis for regulation of stock exchanges, securities contracts, and prohibition of manipulative practices under Section 12B.
  • Book-built issues use a Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) for price discovery; anchor investors typically subscribe before the book opens to build market confidence.
  • A 25% threshold triggers mandatory disclosure under SAST Regulations; 26% open-offer threshold ensures minority shareholders get an exit opportunity on change of control.
  • UPSI is defined objectively — information a reasonable investor would consider material to price — covering financial results, M&A, buy-backs, management changes, and major contracts.
  • NSDL (est. 1996) and CDSL (est. 1999) are the two depositories; all listed equity shares must be held in dematerialized form.
  • MFs must maintain a minimum Net Worth of ₹5 crore for the AMC; sponsor, trustee, AMC, custodian, and RTA form the mutual fund ecosystem.
  • Merchant bankers are regulated under SEBI (Merchant Bankers) Regulations, 1992 and must perform due diligence on prospectus contents.
  • Section 68 of Companies Act, 2013 limits buy-back to 25% of paid-up capital in a financial year with a one-year completion window.
  • IEPF receives unclaimed dividends and matured amounts after seven years; claims are filed through the IEPF portal for refund.
  • FPI Regulations, 2014 unified the FII/FPI regime into a single registration framework with three categories based on investor profile.
  • Top 1000 listed entities must comply with BRSR (ESG reporting) and maintain a Risk Management Committee of the Board.
  • SEBI's enforcement mechanism includes adjudication, SAT appeal (Section 15K), and further appeal to Supreme Court on substantial questions of law.
  • Persons Acting in Concert (PAC) is a broad concept aggregating promoters, group entities, relatives, financiers, and advisors for takeover analysis.
  • PIT Regulations require a Designated Officer, Insider Register, Pre-clearance mechanism, Closed Period rules, and Quarterly Compliance Reports.
