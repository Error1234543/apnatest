1. Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Essentials of a Valid Contract

The Indian Contract Act, 1872 is the foundation of all commercial law in India. It governs every agreement that the law enforces. A contract is defined under Section 2(h) as 'an agreement enforceable by law'. The act is divided into two parts: Part II (Sections 1–75) deals with general principles of contract law, while Part B (Sections 76–167) deals with specific contracts such as indemnity, guarantee, bailment, pledge, agency, and quasi-contracts.

For an agreement to be enforceable as a contract, the following essentials must be satisfied:

• Offer and Acceptance (Sections 2(a), 2(b)): There must be a lawful offer by one party and a lawful acceptance by the other. The offer must be communicated, and the acceptance must be absolute and unconditional. A counter-offer kills the original offer. Acceptance must be communicated in a prescribed or reasonable manner.

• Lawful Consideration (Section 2(d)): Consideration means 'quid pro quo' — something in return. It must move at the desire of the promisor and may move from the promisee or any other person (except in England, where consideration must move only from the promisee). Consideration must be real, not illusory, and must be lawful. Past consideration is generally invalid unless it falls within the three exceptions recognized by Indian courts.

• Capacity of Parties (Section 11): Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority according to the law to which he is subject (18 years under the Majority Act, 1875; 21 years in cases of guardianship), is of sound mind, and is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject. Minors' agreements are void ab initio (Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose). Persons of unsound mind include those intermittent in soundness — contracts made during lucid intervals are valid.

• Free Consent (Sections 13–22): Consent is said to be free when not caused by coercion (Section 15), undue influence (Section 16), fraud (Section 17), misrepresentation (Section 18), or mistake (Section 20–22). A contract with defective consent is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was not free.

• Lawful Object and Consideration (Section 23): The object and consideration of an agreement must not be forbidden by law, fraudulent, injurious to the person or property of another, or deemed immoral or opposed to public policy. Any agreement with unlawful object or consideration is void.

• Certainty and Possibility of Performance (Sections 29–30): Agreements must not be vague or uncertain. Conditional agreements that are impossible to perform are void. Agreements by way of wager are void.

• Legal Formalities: Certain contracts must be in writing and registered (e.g., sale of immovable property, gift deeds). Oral contracts are generally valid except where statute requires writing.

2. Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Void, Voidable and Illegal Agreements

Understanding the classification of agreements is critical for commercial practice.

• Void Agreement (Section 2(d), 2(g)): An agreement not enforceable by law is a void agreement. Examples: agreement without consideration, agreement by minor, agreement in restraint of marriage, trade or legal proceedings, ambiguous agreements, wagering agreements. A void agreement is void from its very inception (void ab initio).

• Voidable Agreement (Section 2(i)): An agreement enforceable at the option of one or both the parties is a voidable contract. The aggrieved party may affirm or rescind the contract. If affirming, the contract remains valid; if rescinding, the party must restore benefits received. A voidable contract is valid until avoided.

• Illegal Agreement: An agreement whose object or consideration is unlawful. All illegal agreements are void, but not all void agreements are illegal. The distinction matters because in an illegal agreement, the court will not assist either party (ex dolo non oritur actio), whereas in a void agreement, restitution may be available under certain circumstances.

• Contingent Contracts (Sections 31–36): These are contracts to do or not to do something if some event collateral to the contract does or does not happen. Performance depends on the occurrence of an uncertain future event. If the event becomes impossible, the contract becomes void.

• Quasi-Contracts (Sections 68–72): Also called 'real contracts', these are obligations that resemble contracts but are not actually contracts. They are imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment. Categories include:
- Supply of necessities to incapable persons (Section 68)
- Payment by interested person (Section 69)
- Obligation of person enjoying benefit of non-gratuitous act (Section 70)
- Finder of goods (Section 71)
- Money paid by mistake or under coercion (Section 72)

3. Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Specific Contracts

The Act provides detailed rules for five categories of specific contracts.

• Indemnity (Sections 124–126): A contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused by the conduct of the promisor himself or of any other person. The promisee can sue for actual loss suffered. A contract of insurance is a contract of indemnity. Unlike a guarantee, indemnity involves only two parties — the indemnifier and the indemnified.

• Guarantee (Sections 126–140): A 'three-party' contract involving the surety, the principal debtor, and the creditor. The surety gives a promise to perform the promise or discharge the liability of the principal debtor in case of his default. Key points:
- Consideration: The forbearance to sue the principal debtor or any other valuable consideration is sufficient consideration for a guarantee.
- Revocation: A continuing guarantee may be revoked by notice for future transactions.
- Discharge of surety: Occurs by revocation, death of surety, variation of terms without surety's consent, creditor's act or omission impairing surety's eventual remedy, creditor not suing principal debtor within limitation period, and guaranty being conditional.
- Right of surety: Right of subrogation, right of contribution, right to securities.

• Bailment (Sections 148–171): Delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. Pledge is a special type of bailment where goods are delivered as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise.
- Duties of bailee: Take reasonable care, no unauthorized use, return goods, not to mix goods, delay in taking delivery.
- Duties of bailor: Disclose known defects, bear necessary expenses, indemnify for constructive eviction.

• Agency (Sections 148–238): 'Agency' is the relationship arising from a contract where one person (agent) acts on behalf of another (principal). Creation: express (written/verbal), implied (by conduct), apparent/ostensible (by Estoppel), ratification (Section 196), necessity.
- Agent's authority: May be general or specific. Must act within scope.
- Principal's liability: Liable for agent's acts done within authority.
- Effects of non-disclosure of principal: Agent personally liable; principal can be sued once disclosed.
- Revocation: By act of parties or by operation of law.
- Effect of termination: Section 212 protects third parties dealing in good faith before notice of revocation.

4. Sale of Goods Act, 1930

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 regulates the sale of goods and is based on the English Sale of Goods Act, 1893.

• Definition (Section 4): A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price. 'Goods' include all moveable property other than actionable claims and money.

• Key Distinctions:
- Sale: Transfer of property immediately; absolute contract.
- Agreement to Sell: Transfer of property at a future time or on conditions; executory contract.
- On a sale, risk passes with property (Section 26); on an agreement to sell, risk remains with the seller.

• Conditions and Warranties (Sections 12–14):
- Condition: A stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract. Breach gives rise to a claim for damages and also to treat the contract as repudiated.
- Warranty: A stipulation collateral to the main purpose. Breach gives rise to a claim for damages only, not to reject the goods.
- Note: Whether a clause is a condition or warranty depends on the intention of the parties and the terms of the contract.

• Rules for Transfer of Property (Section 17–23):
- Specific goods: Property passes when intended to pass. Rules include unconditional contract, ascertained goods, delivery of stated number, goods in deliverable state, approval or 'or pay' sales.
- Unascertained goods: Property passes when goods of the contract description are unconditionally appropriated.

• Transfer of Title (Section 27–30): Nemo dat quod non habet — no one can give better title than he has. Exceptions:
- Seller in possession after sale (Section 30)
- Buyer in possession (Section 29)
- Sale under voidable title (Section 28)
- Seller under rescinded right of lien
- Market overt (abolished in India)
- Estoppel (Section 27)

• Rights of Unpaid Seller (Sections 45–60):
- Against the goods: Right of lien, right of stoppage in transit, right of resale.
- Against the buyer personally: Suit for price, damages for non-acceptance.
- Lien exists when goods are sold without credit or when credit term has expired or buyer becomes insolvent.

5. Indian Partnership Act, 1932

Partnership is a vital business structure governed by this Act.

• Definition (Section 4): 'Partnership' is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Such persons individually are called 'partners' and the collective name is a 'firm'.

• Registration (Section 58–59): Registration is optional but highly advisable. An unregistered firm cannot sue a third party or a partner to enforce rights arising from the contract. However, third parties can still sue an unregistered firm. Sections 69 bars suits by unregistered firms but does not affect enforcement of rights by third parties.

• Nature of Partnership Rights and Duties (Sections 12–19):
- Joint and several liability: Each partner is jointly liable with the others for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner. After dissolution, remaining partners are liable for acts done before dissolution.
- Mutual agency: Every partner is both an agent and a principal.
- Sharing of profits: Presumption of equal sharing unless otherwise agreed.
- Duty of partners: To carry on business to greatest common advantage, to be just and faithful, to render true accounts, to indemnify for fraud, to indemnify for willful neglect.

• Admission, Retirement and Expulsion (Sections 31–33):
- Admission: Requires consent of all partners unless otherwise agreed.
- Retirement: With consent of all partners or as per agreement.
- Expulsion: Only if conferred by agreement and after inquiry and opportunity to be heard.

• Dissolution of Firm (Sections 40–44):
- Dissolution by agreement (Section 40)
- Compulsory dissolution (Section 41) — e.g., all partners adjudicated insolvent, all or all but one partner become of unsound mind, business becomes unlawful.
- Dissolution on happening of certain contingencies (Section 42) — expiry of term, completion of venture, death, insolvency.
- Dissolution by notice (Section 43) — partnership at will.

• Role of Adjudicated Insolvent Partner: His property vests in the Official Assignee/Trustee for distribution among creditors. The partner's interest in the firm can be attached and sold.

6. Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) Act, 2008

The LLP Act, 2008 introduced a new form of business entity that combines features of both partnerships and companies.

• Definition (Section 3): An LLP is a body corporate and a legal entity separate from its partners. It is an alternative corporate business vehicle that provides the flexibility of a partnership with the limited liability feature of a company.

• Key Features:
- Separate legal entity: LLP is distinct from its partners. It can own property, sue and be sued in its own name.
- Perpetual succession: Continuity of existence unaffected by change in partners.
- Limited liability: Partners are not personally liable for the debts of the LLP beyond their agreed contribution. However, partners are liable for their own wrongful acts.
- Flexible internal structure: Governed by an LLP Agreement (Section 4).
- Minimum two designated partners: At least one must be a body corporate or an individual resident in India.
- No requirement for minimum capital.

• Formation (Section 7–11): Incorporation document filed with the Registrar along with subscribed LLP agreement. Designated partners identified. Digital signatures required.

• Compliance Requirements:
- Filing of annual returns (Form 11) within 60 days of closure of financial year.
- Filing of statement of account and solvency (Form 8) within 30 days of close of 6 months.
- Maintenance of books of account.
- Appointment of Chartered Accountant for audit if turnover exceeds ₹40 lakhs or contributions exceed ₹25 lakhs.

• Conversion: Existing partnerships and companies can convert into LLPs under specified provisions.

7. Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

This Act governs negotiable instruments — documents entitled to priority in commercial transactions.

• Definition (Section 13): A 'negotiable instrument' means any instrument entitling the bearer to the possession thereof and allowing by virtue of delivery thereof to transfer the right to the property contained therein.

• Types of Negotiable Instruments (Section 5, 85–85A, 127, 138):
- Promissory Note (Section 4): Two parties — maker and payee. Unconditional promise to pay a certain sum.
- Bill of Exchange (Section 5): Three parties — drawer, drawee, and payee. Unconditional order to pay.
- Cheque (Section 6): A bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and payable demand. Three kinds: open cheque, crossed cheque (general/crossed & account payee).
- Hundi: Customary instrument, recognized under commercial usage.
- Currency Note and Bank Note: Not negotiable instruments.

• Holder and Holder in Due Course (Sections 8, 9):
- Holder: Any person entitled in his own name to possession and to recover the amount.
- Holder in due course: One who took the instrument for value, in good faith, without defect in title, before maturity.
- Privileges of HDC: Takes free from prior equities, defective titles, and personal defenses.

• Crossing of Cheque (Sections 123–131):
- General crossing: Two parallel lines with or without words 'and company' or 'not negotiable'. Paid only through a bank.
- Special crossing: Name of a specific banker. Must be paid only to that banker.
- 'Account Payee Only': Direction to collecting banker to credit only the account of the payee. Provides protection under Section 131 to the collecting banker.

• Dishonour and Liability (Sections 92–100):
- Dishonour by non-acceptance or non-payment.
- Notice of dishonour must be given within reasonable time.
- Liabilities: Drawer is liable to indorser and indorsee; each indorser liable to subsequent indorsees; acceptor primarily liable.

• Cheque Bouncing — Section 138: A crucial provision criminalizing the dishonour of cheques for insufficiency of funds. Essentials: (1) Cheque drawn by person in India, (2) in discharge of a legally enforceable debt or liability, (3) presented within 3 months of issue, (4) returned unpaid due to insufficient funds or exceeding arrangement, (5) payee gives written notice within 30 days of receipt of information, (6) drawer fails to make payment within 15 days of notice. Penalty: Up to 2 years imprisonment or fine up to twice the cheque amount or both. The proviso was amended vide Act 26 of 2018 to allow settlement between parties.

8. Information Technology Act, 2000

The IT Act, 2000 (amended in 2008) is the primary law dealing with cybercrime and e-commerce in India.

• Objective: To provide legal recognition for electronic commerce and faciliate e-governance by electronic records.

• Key Concepts:
- Electronic Record (Section 2(1)(r)): Data, text, image, sound stored, transmitted or received electronically.
- Digital Signature (Section 2(1)(ta)): Authentication of any electronic record by a subscriber using an electronic method.
- Public Key and Private Key: Pair of cryptographic keys used for encryption/decryption.
- Certifying Authority (Section 2(1)(c)): Any person authorized under Section 24 to provide certification services.
- Controller (Section 2(1)(d)): The Central Government appoints the Controller of Certifying Authorities.

• Digital Signature Certificates: Classes include Class 1 (for individuals/email verification), Class 2 (for members/directors), Class 3 (for high-security transactions).

• Key Offences (Chapter XI, Sections 43–49, 65–68, 72A):
- Section 43: Penalty for damaging computer systems, unauthorized access, data theft, introduction of viruses, denial-of-service attacks.
- Section 65: Tampering with computer source documents.
- Section 66: Computer-related offences — hacking, identity theft, cheat by personation, breach of confidentiality.
- Section 66C: Punishment for identity theft.
- Section 66D: Cheat by personation using computer resource.
- Section 66E: Violation of privacy.
- Section 66F: Cyber terrorism — punishable with imprisonment for life.
- Section 67: Publishing obscene material electronically.
- Section 67A: Publishing sexually explicit material.
- Section 67B: Publishing material containing children in sexual conduct.
- Section 72A: Disclosure of information in breach of lawful contract (by intermediaries).

• Intermediary Guidelines (Section 79): Safe harbour provision — intermediaries not liable for third-party content if they comply with due diligence under the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules.

• Cyber Crime Investigation: National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (cybercrime.gov.in), Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C).

9. Consumer Protection Act, 2019

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 replaced the 1986 Act and strengthens consumer rights in India.

• Definition of 'Consumer' (Section 2(7)): Any person who buys any goods or hires/avails any service for consideration. Includes any person who buys goods with the approval of the first buyer and any person who avails services with the approval of the first person who availed the service. Does not include a person who obtains goods for resale or for commercial purpose.

• Definitions:
- 'Defect' (Section 2(10)): Any fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard required to be maintained.
- 'Deficiency' (Section 2(11)): Any fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature and manner of performance of a service.
- 'Product Liability' (Section 2(24)): Responsibility of a product entity to compensate for any harm resulting from a defect in a product.
- 'Central Consumer Protection Authority' (CCPA) (Section 10): Body empowered to investigate violations of consumer rights and enforce consumer protection laws.

• Consumer Rights (Section 19):
- Right to be protected against marketing of goods/services hazardous to life and property.
- Right to be informed about quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price.
- Right to be heard and assured that consumer's interests receive due consideration.
- Right to seek redressal against unfair trade practices and restrictive trade practices.
- Right to consumer education.
- Right to healthy environment.

• Jurisdiction of Consumer Commissions:
- District Commission: Complaints where compensation claimed is up to ₹50 lakhs.
- State Commission: Complaints where compensation claimed is above ₹50 lakhs but up to ₹2 crores.
- National Commission: Complaints where compensation claimed exceeds ₹2 crores; also appeals against orders of State Commissions.
- All commissions have pecuniary jurisdiction extended by Central Government notification.

• E-filing and Mediation (Sections 31, 34):
- Consumers can file complaints online through the Consumer Complaints portal.
- Mediation cells set up with certified mediators.
- CCPA can impose penalty up to ₹10 lakhs and order recall of products, cease discontinuation of unfair practices, and pay compensation.

• Product Liability Action (Sections 84–91): A product liability action can be instituted by a consumer or a class of consumers. Product entities include manufacturer, service provider, seller, product service provider, and anyone in chain of supply. Defences include: development risks defence, state-of-the-art defence, compliance with statutory standards, and consumer's failure to follow instructions.

10. Competition Act, 2002

The Competition Act, 2002 (amended multiple times) was enacted to replace the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices (MRTP) Act, 1969. Its objective is to protect and promote competition in India.

• Key Institutions:
- Competition Commission of India (CCI): Apex regulatory body.
- Competition Appellate Tribunal (COMPAT): Now subsumed into the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) following the 2017 amendment.

• Anti-Competitive Agreements (Section 3):
- Horizontal agreements (cartels — Section 3(3)): Agreements between competitors at the same level of production/distribution. Includes price-fixing, limit on production/sales, market allocation, bid-rigging, and market sharing. Presumed to have appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC).
- Vertical agreements (Section 3(4)): Agreements between enterprises at different levels of production. Includes tie-in arrangements, exclusive supply agreements, exclusive distribution agreements, refusal to deal, and predatory pricing. Rule of reason applies — need to establish AAEC.
- Punishment for cartel: Three times the profit or 10% of turnover, whichever is higher.

• Abuse of Dominant Position (Section 4):
- Dominant position: Position of strength enjoyed by an enterprise in the relevant market enabling it to operate independently of competitive forces or affect its competitors/consumers.
- Factors for determination of dominance (Section 19(4)): market share, size and resources, competitive structure, dependency of consumers, barriers to entry, consumer base, cross-market power.
- Abusive practices: Predatory pricing, limiting production, refusing to deal, discriminatory pricing, tying of products, imposing unreasonable conditions.
- Penalties: Up to 10% of average turnover for three preceding years or imprisonment up to 3 years or both.

• Combination Regulation (Sections 5–6):
- Acquisitions, mergers, amalgamations, and integrations that cross threshold limits require CCI approval.
- Thresholds are based on asset turnover and group-level assets/turnover (as amended periodically).
- Failure to notify is punishable with 1% of total assets/turnover.

• Competition Advocacy (Section 49): CCI can give opinions to government on policy matters and recommend reforms.

11. Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA)

FEMA 1999 replaced the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1974. Its aim is to facilitate external trade and payments and promote orderly development of foreign exchange market.

• Key Definitions (Section 2):
- 'Foreign Exchange' includes foreign currency and any instrument payable in foreign currency.
- 'Person' includes a firm, HUF, company, association, trust, body corporate, or government.
- 'Resident' (Section 2(t)): A person who is in India for 182 days or more during the preceding financial year or who is a body corporate/incorporated in India. Also includes NRIs and OCIs as specified.

• Current Account Transactions (Section 5): Permissible without prior approval of RBI for bona fide current account transactions such as trade, payment of professional fees, maintenance of dependants abroad, travel, etc. Prohibited: remittance for betting/gambling, margin calls on international exchanges, illegal exports, imports not permitted by customs law.

• Capital Account Transactions (Section 6): Permitted only as prescribed by regulations. Capital account transactions include investment in securities, loans, guarantees, remittance for acquisition of immovable property abroad, import/export of capital goods, capital contribution to foreign entities.

• Repatriation of Foreign Exchange (Section 8): Every person authorized to hold foreign exchange must sell it to authorized dealers within specified time.

• Offences and Penalties (Sections 11–18):
- Contravention of Sections 5, 6, 7 is punishable with monetary penalty up to thrice the sum involved where quantifiable, or up to ₹2 lakh where not quantifiable.
- If contravention continues, additional penalty up to ₹5,000 per day.
- Criminal liability: Confiscation of property involved in contravention.
- Appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal (SAT — Securities Appellate Tribunal).

• Adjudication: Director of Enforcement or Authorized Officer adjudicates. Appeal to SAT, then to High Court on question of law.

• RBI Powers: Issue directions, inspect books, require information, compound offences.

12. Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA)

PMLA was enacted to prevent money laundering and provide for confiscation of property derived from or involved in money laundering.

• Definition of Money Laundering (Section 3): Whoever directly or indirectly attempts to indulge, knowingly assists, or knowingly is a party or is actually involved in any process or activity connected with the proceeds of crime and projecting it as untainted property commits money laundering.

• Scheduled Offences (Section 2(w)): All offences listed in Schedule to the Act, including offences under IPC, NDPS Act, POCSO, IP Act, Prevention of Corruption Act, Excise Act, Customs Act, Banking Offences, SEBI Act, FEMA, and many others. The schedule is notified by the Central Government.

• Proceeds of Crime (Section 2(u)): Property derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, by any person as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence.

• Enforcement Directorate (ED): The enforcing authority under PMLA. Powers include investigation, arrest, attachment of property, seizure, search, and prosecution.

• Key Provisions:
- Section 12: Reporting companies (banks, financial institutions, intermediaries) must maintain records, verify client identity, and report suspicious transactions to FIU-IND.
- Section 17: Attachment of property — provisional attachment order by Adjourding Authority.
- Section 19: Arrest — Director/Authorized Officer can arrest after recording reasons in writing.
- Section 20: Search and seizure powers.
- Section 24: Burden of proof shifts to accused — presumption of guilt once facts are established.
- Section 44: Extra-territorial application.

• Offences and Penalties (Section 4): Punishable with rigorous imprisonment for 3–7 years and fine up to ₹5 lakh. If related to Schedule III offences (more serious), imprisonment may extend to 10 years.

• Compounding (Section 45): Offences are cognizable and non-bailable. Bail requires satisfaction of conditions regarding flight risk and tampering with evidence.

13. Right to Information Act, 2005

The RTI Act empowers citizens to seek information from public authorities, promoting transparency and accountability.

• Object (Section 1): To provide for setting out the practical regime of right to information for citizens to secure access to information under the control of public authorities.

• 'Public Authority' (Section 2(h)): Any authority or body or institution of self-government established or constituted by or under the Constitution, by any other law made by Parliament or State Legislature, or by notification/order of the appropriate Government.

• 'Information' (Section 2(f)): Any material in any form including records, documents, memoranda, e-mails, opinions, advisories, press releases, circulars, orders, contracts, permits, samples, models, data material held in any electronic form.

• 'Right to Information' (Section 2(j)): The right to inspect work records, take notes, extracts, certified copies, and take samples.

• Obligations of Public Authorities (Section 4):
- Maintain all records duly indexed and catalogued.
- Publish certain particulars of its functions, responsibilities, officer details.
- Provide reasons for its decisions in writing.
- Publish regularly all pertinent facts relevant to transparency.

• Procedure for Seeking Information (Section 6):
- Application in writing or through electronic means in Hindi or English or regional language.
- Fee of ₹10 (additional fees for photocopying, sampling, etc.).
- Time limit: 30 days for normal information; 48 hours if concerning life/liberty.
- If filed with wrong authority, must be transferred within 5 days and intimation given to applicant.

• Exemptions (Sections 8, 9):
- Information prejudicial to sovereignty, integrity, security of India.
- Information which has been expressly forbidden or which cannot be compiled by courts.
- Cabinet papers including records of deliberations of Council of Ministers.
- Commercial confidence, trade secrets, intellectual property.
- Information available to Parliament or State Legislature.
- Information causing breach of privilege of Parliament/State Legislature.
- Personal information unrelated to public activity or which would invade privacy unjustifiably.
- Information received in confidence from foreign government.

• Appeals (Sections 18, 19):
- First appeal to senior official within the public authority within 30 days.
- Second appeal to Information Commission — Central Information Commission (CIC) or State Information Commission (SIC).

• Penalties (Section 20): Public Information Officer can be penalized ₹250 per day up to ₹25,000 for unreasonable delay, denying information, providing incorrect/incomplete/malafide information.

14. Important Legal Concepts and Recent Developments

Several important legal developments and concepts are frequently tested in CS exams.

• Corporate veil and its piercing:
- General rule: Company is a separate legal entity from its members (Salomon v. Salomon). The corporate veil separates the company's personality from its members.
- Exceptions (veil piercing): Fraud or improper conduct, statutory provisions, agency relationship, enemy alien, holding and subsidiary companies (group enterprises), single economic unit doctrine, tax evasion, circumvention of legal obligation, national security.

• Unfair Trade Practices and Restrictive Trade Practices:
- Unfair Trade Practices (now covered under Consumer Protection Act 2019 and Competition Act): misleading advertisements, false representations, bait advertising, tie-in sales, adulteration, hoarding.
- Restrictive Trade Practices (largely absorbed into Competition Act): practices that cause admission to monopoly or eliminate competition.

• Legal Remedies for Breach of Contract:
- Rescission (Section 27, 39, 56): Right to avoid contract.
- Damages (Section 73–75): Compensation for loss or damage arising naturally from breach or known to parties at time of contract. Consequential damages are recoverable if known. Nominal, exemplary, and compensation damages exist.
- Specific Performance (Specific Relief Act, 1963): Court orders performance of contract when damages are inadequate.
- Injunction: Court order restraining a party from doing something.
- Quantum Meruit: Recovery for work done when contract becomes void or is discharged.
- Restitution (Section 65, 66): Obligation to restore benefits when agreement is discovered to be void.

• Recent amendments and updates (subject to latest notifications):
- Companies Act 2013 continuous amendments on CSR, NCLT/NCLAT jurisdiction.
- Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code amendments 2019, 2020, 2021.
- FEMA amendments on residential property acquisition by NRIs.
- PMLA amendments expanding definition of 'proceeds of crime' and enhancing penalties.
- Contract reforms: Indian Contract (Second Amendment) Bill proposals regardingliquidated damages, third-party rights, and quasi-contracts.

Important facts

  • A minor's agreement is void ab initio (Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose, 1903).
  • Section 138 of NI Act requires written notice within 30 days of dishonour; drawer gets 15 days to pay.
  • In a Sale of Goods contract, risk passes with property, not necessarily with delivery.
  • An unregistered partnership firm cannot sue third parties or partners for enforcement of rights (Section 69).
  • LLP is a body corporate with perpetual succession and limited liability for partners.
  • Holder in due course takes free from all prior equities and defects in title.
  • CCP under Consumer Protection Act 2019 can order recall of products and impose penalties up to ₹10 lakhs.
  • Horizontal agreements (cartels) under Competition Act are presumed to have AAEC — no need to prove impact.
  • Under FEMA, current account transactions are freely permissible; capital account transactions are regulated.
  • PMLA applies to all scheduled offences; burden of proof shifts to accused once predicate offence is established.
  • RTI time limit is 30 days; for life/liberty matters, it is 48 hours.
  • Section 66F IT Act: Cyber terrorism is punishable with imprisonment for life.
  • Unpaid seller's lien is lost on delivery of goods to carrier or on waiver.
  • Carriage of goods by sea: seller's right of stoppage in transit continues until goods are delivered to consignee.
  • Corporate veil may be pierced in cases of fraud, statutory mandate, agency, or tax evasion.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing 'void' with 'voidable' — a void contract is unenforceable from the beginning; a voidable contract is valid until avoided by the aggrieved party.
  • Thinking an illegal agreement is always void — all illegal agreements are void, but not all void agreements are illegal. Illegality involves moral turpitude.
  • Assuming registration of partnership is mandatory — it is not mandatory but highly recommended for evidentiary purposes and enforceability.
  • Not distinguishing between condition and warranty — breach of condition allows rejection of goods; breach of warranty allows only damages.
  • Mistaking 'sale' for 'agreement to sell' regarding risk passage — risk passes with property, not delivery.
  • Forgetting that Section 138 of NI Act requires the cheque to be presented within 3 months of issue; beyond that, the protection does not apply.
  • Assuming the holder of a negotiable instrument is always a holder in due course — HDC requires value, good faith, and absence of defect in title.
  • Not recognizing that in a guarantee, the surety's liability is co-extensive with the principal debtor's unless otherwise stated (Section 128).
  • Confusing 'dominant position' with 'abuse of dominant position' — having dominance is not illegal; abusing it is.
  • Thinking PMLA applies only to banking sector — it covers all scheduled offences across multiple statutes.
  • Overlooking that CCP under CPA 2019 has suo motu powers to investigate violations of consumer rights.
  • Assuming FEMA offences attract imprisonment — generally FEMA contraventions attract only monetary penalties.
