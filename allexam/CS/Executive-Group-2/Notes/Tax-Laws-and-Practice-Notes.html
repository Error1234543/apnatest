Overview of Taxation System in India

India follows a dual taxation structure where both the Union Government and State Governments have concurrent powers to levy taxes on different objects. The Constitution of India distributes taxing powers under List I (Union List), List II (State List), and List III (Concurrent List) of the Seventh Schedule. Direct taxes are levied and collected by the Central Government, while indirect taxes may be shared between Centre and States. Under Article 246 read with the Seventh Schedule, Parliament has exclusive power to make laws on customs, central excise, corporate tax, income-tax other than agricultural income, and goods and services tax on inter-state trade or commerce. State Legislatures have power over state taxes on sale/purchase of goods, VAT/GST on intra-state supply of goods and services, excise duties on medicinal preparations, and taxes on entry of goods.

Direct taxes are imposed directly on the income or wealth of the assessee and the burden cannot be shifted to another person. These include income-tax under the Income-tax Act, 1961, securities transaction tax, wealth-tax (now abolished w.e.f. 2015-16), and gift-tax (subsumed under income-tax). Indirect taxes are levied on goods and services; the burden can be passed on. Major indirect taxes include GST (Central GST, State/UT GST, and Integrated GST) under the CGST Act 2017, IGST Act 2017, SGST Acts of respective states, and the GST (Compensation to States) Act 2017. Additional indirect taxes include customs duty under the Customs Act, 1962, and the remaining non-GST Central Excise and Service Tax provisions that were largely subsumed.

The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016 introduced GST in India, replacing a complex web of central and state indirect taxes including excise duty, service tax, VAT/Sales Tax, octroi, entry tax, luxury tax, and many cesses. GST is considered an integrated tax comprising CGST, SGST/UTGST, and IGST. A GST Council was constituted under Article 246A to recommend principles of levy, incidence, and allocation of GST. The GST regime became operational from 1 July 2017.

Income Tax — Basic Concepts and Residential Status

Income-tax in India is governed primarily by the Income-tax Act, 1961, which applies throughout India except Jammu & Kashmir. The Act defines key concepts such as 'income', 'assesse', 'assessment year', 'previous year', and 'total income'. The financial year (April to March) is called the previous year, and the year immediately following is the assessment year in which the income of the previous year is assessed and tax is charged.

Residential status is fundamental to determining the extent of total income liable to tax. An individual is resident in India in any previous year if he satisfies at least one of the two basic conditions: (a) he is in India during the previous year for a period of 182 days or more, or (b) he is in India during the previous year for a period of 60 days or more and has been in India for 365 days or more during the 4 years immediately preceding the previous year. For Indian citizens leaving India for employment abroad or as members of crew of an Indian ship, the 60-day period is extended to 182 days.

Under the amended provisions effective from FY 2020-21, an Indian citizen who is not liable to tax in any other country/territory and has total income other than foreign source income exceeding Rs. 15 lakh during the previous year shall be deemed resident in India if he is in India for 120 days or more but less than 182 days during the previous year, provided he has been in India for 182 days or more during the 4 years immediately preceding.

Once a person is established as resident, further classification determines scope of total income:
- Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR): Worldwide income is taxable.
- Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR): Indian income plus foreign income from business controlled in or profession set up in India is taxable.
- Non-Resident (NR): Only Indian-sourced income is taxable.

For companies, an Indian company is always resident. A foreign company is resident if its place of actual management wholly/partially in India during the previous year; otherwise it is non-resident. The concept of place of actual management refers to the place where the real control and policy-making decisions of the company are made.

Heads of Income and Computation of Total Income

The Income-tax Act classifies income under five heads for computation of total income under Section 14. The order of set-off and adjustment follows a statutory hierarchy:

(1) Income from Salaries [Section 15-17]: Salary includes wages, pension, Gratuity, leave salary, perquisites, profit in lieu of salary, and advance of salary. Standard deduction of Rs. 50,000 is available. Allowable deductions include family pension (50% or Rs. 15,000, whichever is less), contributory Provident Fund (exempt if recognized). Tax on perquisites and certain salaries is deductible at source.

(2) Income from House Property [Section 22-27]: Annual value of property consisting of any buildings or land appurtenant thereto, used for residential/commercial purposes, is chargeable to tax. For let-out property, standard deduction of 30% of annual value is available, along with interest on borrowed capital (up to Rs. 2,00,000 for self-occupied). For self-occupied property, annual value is taken as nil, and interest on housing loan is deductible up to Rs. 2,00,000. Municipal taxes paid are deductible from gross annual value.

(3) Profits and Gains of Business or Profession [Section 28-43D]: Includes profits of any business/profession carried on during the previous year. Permissible deductions include repairs, insurance, rent, rates, taxes, rates, interest on borrowed capital, depreciation, amortisation, and business expenses. Disallowances under Sections 37(2B), 40(a), 40A, etc., must also be noted. Specified professional fees are admissible under Section 36(1)(v).

(4) Capital Gains [Section 45-55]: On transfer of a capital asset, short-term capital gain or long-term capital gain is computed. For equity shares/funds, LTCG applies if held for more than 12 months (Section 112A) and STCG if held for less than 12 months (Section 111A). For other assets, LTCG applies if held for more than 24 months. Indexation benefit is available for LTCG on immovable property/debentures/fund units.

(5) Income from Other Sources [Section 56-59]: Residuary head of income including dividends, interest income, lottery winnings, gift income exceeding Rs. 50,000 in a year, and any other receipts not chargeable under any other head. Deduction for interest on money borrowed for investment is allowed here.

After computing income under each head, losses under one head may be set off against profits/gains under another head within the same category (cross head-set off subject to restrictions). Brought forward losses under Section 72 (business loss), Section 74 (capital loss), Section 71B (house property loss), and Section 91 (foreign tax credit) may also be carried forward subject to conditions.

Deductions from Gross Total Income (Chapter VI-A)

Chapter VI-A of the Income-tax Act provides deductions from Gross Total Income to arrive at Total Income. These deductions encourage savings and investments and support socially desirable activities.

Key deductions include:
- Section 80C: Investments in life insurance premium, PPF, NSC, ELSS, 5-year deposit schemes, tuition fees, Principal repayment of home loan, EPF, and others — up to Rs. 1,50,000.
- Section 80CCD(1) and 80CCD(1B): Additional deduction of Rs. 50,000 for contributions to NPS over and above the Rs. 1,50,000 limit under Section 80C. This is additional and separate.
- Section 80D: Medical insurance premium for self, family, and parents. Rs. 25,000 for self/family (Rs. 50,000 for senior citizen parents), plus additional Rs. 25,000 or Rs. 50,000 for parents depending on their age.
- Section 80E: Interest on education loan — no upper limit, but must be for higher education of the assessee, spouse, or children.
- Section 80TTA: Interest on savings bank account up to Rs. 10,000 (applicable to individuals and HUF only, not senior citizens who fall under 80TTB).
- Section 80TTB: Interest income from deposits with banking companies/Post Office for senior citizens — up to Rs. 50,000.
- Section 80G: Donations to approved funds, charitable institutions — 50% or 100% of donation as specified, subject to qualifying limits.
- Section 80GG: Rent paid by assessee not receiving HRA — up to Rs. 5,000 per month or 25% of total income or actual rent paid minus 15% of total income, whichever is least.
- Section 80GGC: Donations by bodies/corporations other than individuals/HUF — 100% deduction without any qualifying limit.
- Section 80HHC/80HHD: Export-oriented deductions (largely subsumed/restructured).
- Section 80IB to 80U: Various deductions for specific sectors (SEZs, infrastructure, tourism, industrial parks, housing, etc.) and disabilities.

Important: All these deductions together reduce Gross Total Income to arrive at Total Income. However, deductions under Chapter VI-A are not allowable to non-residents on their foreign-source income.

Goods and Services Tax (GST) — Framework and Levy

GST is a destination-based indirect tax levied on the supply of goods and services. It subsumed multiple taxes and eliminated the cascading effect of taxes. The constitutional amendment inserted Article 246A, giving concurrent power to Parliament and State Legislatures to make laws with respect to GST. The GST Council under Article 246A is the apex body for determining GST rates, exemptions, and threshold limits.

Structure of GST:
- CGST (Central GST): Levied by the Union Government on intra-state supply. Collected by the Centre.
- SGST (State GST): Levied by the State Government on intra-state supply. Collected by the State.
- UTGST (Union Territory GST): Applicable in union territories without legislature.
- IGST (Integrated GST): Levied by the Union Government on inter-state supply and imports. Collected by the Centre and allocated to the consuming state.

Threshold exemptions: Suppliers with aggregate turnover up to Rs. 40 lakh (Rs. 20 lakh for special category states) are exempt from GST registration, though voluntary registration is permitted. Special category states have a lower threshold.

Tax rates under GST are classified under four slabs — 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28% — along with items that are either nil-rated or exempt. Items in the 28% slab may attract a compensation cess in addition to the GST rate.

Zero-rated supplies under Section 16 of the IGST Act include exports of goods/services and supplies to SEZ developers/users. The exporter or SEZ developer can supply under bond/LUT without payment of IGST and claim refund of input tax. Alternatively, they can pay IGST and claim refund.

GST applies to all supplies of goods and services unless specifically excluded. Supply includes sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease, or disposition made for consideration. Composite supply (Section 2(30)) means a supply made by a taxable person consisting of two or more supplies naturally bundled together in the ordinary course of business, with one being the principal supply. Mixed supply (Section 2(74)) means any combination of two or more independent supplies made in conjunction but not naturally bundled, where the supply attracting the highest rate of tax applies to the entire mixed supply.

Input Tax Credit under GST

Input Tax Credit (ITC) is the cornerstone of GST, designed to eliminate the cascading effect of taxes. Under Section 16 of the CGST Act, every registered person is entitled to take credit of input tax charged on any supply of goods or services used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of his business. The credit chain flows from supplier to recipient, from recipient to ultimate consumer.

Eligibility conditions for claiming ITC under Section 16:
- The person must be registered under the GST Act.
- The person must have received the goods or services.
- The supplier must have actually paid the tax to the government (as evidenced by the tax invoice and GSTR-2B/2A).
- The tax invoice must contain all prescribed particulars.
- The person must have filed the required returns (typically GSTR-3B and GSTR-1).
- The tax charged must have been actually paid to the government by the supplier.

Restrictions on ITC under Section 17:
- ITC cannot be claimed on goods/services used for making exempt supplies.
- Blocked credits under Section 17(5) include motor vehicles (other than those used for specified purposes), food, beverages, beauty treatment, health, fitness, and cosmetic surgery, building materials/construction of immovable property (except plant and machinery), goods sent for repair, and certain membership fees of clubs/health centres.
- ITC on capital goods is restricted — only a proportionate amount may be claimed.
- ITC reversal is required when goods on which credit was taken are disposed of or lost/stolen.

Mechanism: ITC is claimed through the GST portal. The auto-populated ITC statement is available in Form GSTR-2B, generated from suppliers' GSTR-1 filings. Matching and mismatching of invoices affects the availability of credit. Annual reconciliation with the supplier's books is necessary.

Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM) under Section 9(3): When notified categories of goods/services are received from an unregistered supplier, the recipient is liable to pay GST instead of the supplier. Examples include legal services, GST advocate services, services supplied by a director to the body corporate, and services by an SAC codified as 999311. This ensures tax leakage is plugging the credit chain.

Transfer Pricing and International Taxation

Transfer pricing deals with the pricing of transactions between associated enterprises (AEs). The objective is to ensure that artificial transfer prices do not erode the tax base of a country. Section 92BA of the Income-tax Act defines 'international commercial transaction' and 'domestic commercial transaction' with an AE.

Arm's Length Price (ALP):
Section 92C prescribes methods to determine the ALP of an international commercial transaction or domestic transaction with an AE. The Transfer Pricing Officer (TPO) may use any of the following methods:
- Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) Method: Compares the price charged in the controlled transaction with the price charged in a comparable uncontrolled transaction.
- Resale Price Method (RPM): Starting from the price at which purchased products are resold to an independent party, an appropriate resale margin is deducted to arrive at the price at which the product should have been purchased from the AE.
- Cost Plus Method (CPM): An appropriate cost-plus margin is added to the costs incurred by the AE to arrive at the price.
- Profit Split Method (PSM): Profits are split based on the value contributed by each AE, considering functional analysis.
- Discounted Cash Flow Method (DCF Method): Projects future cash flows and discounts them to present value.
- Net Margin Method: Compares the net margin relative to an appropriate base.
- Any other method prescribed by CBDT rules.

The TPO selects the most appropriate method having regard to the nature of the transaction, class of transaction, functions performed, assets employed, and risks assumed.

Penalties:
- Failure to maintain documentation under Section 92D: Penalty of Rs. 1,00,000.
- Under-reporting due to failure to report international transaction: 200% of tax shortfall under Section 270A.
- Disallowed deduction for domestic transaction with AE: Penalty equivalent to the amount disallowed.

Threshold for maintenance of documentation: As per Section 92D read with Rule 10D, every assessee entering into an international transaction or domestic transaction with AE whose aggregate value exceeds the prescribed threshold (currently Rs. 10 crore for international transactions and Rs. 5 crore for domestic transactions) must obtain a CA certificate in Form 3CEB regarding compliance with ALP provisions.

Specified Domestic Transactions (SDT): Certain specified domestic transactions (Section 92CA) can also be referred to the TPO. However, as per the Finance Act amendments, the mechanism for SDT has been significantly modified.

Wealth Tax and Gift Tax

Wealth-tax under the Wealth-tax Act, 1957 was levied on the net wealth of individuals, HUFs, and companies at the rate of 0.1%. However, the Finance Act, 2015 abolished wealth-tax w.e.f. Assessment Year 2016-17. The rationale was that wealth-tax was largely ineffective and created compliance burdens. Consequently, this provision is now part of the historical framework, and CS students should understand the erstwhile law and its abolition. Prior to abolition, assets included immovable property, motor vehicles, ornaments, archaeological collections, jewelry, precious stones, gold coins, urban land, and specified shares/securities. Exclusions were agricultural land, HUF assets used in business, and assets forming part of a business trust.

Gift-tax was originally under the Gift-tax Act, 1958. This Act was also repealed w.e.f. 1 April 1998, and gifts are now chargeable to tax under Section 56(2)(x) of the Income-tax Act, 1961. Under Section 56(2)(x), any sum of money or property received without consideration or for inadequate consideration by an individual or HUF is includible in total income, subject to exemptions. Key provisions include:
- Money received: If aggregate value exceeds Rs. 50,000 in a year, the excess is taxed as income from other sources.
- Property received: If the fair market value (FMV) exceeds Rs. 50,000, the excess of FMV over the consideration paid is taxable.
- Exceptions: Gifts received from relatives (as defined under Section 56(2)(x)) are fully exempt. Relatives include spouse, brother/sister, lineal ascendant/descendant, and their spouses.
- Gifts of immovable property: Stamp duty valuation is taken as FMV if the stamp duty value exceeds the consideration paid.
- Specified securities/shares: For unlisted shares, FMV is determined as per Section 56(2)(vii)(b) using prescribed methods (net asset value or income capitalization method).

These provisions ensure that substantial gratuitous transfers are brought to tax, while genuine family gifts remain exempt.

Tax Administration and Compliance Procedures

The Income-tax Act provides a comprehensive framework for assessment, collection, and enforcement of taxes. Key aspects relevant to CS professionals include:

Assessment Types:
- Self-Assessment (Section 140): Every assessee computes own tax liability, pays the tax due, and files return. This is the default assessment mechanism.
- Regular Assessment (Section 143): The Assessing Officer (AO) processes the return and issues intimation of error, adjustment, or demand/refund.
- Best Judgment Assessment (Section 144): Where the assessee fails to file return, fails to comply with notice, or the AO is not satisfied with the accounts, the AO makes assessment to the best of his judgment.
- Scrutiny Assessment (Section 143(2)): Where the AO considers it necessary to verify the correctness of the return, he issues a notice requiring the assessee to provide evidence.
- Completion of Assessment (Section 147): If the AO has reason to believe that any income has escaped assessment, he may reassess such income, subject to time limits.

Tax Audit (Section 44AB):
A tax audit is mandatory for certain classes of assessees. For business/profession, audit is required if turnover/gross receipts exceed the prescribed limit (Rs. 1 crore for business, Rs. 50 lakh for profession — higher limits if cash transactions are below 5%). The audit must be conducted by a Chartered Accountant and reported in the specified forms (Form 3CA/3CB and Form 3CD). Digital signing and filing of audit report is mandatory.

Advance Rulings:
The Authority for Advance Rulings (AAR) and the Special Bench of AAR provides binding rulings on questions relating to liability to tax, determination of income, and applicability of provisions. Rulings are binding on the applicant and the concerned department. Appeals lie before the High Court on substantial questions of law.

Appeals: An appeal against an order of the AO lies first to the Commissioner of Income-tax (Appeals) [CIT(A)] under Section 246A, then to the Income-tax Appellate Tribunal [ITAT] under Section 253, and finally to the High Court under Section 260A on substantial questions of law. The Finance Act, 2017 restructured the appellate mechanism.

Penalties: Various penalties are provided under Chapter XXI of the Income-tax Act for concealment of income (Section 270A — 50% to 200% of tax shortfall), failure to furnish return (Section 234F), failure to deduct or pay TDS (Section 201), and other defaults. Penalties are quasi-criminal in nature and require proof of mens rea in many cases.

Important facts

  • India follows a dual taxation structure with concurrent powers distributed under the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution.
  • GST was introduced w.e.f. 1 July 2017 through the 101st Constitutional Amendment, replacing excise, service tax, VAT, octroi, entry tax, luxury tax, and various cesses.
  • GST Council under Article 246A is the apex body with representation from Centre and States for making GST policy decisions.
  • Four main GST slabs exist: 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%, plus items that are nil-rated or exempt. A compensation cess applies on select goods in the 28% slab.
  • Composite supply attracts the tax rate of the principal supply; mixed supply attracts the rate of the highest-taxed component.
  • Input Tax Credit is available only when the supplier has actually paid the tax to the government and the recipient has filed required returns.
  • Blocked credits under Section 17(5) of CGST Act include motor vehicles (for personal use), food/beverages, beauty treatments, construction of immovable property (except plant/machinery), and membership of health/clubs.
  • Reverse Charge Mechanism under Section 9(3) places the GST liability on the recipient for notified categories of goods/services from unregistered persons.
  • Arm's Length Price (ALP) must be determined under Section 92C for international commercial transactions and specified domestic transactions with associated enterprises.
  • Five transfer pricing methods are prescribed: CUP, RPM, CPM, PSM, and DCF/Net Margin Method. The Most Appropriate Method (MAM) must be selected after considering functional analysis.
  • Wealth-tax was abolished w.e.f. AY 2016-17 (Finance Act, 2015). Gift-tax Act was repealed w.e.f. 1998.
  • Gifts from relatives are exempt under Section 56(2)(x); gifts from non-relatives exceeding Rs. 50,000 in a year are taxable as income from other sources.
  • Tax audit is mandatory under Section 44AB for businesses with turnover exceeding Rs. 1 crore and professions with gross receipts exceeding Rs. 50 lakh (with enhanced thresholds for low cash transactions).
  • Form 3CEB certified by a Chartered Accountant is required for reporting compliance with transfer pricing provisions when aggregate value of international/domestic transactions exceeds prescribed limits.
  • Residential status determines the scope of total income: ROR → worldwide income taxable; RNOR → India + controlled foreign business income taxable; NR → only Indian income taxable.
  • Standard deduction of Rs. 50,000 is available under the head Salaries. Deduction of Rs. 50,000 under Section 80CCD(1B) is over and above the Rs. 1,50,000 limit under Section 80C.
  • Appeals follow a hierarchy: CIT(A) → ITAT → High Court. AAR rulings are binding on the applicant and the department.
  • Short-term capital gains on equity/shares (held <12 months) are taxed at 15% under Section 111A; Long-term capital gains (held >12 months) on equity are taxed at 10% under Section 112A (above Rs. 1 lakh exemption).

Formulas and relationships

Total Income = Gross Total Income − Deductions under Chapter VI-A (Sections 80C to 80U)
Net Wealth = Total Assets (excluding excluded assets) − Total Liabilities (excluding liabilities for excluded assets)
Gain on Transfer (Capital Gains) = Full Value of Consideration − (Cost of Acquisition × indexation factor) − Expenditure wholly/exclusently on transfer
Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG) on Immovable Property = Full Value of Consideration − Indexed Cost of Acquisition − Expenses on Transfer
Indexed Cost of Acquisition = Cost of Acquisition × (CII of Year of Transfer / CII of Year of Acquisition)
Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG) on Equity = Sale Consideration − Cost of Acquisition − Expenses on Transfer
Arm's Length Price (ALP) = Price at which the transaction would have been carried out between independent parties under similar circumstances
Adjusted ALP (for penalty purpose) = Reported ALP × 1.10 (i.e., 10% upward adjustment if TPO adjusts ALP)
Standard Deduction from Salary income = Lower of Rs. 50,000 or actual salary received
Net Tax Liability = Tax on Total Income + Surcharge + Health & Education Cess (4%) − Relief under Section 89/90/91 − Tax Credit u/s 90A/91 − TDS/TCS/Tax on Estimate → Tax Payable/Demand
Surcharge on Individual/HUF: 10% on income > Rs. 10 lakh but ≤ Rs. 50 lakh; 15% on income > Rs. 50 lakh but ≤ Rs. 1 crore; 25% on income > Rs. 1 crore but ≤ Rs. 2 crore; 37% on income > Rs. 2 crore (plus applicable cess)
Rebate u/s 87A = Lower of Rs. 12,500 or tax payable; applicable when total income does not exceed Rs. 5 lakh

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the limit under Section 80C (Rs. 1,50,000) with the additional Section 80CCD(1B) deduction (Rs. 50,000 for NPS) — these are separate and can be claimed cumulatively.
  • Taking ITC on blocked credits under Section 17(5) — especially on food/beverages, club memberships, and construction of immovable property (except plant and machinery).
  • Forgetting that gifts from relatives are fully exempt while gifts from non-relatives exceeding Rs. 50,000 in aggregate become taxable.
  • Not distinguishing between composite supply (principal supply attracts) and mixed supply (highest rate attracts) — applying the wrong principle leads to incorrect GST liability.
  • Claiming ITC on goods/services used for making exempt supplies — ITC attributable to exempt supplies must be reversed.
  • Treating wealth-tax as currently applicable — it was abolished from AY 2016-17; do not compute wealth-tax for current assessment years.
  • Applying the old gift-tax Act instead of Section 56(2)(x) of the Income-tax Act for taxation of gifts.
  • Using wrong holding period for determining short-term vs long-term capital gains — equity/shares require 12 months; immovable property requires 24 months; under pre-FY 2017-18 rules it was 36 months.
  • Forgetting the 10% surcharge bracket for individuals at income between Rs. 10 lakh and Rs. 50 lakh — this is often overlooked in tax computation.
  • Not considering that residential status is determined annually — the same person can be resident in one year and non-resident in another.
  • Failing to apply the lower of actual rent or 15% of total income when claiming deduction under Section 80GG for house rent paid.
  • Not reversing the proportionate ITC when capital goods are used partly for taxable supplies and partly for exempt supplies — Section 17(2) and Section 17(3) require pro-rata reversal.
