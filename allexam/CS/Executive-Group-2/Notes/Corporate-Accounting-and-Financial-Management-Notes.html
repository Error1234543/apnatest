1. Introduction to Corporate Accounting and Accounting Standards/Ind AS Framework

Corporate Accounting deals with the preparation of financial statements of companies under the Companies Act, 2013 and relevant Accounting Standards (AS) or Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS). It goes beyond basic bookkeeping to address complex transactions such as issue of shares and debentures, redemption of capital, branch accounting, joint ventures, inter-se transfers, and consolidated financials.

The regulatory framework governing corporate accounting in India includes:
- Companies Act, 2013: Section 128 requires every company to maintain proper books of account; Sections 129–133 deal with financial statements, Board's report, and accounting standards.
- Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Master Circulars on accounting: applicable for NBFCs and banking companies.
- Accounting Standards (AS) issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI): currently applicable to many entities not yet mandatorily under Ind AS.
- Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS): notified under Rule 3 of Companies (Indian Accounting Standards) Rules, 2015, mandatorily applicable to listed companies and certain large unlisted companies as per phased criteria.

Key differences between AS and Ind AS:
- AS are principle-based but historically less rigorous on fair value and disclosure; Ind AS are converged with IFRS, require fair value measurement for more instruments, have explicit derecognition criteria, and demand significantly more footnote disclosures.
- Ind AS introduce concepts like impairment testing (Ind AS 36), revenue recognition from contracts with customers (Ind AS 115), lease accounting with on-balance-sheet recognition (Ind AS 116), and financial instrument classification based on business model and contractual cash flow characteristics (Ind AS 109).

For CS Executive purposes, students must know which standards are most frequently tested: AS 2 (Inventories), AS 3 (Cash Flow Statements), AS 4 (Contingencies and Events After Reporting Date), AS 7 (Construction Contracts), AS 9 (Revenue Recognition), AS 10 (Property, Plant and Equipment), AS 11 (Foreign Exchange Transactions), AS 13 (Accounting for Investments), AS 15 (Employee Benefits), AS 17 (Segment Reporting), AS 18 (Related Party Disclosures), AS 19 (Leases), AS 20 (Goodwill and Derivatives), AS 21 (Consolidated Financial Statements), AS 22 (Taxation), AS 23 (Investments in Associates), AS 24 (Discontinued Operations), AS 26 (Intangible Assets), AS 28 (Impairment), AS 29 (Provisions), Ind AS 1 (Presentation), Ind AS 7 (Cash Flows), Ind AS 10 (Events After Reporting Period), Ind AS 18/IFRS 15 (Revenue), Ind AS 116 (Leases), Ind AS 109 (Financial Instruments), Ind AS 110 (Consolidated Financial Statements).

Students should be comfortable reading and preparing financial statements in compliance with Schedule III of the Companies Act, 2013, which prescribes the format of balance sheet and statement of profit and loss for different classes of companies.

2. Issue of Shares — Methods, Forfeiture, Reissue and Renunciation

Shares may be issued at par, premium, or discount. The Companies Act, 2013 prohibits issuing shares at a discount except in limited circumstances permitted by law (e.g., re-issue of forfeited shares). Issue at premium requires a securities premium account (Section 52); the premium can be used to write off preliminary expenses, share issue expenses, or the premium on debentures, or for issuing fully paid bonus shares.

Methods of issue:
- By prospectus: subscription list is invited from the public; application money is received in instalments (application, allotment, call). If an applicant fails to pay subsequent calls, shares may be forfeited.
- By private placement (Section 42): offer to select persons not exceeding 200 (excluding employees and QIBs), via letter of offer, within 30 days of board approval.
- Rights issue (Section 81): existing shareholders have pre-emptive rights to subscribe in proportion to their holding.
- Bonus issue (Section 63): capitalisation of reserves (general reserve, securities premium, capital redemption reserve) into share capital. Certain reserves like revaluation reserve generally cannot be capitalised if it results in an increase in paid-up capital exceeding the book value of assets.
- Buyback (Section 68): companies can buy back their own shares out of free reserves, securities premium, or proceeds of shares; maximum buyback cannot exceed 25% of total paid-up equity capital in a year; requires special resolution or board resolution depending on conditions.

Forfeiture and reissue: when a shareholder defaults in payment of calls, the company may forfeit the shares after following due process. On forfeiture, the forfeited share capital account is credited with the amount called up but unpaid, and the shares become property of the company. On reissue at a discount, the loss on reissue is debited to forfeited shares account to the extent available, otherwise to capital reserve.

Renunciation of shares: a person who subscribes to shares may renounce in favour of another before allotment (renunciation). After allotment, transfer of shares is governed by the articles and Companies Act provisions on transmission and transfer.

Important journal entries:
- Forfeiture: Dr Share Allotment/Call A/c, Cr Forfeited Shares A/c
- Reissue at premium: Dr Bank, Dr Forfeited Shares (if any loss), Cr Share Capital
- Reissue at discount: Dr Bank, Dr Loss on Reissue (to extent not covered by forfeited amount), Cr Share Capital

3. Redeemable Preference Shares and Debentures

As per Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013, preference shares can be redeemed only out of:
- Fresh issue of shares made for the purpose of redemption (proceeds thereof)
- Free reserves of the company (profits available for distribution)
- A combination of the above

The Company cannot redeem redeemable preference shares out of capital, except in the case of unlisted companies where certain conditions apply. A Company Redeemable Preference Shares Account (CRPSA) must be created before redemption to the extent equal to the amount of redemption out of free reserves; this account is funded out of profits available for dividend.

Accounting for redemption:
- On issue of RPS: Dr Cash/Bank, Cr Redeemable Preference Share Capital; simultaneously, if redemption is to be funded from profits, create CRPSA by transferring from general reserve/profits: Dr Profit & Loss Appropriation A/c, Cr CRPSA.
- On redemption: Dr Redeemable Preference Share Capital, Cr Cash/Bank.
- Any premium on redemption is debited to Securities Premium Account or Profit & Loss Account.

Debentures may be issued at par, premium, or discount. Discount on issue of debentures is treated as a deferred expense and written off over the tenure of the debentures. If debentures are secured by a charge on assets, the charge must be registered with the Registrar of Companies.

Types of debentures:
- Secured vs. Unsecured
- Redeemable vs. Irredeemable (irredeemable debentures are virtually prohibited under modern company law)
- Convertible vs. Non-convertible vs. Partly convertible
- Registered vs. Bearer

Accounting for convertible debentures: on conversion, the debenture liability is extinguished and equity is issued. Under Ind AS, such instruments may need to be split into liability and equity components at inception (Ind AS 32). The conversion entry is typically: Dr Debentures A/c, Dr Securities Premium (if any), Cr Share Capital, Cr Securities Premium (on conversion premium).

Sinking fund method: where debentures are redeemed by setting aside equal annual sums into a separate fund invested in specified securities. At maturity, the investments plus the accumulated fund are utilised for redemption. Annual investment entry: Dr Sinking Fund A/c, Cr Bank. Income from investment: Dr Sinking Fund Investment A/c (or Bank), Cr Sinking Fund Income A/c. At maturity: Dr Sinking Fund Investment A/c, Cr Sinking Fund A/c, and then Dr Debentures A/c, Cr Bank.

4. Amalgamation, Acquisition and Business Combination

Amalgamation involves the fusion of two or more companies into one. Under AS 14 (and Ind AS 3.1 / Ind AS 103 forInd AS entities), there are two methods:

Purchase Consideration (PC): the total amount payable by the purchasing company to the shareholders of the vendor company, as agreed upon. PC can be determined on a net asset basis or net income basis.
- Net Asset Method: PC = Fair value of identifiable net assets of vendor company (assets at fair value minus liabilities assumed). Goodwill arises if PC > net assets; bargain purchase if PC < net assets.
- Net Income Method: PC is based on the earning capacity of the vendor company, often using a capitalisation of average profits approach.

Methods of accounting for amalgamation:
- Purchase Method: assets and liabilities of the vendor company are taken over at their fair values; goodwill or capital reserve is recognised. The reserves of the vendor company are not carried forward.
- Pooling of Interests (Pooling) Method: used only when the amalgamation is in the nature of a true merger (all conditions of AS 14 are met). Assets, liabilities, and reserves of both companies are carried forward at their existing book values; no goodwill arises.

Under Ind AS 103 (Business Combinations), the acquisition method is mandatory for all business combinations. Key steps:
1. Identify the acquirer.
2. Determine the acquisition date.
3. Recognise and measure the identifiable assets acquired, liabilities assumed, and any non-controlling interest at fair value.
4. Recognise and measure goodwill or bargain purchase gain.

Goodwill = Consideration transferred + NCI + Fair value of any previous equity interest – Fair value of net identifiable assets.
Bargain purchase gain is recognised in profit or loss.

Intra-group transactions and inter-company balances must be eliminated in consolidation. Minority (non-controlling) interest is shown separately in the consolidated balance sheet under equity.

5. Branch Accounts — Head Office Adjustments and Departmental Accounts

Branch accounts arise when a company operates through multiple locations. The nature of branch accounting depends on the degree of autonomy:

- Dependent Branch: stock and invoices are sent from head office; the branch acts as an agent. Stock on approval or goods on consignment basis may be maintained.
- Independent Branch: maintains its own full set of books and sends periodic accounts to head office.

Key inter-company accounts:
- Branch Stock A/c: records goods sent to branch. Debit: goods sent; Credit: goods returned, adjustments, closing stock.
- Branch Account: records transactions with the branch — debit: goods sent, cash received, receivables; credit: goods returned, cash remitted, payables.
- Goods Sent on Approval A/c: goods sent on approval are not sales until approved; return of rejected goods is credited to this account.
- Provision for unrealised profit: when goods are transferred from head office to branch at cost plus profit (transfer price), a provision for unrealised profit must be made on closing stock at branch to avoid overstating profit.

Formula for provision for unrealised profit:
- If marked up on cost: PUP = Closing stock × (Markup percentage / (100 + markup %))
- If marked up on selling price: PUP = Closing stock × (Markup percentage)

Departmental Accounts: when a company has multiple departments operating under one entity, departmental trading and profit and loss account is prepared. Common expenses (rent, salaries, advertising) are apportioned on a reasonable basis (sales value, floor space, number of employees, etc.). Direct expenses are charged entirely to the respective department. Inter-departmental transfers of goods must include unrealised profit on closing stock, similar to branch adjustments.

6. Joint Venture, Consignment and Hire Purchase Accounts

Joint Venture (JV): a contractual business arrangement where two or more parties undertake a single economic activity. Accounting may be conducted through a separate set of books (co-venturers maintain individual accounts) or through a Joint Venture Account.

Joint Venture Account method: all co-venturers contribute to a common account. Each venturer records their share of contributions and receipts. The joint venture account is analogous to a nominal account — debits include purchases, expenses, contributions; credits include sales, contributions by others. Balance represents profit or loss to be shared per agreement.

Accounting entries:
- Dr Joint Venture A/c, Cr Bank/Stock (when contributing assets/cash)
- Dr Bank, Cr Joint Venture A/c (when receiving proceeds)
- On closure: balance transferred to Profit & Loss A/c and shared between venturers.

Consignment Account: goods are sent by the principal (consignor) to an agent (consignee) for sale. The consignee is entitled to a commission (usually percentage of sales). The consignor retains ownership until sale. Key features:
- Abnormal loss (e.g., fire) is valued at cost plus proportionate direct expenses, unless insured.
- Normal selling expenses are borne by consignee and deducted from sale proceeds.
- Commission may be ordinary (on sales) or overlapping (on surplus sales above a target).
- Unsold stock is valued at cost plus proportionate non-recurring expenses (freight, insurance, loading).

Hire Purchase Account: goods are sold on hire-purchase terms where possession is delivered but ownership transfers only on payment of all instalments. If the buyer defaults, the seller can repossess the goods. Valuation of repossessed goods:
- Full value method: repossessed stock is valued at contract price less instalments received plus proportionate part of unpaid instalments.
- Proportionate gross profit method: GP ratio is applied to the contract price to estimate profit, then revaluation is done accordingly.
- When partial repossession occurs, unsold stock is valued at cost plus a proportionate share of unrealised profit.

7. Amortisation of Preliminary Expenses and Deferred Revenue Expenditure

Preliminary expenses are costs incurred before the company commences business or raises capital. These include prospectus expenses, incorporation fees, legal fees, underwriting commissions, and preliminary issue expenses. Under the Companies Act, 2013, preliminary expenses cannot be written off against profits unless they meet specific conditions and disclosure requirements.

AS 26 (Intangible Assets) and relevant ICSI guidance prescribe that preliminary expenses should be written off over a period not exceeding five years from the date of commencement of business, unless a longer period is justified. In practice, a standard write-off period of 5 years is commonly used.

Journal entry for writing off preliminary expenses:
Dr Profit & Loss A/c, Cr Preliminary Expenses A/c

Deferred revenue expenditure refers to expenses of a revenue nature whose benefits extend over more than one accounting period. Examples include large advertising campaigns, heavy brokerage on issue of shares/debentures, and preliminary expenses of long-term projects. These are gradually written off over the period of benefit.

Under AS 26 and Ind AS 38, development expenditure may be capitalised as an intangible asset only if strict criteria are met (technical feasibility, intention to complete, ability to use/sell, probable future economic benefits, reliable measurement of expenditure). Research expenditure is always expensed.

For disclosure purposes, the unamortised balance of preliminary expenses and deferred revenue expenditure is shown under Non-Current Assets — Other Non-Current Assets in the balance sheet, with the amount and period of amortisation disclosed in the notes.

8. Cash Flow Statement (AS 3 / Ind AS 7)

AS 3 (Revised) / Ind AS 7 requires every entity to prepare a Cash Flow Statement for the period, showing inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents, classified into operating, investing, and financing activities. Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible to known amounts of cash and subject to insignificant risk of changes in value (e.g., treasury bills, commercial paper).

Operating Activities: principal revenue-producing activities and other activities that are not investing or financing. Includes cash received from customers, payments to suppliers and employees, interest paid/received, dividends received, taxes paid.

Investing Activities: acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents. Includes purchase/sale of PPE, investments in subsidiaries/associates/joint ventures, loans advanced and repaid.

Financing Activities: activities that result in changes in the size and composition of contributed equity and borrowings. Includes issue/buyback of shares, issue/redemption of debentures and bonds, repayment of loans, dividends paid.

Methods of preparation:
- Direct Method: shows major classes of gross cash receipts and payments. Rarely used in practice.
- Indirect Method (more common): starts with net profit before tax and adjusts for non-cash items (depreciation, amortisation, gains/losses on sale), changes in working capital, and other non-operating items to arrive at operating cash flow.

Key adjustments in indirect method:
- Add back depreciation and amortisation
- Add losses / subtract gains on sale of assets
- Adjust for gains/losses on foreign exchange
- Decrease in current assets = source; Increase = use
- Increase in current liabilities = source; Decrease = use
- Dividends paid are financing activities; dividends received are operating (AS 3) or investing (Ind AS 7)
- Interest paid is operating (AS 3) or financing (Ind AS 7)

Format as per Schedule III: Operating Activities → Investing Activities → Financing Activities → Net increase/decrease in cash and cash equivalents → Opening balance → Closing balance.

9. Time Value of Money and Concept of Compound Interest

Time Value of Money (TVM) is the foundational concept of financial management: a rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow because of its potential earning capacity. This principle underlies all capital budgeting, valuation, and investment decisions.

Key concepts:
- Present Value (PV): the current worth of a future sum of money discounted at a given rate.
- Future Value (FV): the value of a present amount at a future date compounded at a given rate.
- Discount Rate / Cost of Capital (r): the rate used to bring future cash flows to present value.
- Compounding Frequency: interest may be compounded annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly, or continuously.

Formulas:
- FV of a single sum: FV = PV × (1 + r)^n
- PV of a single sum: PV = FV / (1 + r)^n = FV × (1 + r)^(-n)
- FV of an annuity (ordinary): FV_annuity = P × [(1 + r)^n − 1] / r, where P = periodic payment
- PV of an annuity (ordinary): PV_annuity = P × [1 − (1 + r)^(-n)] / r
- Annuity due (payments at beginning): multiply ordinary annuity values by (1 + r)
- Perpetuity: PV = P / r
- Growing perpetuity: PV = P / (r − g), where g = growth rate
- Effective Annual Rate (EAR): EAR = (1 + r/m)^m − 1, where m = compounding periods per year
- Continuous compounding: FV = PV × e^(rt)

Annuity tables (PVIFA, FVIFA) provide pre-calculated factors. Students must be熟练 at using these tables in exams. Perpetuities are relevant in valuing preference shares (PV = Dividend / r) and perpetual bonds.

Uneven cash flows: each cash flow is discounted individually and summed. PV = Σ CF_t / (1 + r)^t.

The concept is critical for NPV calculation, bond valuation, share valuation, lease vs. buy decisions, and pension fund calculations.

10. Cost of Capital — Individual, Weighted and Marginal

Cost of capital is the minimum rate of return a company must earn on its investments to maintain its market value and attract funds. It serves as the discount rate in capital budgeting.

Individual Costs:
- Cost of Equity (Ke): the return required by equity shareholders. Calculated using:
* Dividend Valuation Model: Ke = D1 / P0 + g (where D1 = expected dividend, P0 = current market price, g = growth rate)
* CAPM: Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf), where Rf = risk-free rate, β = beta, Rm = market return
* Dividend Yield + Growth: Ke = (D1/P0) × 100 + g%
* Earnings Model: Ke = EPS / Market Price × 100 (if no growth)
- Cost of Debt (Kd): Kd = I × (1 − t) / NP, where I = annual interest, t = tax rate, NP = net proceeds. For redeemable debt: approximate YTM method or IRR method.
- Cost of Redeemable Debt (Kd): Solve for r in: NP = Σ I(1-t)/(1+r)^t + MV/(1+r)^n. Approximate formula: Kd = [I(1-t) + (RV-NP)/n] / [(RV+NP)/2]
- Cost of Pref erence Share Capital (Kp): Kp = Dp / NP, where Dp = preference dividend, NP = net proceeds. For redeemable P shares: similar to debt YTM approach.
- Cost of Retained Earnings (Kr): Kr = Ke × (1 - t) adjusted for any taxes on retained earnings. Often approximated as equal to Ke.
- Cost of New Equity (Ke_new): Ke_new = D1 / (P0 - F) + g, where F = flotation cost.

Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC):
WACC = Σ (wi × ki), where wi = weight of source i, ki = cost of source i. Weights can be book value or market value; market value weights are preferred for decision-making.

Marginal Cost of Capital (MCC): the cost of raising one additional unit of capital. MCC schedules show how WACC increases as more capital is raised, due to changing costs or weights. The point where MCC exceeds the return on investment signals the optimal capital structure.

11. Capital Structure Theories and Leverage

Capital Structure refers to the mix of debt and equity used to finance a company's operations and growth. Key theories:

Net Income Approach (NI): proposes that increasing debt (cheaper than equity) lowers WACC and increases firm value. WACC decreases continuously with higher debt. Assumes constant cost of debt and equity regardless of leverage. Criticised for ignoring increased financial risk.

Net Operating Income Approach (NOI): proposes that WACC and firm value are independent of capital structure. Cost of equity rises linearly with leverage, exactly offsetting the benefit of cheaper debt. Implies perfect capital markets.

Traditional Approach: a middle ground — WACC initially falls with debt (due to cheaper debt), reaches a minimum at an optimal point, then rises as financial risk escalates. There exists an optimal capital structure.

Modigliani-Miller (MM) Hypothesis:
- Without taxes: capital structure is irrelevant; firm value depends only on operating income and risk. WACC is constant.
- With taxes: debt is advantageous because interest is tax-deductible. Firm value = VU + T × D, where T = tax rate, D = debt. Optimal structure is 100% debt.
- MM with taxes but personal taxes: the advantage of corporate tax is partially offset by higher personal taxes on interest income vs. capital gains.

Leverage Analysis:
- Operating Leverage (OL): measures sensitivity of EBIT to changes in sales. OL = Contribution / EBIT = ΔEBIT/EBIT ÷ ΔSales/Sales. High fixed costs → high OL → high business risk.
- Financial Leverage (FL): measures sensitivity of EPS to changes in EBIT. FL = EBIT / (EBIT − Interest). High debt → high FL → high financial risk.
- Combined Leverage (CL): CL = OL × FL = Contribution / (EBIT − Interest). Measures total risk — sensitivity of EPS to sales changes.
- Degree of Leverage formulas: DOL = Q(P-VC) / [Q(P-VC) - FC], DFL = EBIT / (EBIT - I), DCL = DOL × DFL.

Optimal capital structure maximises EPS and minimises WACC while keeping financial risk acceptable.

12. Capital Budgeting Decision Techniques

Capital Budgeting is the process of evaluating and selecting long-term investments that yield returns over multiple years. Key techniques:

Non-Discounted Methods:
- Payback Period (PBP): time required to recover the initial investment. PBP = Initial Investment / Annual Cash Inflow (for even cash flows). For uneven cash flows, cumulative cash flows are computed. Shorter PBP is preferred. Limitations: ignores time value of money, ignores cash flows beyond PBP.
- Accounting Rate of Return (ARR): ARR = Average Post-Tax Profit / Average Investment × 100. Average Investment = (Initial Investment + Salvage Value) / 2. Limitations: uses accounting profit instead of cash flow, ignores TVM.

Discounted Methods:
- Net Present Value (NPV): NPV = PV of cash inflows − Initial investment. Accept if NPV > 0. NPV = Σ CFt / (1+k)^t − C0, where k = cost of capital. Most theoretically sound method.
- Internal Rate of Return (IRR): the discount rate at which NPV = 0. Found by trial and error or interpolation. Accept if IRR > cost of capital. Limitation: multiple IRRs possible with non-conventional cash flows; reinvestment assumption at IRR is unrealistic.
- Profitability Index (PI): PI = PV of inflows / Initial outlay. Accept if PI > 1. Useful for capital rationing.
- Discounted Payback Period: similar to PBP but uses discounted cash flows. Still ignores post-payback flows.

Mutually Exclusive Projects: when projects compete, NPV method is preferred over IRR because IRR may give conflicting rankings due to scale differences or timing of cash flows.

Capital Rationing: when funds are limited, projects are ranked by PI and selected to maximise total NPV within budget.
- Modified IRR (MIRR): assumes reinvestment at cost of capital, solving the multiple IRR problem.
- Equivalent Annual Annuity (EAA): converts NPV into equivalent annual cash flow for comparing projects of different lives.

13. Working Capital Management

Working Capital Management concerns the management of current assets and current liabilities to ensure liquidity and operational efficiency. Two approaches:
- Gross Working Capital: total current assets.
- Net Working Capital: Current Assets − Current Liabilities. Positive NWC indicates adequate short-term liquidity.

Components of Working Capital:
- Inventory Management: raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods. Models include EOQ (Economic Order Quantity): EOQ = √(2 × D × O / C), where D = annual demand, O = ordering cost per order, C = carrying cost per unit per year. Safety stock buffers against uncertainty. Just-In-Time (JIT) aims to minimise inventory holdings.
- Receivables Management: credit policy involves credit period, credit standards, and collection policy. Factors affecting receivables: ageing schedule, bad debt percentage, collection period (Average Collection Period = 365 / Receivables Turnover, where Turnover = Credit Sales / Average Receivables). Discounting and factoring are alternative financing methods.
- Payables Management: trade credit is a natural source of financing. Cost of missing cash discounts: Cost = Discount % / (100 − Discount %) × 365 / (Full credit period − Discount period). Companies should take discounts if cost of forgoing > cost of alternative borrowing.
- Cash Management: cash budget forecasts cash inflows and outflows. Tools include cash column in budget, cash flow statement. Models for optimum cash balance: Baumol Model (similar to EOQ): Optimum Cash = √(2 × T × F / C), where T = total cash needed, F = fixed cost per transaction, C = opportunity cost of holding cash. Miller-Orr Model sets upper and lower control limits for cash balance with a return point.
- Determinants of Working Capital Requirement: nature of business, length of production cycle, seasonality, credit policy, growth prospects, operating efficiency, inflation.
- Financing of Working Capital: short-term sources include trade credit, bank overdraft, short-term loans, commercial paper, factoring; medium/long-term sources include term loans, debentures, equity for permanent working capital.

14. Dividend Policy and Share Repurchase

Dividend Policy determines how much of earnings are distributed to shareholders versus retained for reinvestment. Key concepts:

Dividend Types:
- Interim Dividend: declared and paid by the Board between two AGMs (requires board resolution, not shareholder approval).
- Final Dividend: recommended by the Board and approved by shareholders at the AGM.
- Stock Dividend (Bonus Shares): distribution of additional shares to existing shareholders, capitalising reserves.
- Scrip Dividend: allowing shareholders to choose between cash or additional shares.

Dividend Policies:
- Stable Dividend Policy: paying a consistent dividend per share regardless of earnings fluctuation, building investor confidence.
- Constant Payout Ratio: paying a fixed percentage of earnings as dividend.
- Residual Dividend Policy: dividends are paid only from residual earnings after funding all positive NPV projects.
- Moderate Dividend Policy: a hybrid combining elements of stability and flexibility.

Factors influencing dividend policy:
- Legal constraints: Companies Act prohibits dividend payment out of capital; dividends only from distributable profits; requirement to transfer to reserves if specified conditions apply.
- Profitability and cash position: sufficient distributable profits and adequate cash flows.
- Growth opportunities: high-growth firms tend to retain more earnings.
- Investor preferences: some prefer current income (dividends), others prefer capital gains.
- Tax considerations: dividend taxation affects investor net returns.
- Signalling effect: dividend changes signal management's outlook.
- Liquidity position: dividends are paid in cash; illiquid profitable firms may not pay dividends.

Gordon's Model: P0 = D1 / (Ke − g), where P0 = market price per share, D1 = next dividend, Ke = cost of equity, g = growth rate. Implies that dividend policy is relevant — higher payout increases share price when Ke > g.

Walraven's Model: incorporates taxation; asserts that dividend policy matters because of differential tax treatment of dividends and capital gains.

Buyback of Securities (Section 68): a company may buy back its own shares from shareholders. Buyback is an alternative to dividends. Benefits include returning surplus cash, improving EPS, preventing hostile takeovers, and optimising capital structure. Restrictions: buyback cannot exceed 25% of paid-up capital in a year;不得超过 10 times the average free cash flows of the preceding three years; requires special resolution (or board resolution if conditions met); locked-in period of 2 years for promoters/directors participating in buyback; disclosure requirements; buyback completed within 1 year.

15. Consolidated Financial Statements and Related Party Disclosures

Consolidated Financial Statements present the financial position and performance of a group (parent and its subsidiaries) as a single economic entity. AS 21 (Consolidated Financial Statements) and Ind AS 110 (Consolidated Financial Statements) govern this area.

Key principles:
- Control is the basis for consolidation. A parent controls an subsidiary when it has power over the investee, exposure to variable returns, and the ability to affect those returns.
- All subsidiaries must be consolidated, regardless of the parent's ownership percentage, if control exists.
- Investment in subsidiary is eliminated against the subsidiary's equity.
- Inter-company transactions, inter-company profits, and balances are eliminated in full.
- Non-controlling interest (NCI) represents the portion of subsidiary's equity and profit not owned by the parent. Under AS 21, NCI can be measured at proportionate share of net assets or at fair value. Under Ind AS 110, NCI is measured at fair value or proportionate share — the choice is made on an acquisition-by-acquisition basis.
- Goodwill on consolidation = Consideration transferred + NCI + FV of any previous equity interest − FV of net identifiable assets of subsidiary.
- Capital reserve (bargain purchase) arises when the FV of net assets acquired exceeds the consideration + NCI.

Steps in preparing consolidated FS:
1. Combine line-by-line: add assets, liabilities, revenues, expenses of parent and subsidiary.
2. Eliminate inter-company transactions: sales/purchases, receivables/payables, interest, dividends.
3. Eliminate investment in subsidiary against subsidiary's equity.
4. Adjust for unrealised profit in inventory (URP) — eliminate URP on intra-group sales and reduce inventory value.
5. Calculate and recognise goodwill or bargain purchase.
6. Allocate profit and equity between controlling and non-controlling interests.

Related Party Disclosures (AS 18 / Ind AS 24):
- A related party is a person or entity that is related to the reporting entity. Related parties include:
* Parents, subsidiaries, and fellow subsidiaries
* Associates and joint ventures
* Key management personnel (directors, managers) and their close family members
* Entities controlled, jointly controlled, or significantly influenced by related parties
- Disclosure requirements: nature of related party relationship, description of transactions, amounts involved, outstanding balances, terms and conditions, provisions for doubtful debts relating to outstanding balances, bad debt written off during the period.
- Mere occurrence of transactions in the ordinary course of business between related parties does not, by itself, constitute a conflict of interest.

Important facts

  • Section 55 of Companies Act, 2013: Redeemable preference shares can ONLY be redeemed out of fresh issue proceeds and/or free reserves — never out of share capital.
  • Securities Premium Account (Section 52) can be used to write off preliminary expenses, share issue expenses, premium on debentures, and for issuing fully paid bonus shares — but NOT for losses.
  • Company Redeemable Preference Shares Account (CRPSA) must be created before redemption to the extent equal to the amount of redemption out of free reserves; this protects creditors.
  • AS 14 provides two methods for amalgamation: Purchase Method and Pooling of Interests Method; Ind AS 103 mandates only the Acquisition Method for all business combinations.
  • Goodwill on amalgamation under Purchase Method = Purchase Consideration − Net Assets (at fair value); under Pooling Method, no goodwill arises as assets/liabilities are carried at book values.
  • Provision for Unrealised Profit (PUP) must be made on closing stock when goods are transferred at transfer price including profit; PUP on intra-group sales must be eliminated in consolidation.
  • AS 3 classifies cash flows into Operating, Investing, and Financing activities; Ind AS 7 has similar classification but treats interest paid as financing and dividends received as investing.
  • TVM: PV of annuity formula PV = P × [1 − (1+r)^(-n)] / r is fundamental for bond pricing, lease analysis, and loan amortisation calculations.
  • WACC formula: WACC = Σ(wi × ki); marginal cost of capital increases as more capital is raised due to changing costs/weights.
  • NPV method is the most reliable capital budgeting technique and should be used when ranking mutually exclusive projects, despite IRR being more intuitive.
  • Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL) = Contribution / EBIT; Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL) = EBIT / (EBIT − Interest); Combined Leverage = DOL × DFL.
  • EOQ = √(2DO/C) where D = annual demand, O = ordering cost per order, C = carrying cost per unit per year.
  • Baumol Model for optimum cash balance: √(2TF/C) where T = total cash needed, F = fixed cost per transaction, C = opportunity cost of holding cash.
  • Miller-Orr Model sets three control levels: upper limit, lower limit, and return point (Z) for cash balance management.
  • Section 68 buyback restriction: maximum 25% of paid-up equity capital in a financial year; buyback cannot exceed 10× average free cash flows of preceding three years.
  • Control, not ownership percentage, is the criterion for consolidation under Ind AS 110; even <50% ownership may require consolidation if control exists.
  • Non-controlling interest (NCI) under Ind AS 110: measured at either fair value or proportionate share of net assets — choice is acquisition-specific.
  • AS 18 related party definition includes parents, subsidiaries, associates, JVs, KMP and their close family members, and entities they control/influence.
  • Dividend can only be paid from 'distributable profits' — i.e., accumulated realised profits not exceeded by accumulated unrealised losses (Section 123, Companies Act, 2013).

Common mistakes

  • Confusing Payment of Dividend with Payment of Interest: Dividends are paid out of profits and are not deductible for tax; interest on debt is tax-deductible, which is why cost of debt is computed after-tax as Kd × (1-t).
  • Using Book Values instead of Market Values for WACC weights: Book value weights reflect historical costs and may misrepresent the true cost of capital; exam questions typically require market value weights unless specifically stated otherwise.
  • Forgetting to adjust depreciation in Cash Flow Statement: Depreciation is a non-cash expense and must be added back to net profit in the operating activities section under the indirect method.
  • Not creating CRPSA before redeeming preference shares: Section 55 requires creation of CRPSA equal to the redemption amount out of free reserves BEFORE redemption; forgetting this step violates the statute and attracts penalties.
  • Confusing Purchase Consideration with Net Assets: PC is what the purchasing company AGREES to pay; net assets are what the vendor company OWNS. They are usually different, and the difference is goodwill or capital reserve.
  • Eliminating only the parent's share of unrealised profit in consolidation: ALL unrealised profit on intra-group transactions must be eliminated in full, not just the parent's proportionate share.
  • Treating IRR as superior to NPV for mutually exclusive projects: IRR can give wrong rankings when projects differ in size or timing of cash flows; NPV should always be the deciding criterion in such cases.
  • Applying simple interest instead of compound interest in TVM calculations: All financial management formulas assume compound interest unless explicitly stated otherwise.
  • Valuing repossessed goods at cost alone: When goods are repossessed under hire purchase, they must be valued at contract price less instalments received plus a proportionate share of unpaid instalments' gross profit.
  • Ignoring tax shield in cost of debt calculation: The after-tax cost of debt is always lower than the pre-tax cost because interest is tax-deductible; using pre-tax cost of debt in WACC overstates the true cost.
  • Forgetting to eliminate inter-company dividends in consolidation: Dividends paid by subsidiary to parent are intra-group transactions and must be eliminated against the parent's investment account.
  • Not distinguishing between operating and financing cash flows: Interest paid is classified differently under AS 3 (operating) and Ind AS 7 (financing); this distinction is frequently tested.
  • Confusing Net Income Approach with Net Operating Income Approach: NI approach says WACC decreases with debt (optimal = 100% debt); NOI approach says WACC is constant (structure irrelevant). They are opposite conclusions.
  • Using gross profit ratio instead of overall profit ratio for URP on closing stock: Unrealised profit should be calculated using the overall profit margin (profit/sales), not gross profit margin, unless the question specifically asks for gross profit method.
  • Forfeited Shares Account balance goes to Capital Reserve on reissue: The balance in Forfeited Shares Account, after adjusting for loss on reissue, is transferred to Capital Reserve (not General Reserve) — this is a standard examination point.
