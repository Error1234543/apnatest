Section 1: Conceptual Foundation of Corporate Governance

Corporate governance refers to the system of rules, practices, processes, and relationships by which a company is directed and controlled. It fundamentally addresses the question: who controls whom? At its core, it is about aligning the interests of various stakeholders — shareholders, management, board of directors, employees, creditors, regulators, and the community — through accountability, fairness, transparency, and responsibility.

The Cadbury Committee (UK, 1992), following the collapse of several major UK companies, provided the most cited definition: "the system by which companies are directed and controlled." Bodies within a company are charged with the duties and responsibilities described in that system. The key features include separation of ownership and control, Board accountability, transparency of information, and protection of stakeholder interests.

In India, corporate governance principles have evolved from the Company Law Committee report (1950), through the Kumarswami Sastri Committee (1978), Narayan Murthy Committee (1998), and eventually culminated in SEBI's Regulation 55 of SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015, as amended. The ICSI itself published the 'Guidelines for Corporate Governance' which serve as reference material for company secretaries.

Three pillars support the corporate governance framework: (a) the Board of Directors acting as the primary governance body, (b) internal systems including audit committees, risk management, and compliance functions, and (c) external mechanisms such as statutory auditors, SEBI oversight, institutional investors, and regulatory disclosure requirements. Good governance is not merely legal compliance but goes beyond — it encompasses ethical conduct, social responsibility, environmental stewardship, and long-term value creation.

Section 2: Key Principles of Corporate Governance and Board Responsibilities

The universally recognised principles of corporate governance rest on four foundational pillars:

1. Accountability: The Board is accountable to shareholders and stakeholders. Every director must discharge duties honestly and with due care and skill. Annual reports, financial disclosures, and board reports ensure this.

2. Fairness: Equal treatment of all shareholders including minority and foreign shareholders. Protection of shareholder rights, especially against oppression and mismanagement under Section 241–242 of the Companies Act, 2013. Related party transactions require approval to prevent favouritism.

3. Transparency: Full, accurate, and timely disclosure of material facts concerning the company — financial situation, performance, ownership, and governance. Not仅限于 annual reports; includes quarterly results, board evaluations, and ESG disclosures.

4. Independence: A strong, independent Board free from management or promoter influence. Independent directors provide objective judgment and protect minority interests.

Board composition and responsibilities under the Companies Act, 2013:
- Section 149 mandates a minimum number of directors, with at least one-third being independent directors for listed public companies.
- Independent directors under Section 149(6) must meet conditions in Rule 6 — maximum two terms of five years each, no material pecuniary relationship, and independence certification.
- The Board is responsible for strategic guidance, risk oversight, internal control systems, related party transaction oversight, and evaluating its own performance (Section 177 requires an Audit Committee, while evaluation of non-independent directors is done by the Board and independent directors among themselves, and of the Chairperson by the whole Board).
- Section 177 requires every listed company to establish an Audit Committee comprising only independent directors with specific expertise.
- Section 178 requires a Nomination and Remuneration Committee and a Stakeholders Relationship Committee.
- Section 177(4) requires the Audit Committee to investigate whenever there are doubts about fraud.
- Secretarial Audit under Section 204 and Rule 9 of Companies (Appointment and Remuneration of Managerial Personnel) Rules, 2014 is a critical compliance function.

Case law highlight: In Vijay Mallya v. Union of India, the Supreme Court emphasised that corporate governance requires honesty, integrity, and probity. In Satyam Scandal (2009), the entire board was found complicit due to lack of independent scrutiny, leading to subsequent tightening of governance norms.

Section 3: Role and Responsibilities of Independent Directors

Independent directors are a cornerstone of modern corporate governance. Their appointment ensures that board decisions are not dominated by promoters or management, providing an objective check on power.

Eligibility and tenure under Section 149 read with Section 164 and the Companies Act Rules:
- Must satisfy the independence criteria under Section 149(6): not a relative of promoters/directors, no pecuniary relationship exceeding 2% of turnover or 2% of gross total income, not a subsisting director on another body corporate where promoters hold 10% or more voting power, not a key managerial personnel or employee of the company or its subsidiaries.
- Maximum tenure: two terms of five years each. Cooling-off period of three years before re-appointment.
- Cannot hold more than one directorship on the audit committee of more than fifteen listed companies or such number as prescribed.
- Rotation is mandatory after tenures are exhausted; no reappointment is permissible immediately.

Key functions of independent directors:
- Protecting the interest of minority shareholders.
- Bringing objectivity to board decisions on related party transactions (Section 188 requires shareholder approval for material related party transactions; independent directors play a key role in the NRC).
- Participating in the evaluation of the performance of the board and its members.
- Serving as chairpersons of the Audit Committee and Nomination and Remuneration Committee.
- Investigating matters of fraud under Section 143(12) — the CA must report suspicions of fraud to the independent directors, who then determine the course of action.
- Attending all board meetings and general meetings; if unable to attend, they must provide reasons in writing.
- Signing the board report.
- Filing a declaration of independence annually under Form GST-1.

Consequences of non-compliance: A company failing to appoint the required number of independent directors is in contravention of Section 149. Penalties under Section 147 apply — fine on the company and every officer in default.

Recent developments: The ICSI and SEBI have strengthened the role by mandating e-voting for election of independent directors, requiring specific training programmes, and increasing the scope of their involvement in CSR projects, whistleblower complaints, and investigating fraud.

Section 4: Risk Management — Concepts and Frameworks

Risk management is the systematic process of identifying, assessing, and controlling threats to an organisation's capital and earnings. These threats arise from a wide variety of sources including financial uncertainty, legal liabilities, strategic management errors, accidents, and natural disasters.

Types of risk relevant to corporations:
- Strategic Risk: Arising from adverse business decisions, implementation failures, or failure to adapt to changes in the competitive environment.
- Operational Risk: Arising from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events — includes fraud, IT failures, supply chain disruption.
- Financial Risk: Market risk (interest rate, currency, commodity price movements), credit risk (default by counterparties), liquidity risk (inability to meet obligations).
- Compliance Risk: Exposure to legal or regulatory sanctions, financial loss, or reputational damage from failure to comply with laws, regulations, and codes of conduct.
- Reputational Risk: Damage to public standing arising from negative perception, social media amplification, or stakeholder distrust.
- Environmental/Social/Governance Risk: Climate change exposure, labour practice violations, data privacy breaches.
- Cyber Security Risk: Increasingly critical with digital transformation — data breaches, ransomware, insider threats.

COSO ERM Framework (Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission, updated 2017):
The updated COSO ERM integrates strategy and performance. Its five components are:
1. Governance and Culture — setting the tone at the top, defining operating structure, establishing desired culture of risk awareness.
2. Strategy and Objective Setting — risks must be considered when setting strategy and objectives.
3. Performance — identifying, assessing, and prioritising risks; implementing responses.
4. Review and Revision — monitoring ERM performance and making adjustments.
5. Information, Communication and Reporting — relevant information identified, captured, and communicated in a timeframe that allows the entity to carry out its responsibilities.

SEBI LODR and Risk Management:
Under Regulation 17(1)(b) of SEBI LODR Regulations, 2015, every listed entity must constitute a Risk Management Committee of its Board. The committee must review risk management plan periodically and recommend to the full Board. It should consist of at least three directors, with majority being independent directors, and the chairperson must be an independent director. The Risk Management Committee must meet at least once a year to review the company's risk management policies.

Companies Act, 2013 — Section 135 and CSR relates to longer-term societal risk. However, the general duty of directors under Section 166 includes exercising duties to promote the company's objects with reasonable care, skill, and diligence — implicitly including risk-aware decision-making. The National Companies Accounting and Auditing Standards (NIC/Ind AS) also mandate fair presentation and recognition of contingent liabilities and provisions related to risk exposures.

Key point for CS exams: Risk management is not about eliminating risk but about understanding, quantifying, and managing risk within the organisation's risk appetite. Effective risk management supports strategic decision-making, enhances operational efficiency, and protects shareholder value.

Section 5: Internal Control Systems and Internal Audit

Internal controls are procedures implemented by the board and management to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives in operational effectiveness, reliability of financial reporting, and compliance with applicable laws and regulations.

COSO Internal Control Framework comprises five interrelated components:
1. Control Environment — Sets the tone of an organisation, influencing the control consciousness of its people. Includes integrity, ethical values, management's philosophy, organisational structure, assignment of authority and responsibility, and human resource policies.
2. Risk Assessment — Identifying and analysing risks relevant to achieving objectives.
3. Control Activities — Policies and procedures that help ensure management directives are carried out — approvals, authorisations, verifications, reconciliations, segregation of duties, physical controls, IT general controls.
4. Information and Communication — Relevant information is identified, obtained, and communicated in a timeframe that enables people to carry out their responsibilities.
5. Monitoring Activities — Ongoing evaluations or separate assessments to ascertain whether each component is present and functioning.

Internal Audit under the Companies Act, 2013:
Section 138 mandates internal audit for:
- Listed companies and their subsidiaries
- Every public company with paid-up share capital of Rs. 50 crores or more, or turnover of Rs. 200 crores or more, or outstanding loans/borrowings from banks/financial institutions of Rs. 100 crores or more, or outstanding deposits of Rs. 25 crores or more
- Such classes of companies as may be prescribed

Rule 13 of Companies (Internal Audit) Rules, 2014 specifies who can be appointed as internal auditor — a chartered accountant, cost accountant, or such other professional as may be agreed upon, or any firm composed of such professionals.

Scope of internal audit per Rule 13:
- Verifying adherence to accounting policies
- Verifying internal control procedures
- Assessing adequacy of internal control systems
- Reviewing financial and management accounting records
- Monitoring adherence to budget
- Reviewing internal financial controls
- Reporting deviations

Internal Control vs Internal Audit: Internal controls are the systems and procedures; internal audit is the independent appraisal function that evaluates the design and effectiveness of those controls. Internal audit provides assurance to the Audit Committee.

The CA must conduct a financial audit and may also review internal financial controls if specifically requested or under Section 143(b). Internal financial controls over financial reporting must be evaluated by auditors under Section 143(3)(i).

Section 6: Compliance Management and Secretarial Audit

Compliance refers to the adherence of a company to applicable laws, regulations, standards, and ethical norms governing its operations. Non-compliance exposes the company to legal penalties, reputational damage, financial losses, and in extreme cases, dissolution.

Three lines of defence model:
- First line: Operational management — owns and manages risk directly
- Second line: Compliance and risk functions — oversee and challenge the first line
- Third line: Internal audit — provides independent assurance

Secretarial Audit under Section 204 and Rule 9 of Companies (Appointment and Remuneration) Rules, 2014:
- Mandatory for every listed company and such other class of companies as prescribed (currently, public companies with paid-up share capital of Rs. 50 crores or more, or turnover of Rs. 200 crores or more, or outstanding loans of Rs. 100 crores or more, or outstanding deposits of Rs. 25 crores or more).
- The Board must appoint a practicing company secretary in whole-time practice to conduct secretarial audit.
- The secretarial auditor must report in Form MR-3 to the company, and the company must file a copy with the Registrar along with the annual return.
- If the practicing company secretary finds that the company has defaulted in compliance with provisions of various enacted laws, the report must contain a qualification and explanation for such qualification.
- The company must place the secretarial audit report before the next annual general meeting and also publish it on its website.

Format of Secretarial Audit Report (MR-3):
- Clause (a): Whether proper records maintained under the Companies Act are preserved by the company
- Clause (b): Whether returns, forms, and documents were filed correctly with the Registrar
- Clause (c): Whether monies were received on time in relation to allotment, call, etc.
- Clause (d): Whether approvals were obtained as required
- Clause (e): Other compliance matters as prescribed
- Qualification, if any, must be separately stated with explanation

Other important compliances:
- Secretarial Standards (SS-1 on Meetings of Board, SS-2 on General Meetings) issued by ICSI have binding effect from June 7, 2017 onwards. All companies should comply with them. For listed entities, SEBI LODR additionally mandates compliance.
- ROC filings — annual returns (Form MGT-7), financial statements (Form AOC-4), board reports (DIR-12 for changes in directors)
- SEBI LODR compliance — continuous listing obligations, disclosure requirements, investor grievances
- FEMA compliance — ECB reporting, AD category I bank reporting
- RBI, IRDAI, SEBI, Ministry of Corporate Affairs regulations depending on the nature of business
- GDPR and digital data protection compliance becoming increasingly important

Penalties for non-compliance vary — some offences attract compoundable penalties, others are non-compoundable with rigorous imprisonment. The company secretary plays a critical role in ensuring timely and accurate compliance.

Section 7: Corporate Ethics and Ethical Decision-Making

Ethics refers to moral principles that govern a person's or an organisation's behaviour. In the corporate context, ethics provides the moral compass that goes beyond legal compliance — it asks not just "what can we do" but "what should we do."

Difference between compliance and ethics:
- Compliance means following the law and rules — it is externally imposed and minimum-standard oriented.
- Ethics involves internal moral judgment — it is aspirational and based on values such as honesty, integrity, fairness, and responsibility.
A company can be legally compliant yet ethically questionable (e.g., exploiting loopholes to avoid taxes). Conversely, ethical behaviour often leads to superior compliance because ethical cultures embed rule-abiding behaviour naturally.

Codes of Ethics:
- The ICSI Code of Ethics, framed under the Company Secretaries Act, 1980, contains 5 Fundamental Principles:
(i) Integrity — being straightforward and honest in professional and business relations
(ii) Objectivity — not allowing bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence to override professional judgments
(iii) Professional Competence and Due Care — maintaining professional knowledge and skill at required levels, ensuring clients receive competent professional service
(iv) Confidentiality — respecting the confidentiality of information acquired during the course of professional service
(v) Professional Behaviour — complying with relevant laws and regulations, avoiding any action that may discredit the profession
- The Companies Act, 2013 mandates every company to adopt a code of conduct for its Board and senior management (Regulation 17(1)(e) of SEBI LODR for listed companies).
- Whistleblower mechanism under Section 177(9) and (10) requires every listed company to establish a vigil mechanism and appoint an anchor officer to receive complaints.

Ethical Decision-Making Framework:
When facing ethical dilemmas, professionals should follow a structured approach:
1. Identify the ethical issue and stakeholders affected
2. Gather relevant facts and applicable laws/codes
3. Evaluate alternative courses of action using ethical lenses — utilitarian (greatest good), rights-based (respect for individual rights), justice-based (fairness), virtue-based (character)
4. Make a decision and act
5. Reflect on the outcome

Common ethical challenges in corporate practice:
- Conflict of interest — personal interests interfering with professional duties
- Confidentiality breaches — disclosing client/corporate information improperly
- Misleading statements in board reports or disclosures
- Pressure from management to suppress adverse findings
- Gift and hospitality acceptance crossing ethical boundaries

Case studies for examination:
- Satyam Scandal (2009): Falsified balance sheet worth Rs. 7,000 crores — highlighted failure of audit committee, internal audit, and board oversight. All three independent directors resigned.
- Yes Bank Case: Related party lending to Aditi Gupta — governance failure, board complicity, RBI intervention.
- IL&FS Crisis: Board failed in risk oversight, misclassification of debt as equity, related party transactions without disclosure — led to default and winding up proceedings.
- DHFL Scam: Misappropriation of funds by promoters — governance failure in board and audit committee.

Section 8: ESG — Environmental, Social and Governance Integration

ESG has emerged as a dominant paradigm in corporate governance, moving beyond shareholder primacy towards stakeholder capitalism. ESG factors are used by investors, regulators, and other stakeholders to assess a company's sustainability and ethical impact.

Environmental factors:
- Climate change risk and carbon footprint
- Energy efficiency and renewable energy adoption
- Water usage and waste management
- Biodiversity and pollution control
- Environmental litigation and regulatory compliance
- Green bonds and sustainable finance instruments

Social factors:
- Labour practices and working conditions
- Diversity, equity, and inclusion on the board and workforce
- Human rights in the supply chain
- Community engagement and local development
- Product safety, data privacy, and customer protection
- Employee health, safety, and well-being
- Indigenous rights and displacement issues

Governance factors:
- Board composition, diversity, and independence
- Executive remuneration alignment with long-term performance
- Anti-corruption and anti-bribery policies
- Political contributions and lobbying transparency
- Tax transparency and responsible tax practices
- Business ethics and code of conduct enforcement

SEBI LODR — Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR):
- BRSR is mandatory for top 1000 listed entities (by market capitalisation) under SEBI LODR, replacing the earlier Business Responsibility Report (BRR).
- BRSR Core applies to top 100 listed entities and covers general disclosures plus performance-based disclosures across nine Principle areas aligned with the National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC).
- The nine NGRBC principles: (1) Business should employ ethical, honest, and transparent practices, (2) Businesses should provide goods and services that are safe and contribute to sustainability throughout their life cycle, (3) Businesses should promote the wellbeing of all employees, (4) Businesses should respect the interests of and be responsive towards all its stakeholders, (5) Businesses should respect and promote human rights, (6) Businesses should respect, protect, and make efforts to restore the environment, (7) Businesses, when serving as employers, should empower women, (8) Businesses should support inclusive growth and equitable development, (9) Businesses should engage with and provide value to consumers in a responsible manner.
- BRSR must be certified by a practising company secretary or chartered accountant.

Global ESG frameworks:
- TCFD (Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures) — recommendations on governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics/targets related to climate risk
- SASB (Sustainability Accounting Standards Board) — industry-specific sustainability standards
- GRI (Global Reporting Initiative) — most widely used sustainability reporting standards globally
- ISSB (International Sustainability Standards Board) — issued IFRS S1 (general requirements) and IFRS S2 (climate-related disclosures) in 2023, aimed at creating a global baseline

The company secretary's role in ESG:
- Ensuring compliance with BRSR filing requirements
- Facilitating board discussions on ESG risks and opportunities
- Coordinating with internal and external stakeholders on ESG data collection
- Acting as a bridge between the board and investors on ESG matters
- Developing and monitoring ESG policies and frameworks
- Training board members on emerging ESG regulations and expectations

Section 9: Recent Developments, Amendments and Exam-Focused Hot Topics

Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017:
- Made secretarial audit mandatory for listed companies and large public companies.
- Enhanced penalties and introduced stricter provisions on related party transactions.
- Strengthened whistleblower protections under Section 177.

Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019:
- Introduced provisions for faster resolution of insolvent companies.
- Revised thresholds for certain compliance obligations.

SEBI LODR Amendment, 2021:
- Enhanced requirements for Board committees.
- Stricter disclosure norms for related party transactions.
- Requirements for disclosure of ESG metrics.
- Amendments on dividend distribution, buy-back, and share buy-back disclosures.

SEBI LODR Amendment, 2023:
- Mandatory BRSR Core for top 100 listed entities.
- Increased requirements for cybersecurity and data protection disclosures.
- Stricter norms for promotion of ESG-linked debt instruments.
- Amendments on board evaluation processes and reporting.

Ministry of Corporate Affairs — Companies (Significant Beneficial Owners) Rules, 2018:
- Requires identification and disclosure of persons exercising significant influence or control over a company.
- Important compliance area for company secretaries.

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (as amended):
- Board approval is required before initiating corporate insolvency resolution process.
- Committee of Creditors composition and functioning impacts governance.

NCLT/NCLAT Judgments relevant to governance:
- In re: Satyam Computer Services — Supreme Court upheld the replacement of the entire board and appointment of a fresh board, setting precedent for governance intervention.
- In re: IL&FS — NCLT observed serious governance lapses.
- In re: DHFL — governance failure led to NCLT proceeding.
- Various judgments on oppression and mismanagement where courts have examined board decisions' fairness.

Key amendments post-2020:
- Introduction of e-voting requirements being broadened.
- Relaxation and subsequent restoration of certain compliance relaxations originally granted during COVID-19.
- Greater emphasis on diversity on boards.
- Digital compliance tools and MCA21 enhancements enabling real-time filing and tracking.

Important for CS Exam: Students must be able to discuss how governance failures lead to corporate crises, analyse board composition in case studies, apply SEBI LODR provisions to given fact patterns, and write on the evolving role of the company secretary in governance, risk, and ethics compliance.

Important facts

  • Corporate governance is the system by which companies are directed and controlled, built on accountability, fairness, transparency, and independence.
  • SEBI LODR Regulation 17(1)(b) mandates every listed entity to constitute a Risk Management Committee of the Board with majority independent directors.
  • Independent directors can serve maximum two consecutive terms of five years each (Section 149 read with Section 164); reappointment requires a three-year cooling-off period.
  • Internal audit is mandatory under Section 138 for listed companies and public companies meeting specified financial thresholds (Rs. 50 cr paid-up capital / Rs. 200 cr turnover / Rs. 100 cr borrowings / Rs. 25 cr deposits).
  • Secretarial audit under Section 204 is mandatory for listed companies and large public companies; report must be filed in Form MR-3 with the ROC.
  • The Companies Act, 2013 codifies fiduciary duties of directors under Section 166 — to act in good faith, in the best interest of the company, to promote its objects, with due care and skill, and not to deal in self-interest without authorisation.
  • BRSR (Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report) is mandatory for top 1000 listed entities under SEBI LODR, with BRSR Core applicable to top 100 entities.
  • The three lines of defence model structures risk and compliance management: operational management (first line), compliance/risk functions (second line), and internal audit (third line).
  • ICSI's Code of Ethics comprises five fundamental principles: Integrity, Objectivity, Professional Competence and Due Care, Confidentiality, and Professional Behaviour.
  • SEBI LODR requires listed companies to establish a whistleblower mechanism under Regulation 17(1)(f) and Section 177(9)-(10) of the Companies Act.
  • The COSO ERM Framework (2017) integrates strategy and performance through five components: Governance & Culture, Strategy & Objective Setting, Performance, Review & Revision, and Information/Communication/Reporting.
  • COSO Internal Control Framework (1992, updated 2013) has five components: Control Environment, Risk Assessment, Control Activities, Information & Communication, and Monitoring Activities.
  • Section 177 mandates constitution of Audit Committee for listed companies with at least three directors, majority being independent directors.
  • Related party transactions under Section 188 require approval of the Board (for ordinary course transactions) or shareholders (for material transactions), with independent director concurrence.
  • The Satyam Scandal (2009) exposed catastrophic governance failure across the board, audit committee, and internal audit, leading to sweeping regulatory reforms in India.
