Quick Revision: Jurisprudence, Interpretation and General Laws — CS Executive Group 1

CSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Jurisprudence (from Latin 'juris' = law, 'prudentia' = knowledge) is the science or philosophy of law. It studies the nature, origin, development, and principles of law as a discipline separate from any particular legal system.
  • Natural Law Theory: A school of jurisprudence holding that law is derived from reason, divine will, or nature itself, and that unjust laws (lex injusta non est lex) are not true laws. Associated with Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas, and John Locke.
  • Legal Positivism: A school of thought asserting that law is the command of a sovereign backed by sanction. Law and morality are distinct; validity of a law depends on its source, not its moral content. Key figures: John Austin, H.L.A. Hart, Jeremy Bentham.
  • Historical School of Jurisprudence: Law is not made but found; it grows organically from the customs, traditions, and consciousness (Volksgeist) of a people. Founded by Savigny; associates include Pufendorf and Maine.
  • Realist School: Emphasizes law as it is actually applied by courts and officials rather than as written in books. American Realists: Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Karl Llewellyn; Scandinavian Realists: Axel Hägerström.
  • Sociological School: Law is a social instrument designed to balance competing interests and achieve social engineering. Roscoe Pound coined 'social engineering'; Eugen Ehrlich focused on 'living law'.
  • Legal Right: An interest recognized and protected by law, which the state enforces against interference. Every right has a corresponding duty. (Blackstone: 'There can be no right without a remedy.')
  • Legal Duty: An obligation imposed by law, binding a person to do or refrain from doing something. The correlative of a legal right. E.g., if A has a right to property, B has a duty not to trespass.
  • Possession: Factual control over a thing with the intention to exclude others (corpus possessionis + animus possidendi). Distinct from ownership; possession is a fact, ownership is a right.
  • Ownership (Property): The fullest right a person can have over a thing, including rights of possession, use, enjoyment, and disposition. It is exclusive and perpetual in nature.
  • Person: A legal entity capable of having rights and duties. Includes natural persons (human beings) and juristic/legal persons (companies, firms, temples, etc.). A company is a 'legal person' separate from its members (Salomon v. Salomon).
  • Interpretation of Statutes: The process of ascertaining the meaning of legislative language. Courts interpret statutes to discover the legislative intent and apply the law to factual situations.
  • Maxim: A legally recognised principle expressed in concise Latin or English phrases. E.g., 'Ubi jus ibi remedium' (where there is a right, there is a remedy); 'Lex posterior derogat priori' (a later law repeals an earlier one).
  • General Clauses Act, 1897: An interpretative statute containing definitions and general principles applicable across multiple central enactments unless the context otherwise requires. Section 3 provides standard definitions (e.g., 'document', 'person', 'offence').
  • Doctrine of Severability: If a part of a statute is unconstitutional or invalid, the remainder may survive if it is independent and severable from the invalid portion (Article 13, Constitution of India).
  • Purposive Interpretation: A modern approach where courts look beyond literal words to discern the purpose or object of the legislation and interpret provisions to advance that purpose.
  • Legal Remedy: The means by which a right is enforced or a wrong is redressed. Rooted in the maxim 'Ubi jus ibi remedium' — every legal right carries with it a legal remedy.

Key concepts

  • NATURE AND Necessity of Jurisprudence: Jurisprudence provides a theoretical foundation for understanding law. It helps in critical examination of legal concepts, harmonising legal rules, and evolving sound legal reasoning. For CS, it underpins the interpretative skills needed for Company Law and other statutes.
  • SOURCES OF LAW: Customs, Precedent (Case Law), Legislation (Statute), Equity, Justice, Equity and Good Conscience, Professional Literature (writings of jurists). Custom must be ancient, certain, reasonable, continuous, and not contrary to statute or public policy to be a valid source. Precedent (stare decisis) binds lower courts; legislation is the primary source in modern legal systems.
  • SCHOOLS OF JURISPRUDENCE — COMPARATIVE NOTES: Natural Law focuses on higher moral principles; Legal Positivism focuses on enacted law as command; Historical School emphasises organic growth from custom; Realist School looks at judicial behaviour; Sociological School treats law as social engineering. ICSI frequently asks identification-based and matching questions on these schools.
  • RIGHTS AND DUTIES — CLASSIFICATION: Rights may be claim-rights (correlative duty on another) or liberty-rights (no duty on others to refrain). Duties may be positive (to do) or negative (to refrain). Personal rights relate to individuals; proprietary rights relate to property.Absolute rights (against world) vs. relative rights (against specific persons).
  • LEGAL PERSONS: Natural persons are human beings from birth to death. Juristic persons include companies (incorporated under Companies Act), firms, trusts, temples, idols, municipalities, and the State. A company has perpetual succession, a common seal, and can sue/be sued in its own name. Minors are not persons in the full legal sense (cannot contract).
  • POSSESSION VS OWNERSHIP: Possession is de facto control (fact); ownership is de jure right (law). Possession can be actual or constructive, direct or indirect, lawful or unlawful. Ownership includes dominion over res. Roman law distinction: possessio vs. dominium. In Indian law, possessory protection is available even without title (Section 4 (4) of Specific Relief Act, 1963 — suit within 6 months of dispossession).
  • RULES OF STATUTORY INTERPRETATION — LITERAL RULE: Where the language is clear and unambiguous, give effect to the plain meaning even if it leads to absurd results. Applied in 'mischief and gap' cases only when the words are clear. Landmark illustration: Grover's case, AISSCE v. Parkash Chander (1962).
  • GOLDEN RULE: A modification of the literal rule — words are given their ordinary meaning unless that leads to an absurdity, inconsistency, or repugnance, in which case a sensible interpretation is adopted. Used to avoid irrational outcomes while respecting parliamentary sovereignty.
  • MISCHIEF RULE (Heydon's Case, 1584): Look to the 'mischief and defect' that the statute was intended to remedy; interpret the provision to suppress the mischief and advance the remedy. This is the traditional purposive approach and remains authoritative in Indian law.
  • PURPOSIVE / GOLDEN MODERN APPROACH: Modern Indian courts, especially the Supreme Court, adopt a purposive construction — reading the statute as a whole, considering preamble, object, and scheme, to give effect to legislative intent. This is reinforced by Article 358A of the Constitution (when inserted) and judicial precedents like CIT v. Hazarimal Somppa (1972) and India Benz Mills v. Asstt. CTO (1980).
  • CANONS OF CONSTRUCTION: Noscitur a sociis (a word is known by the company it keeps); Ejusdem generis (general words follow the genus of specific words); Expressio unius est exclusio alterius (expression of one is exclusion of others);reddenda est sensui (words must yield to sense); Beneficial construction (in favour of labour/consumer when ambiguous).
  • INTERNAL HELP (Intra Statute): Preamble, Definitions section, Schedules, Provisos, Explanation, Illustrations, Marginal notes (not decisive but helpful), Headings, Saving clauses. These help resolve ambiguity within the same enactment.
  • EXTERNAL HELP (Extra Statute): Reports of Law Commission, Parliamentary Debates (Statement of Objects and Reasons), Dictionaries, Treatises, Previous statutes on the same subject, International conventions, Judicial pronouncements, Constitutional ethos.
  • GENERAL CLAUSES ACT, 1897 — IMPORTANT SECTIONS: Section 3 defines key terms used across central laws: 'document' includes any matter expressed on any substance; 'person' includes any company or association; 'offence' means any act/omission punishable under any law; 'year' means calendar year; 'month' means calendar month; 'service' includes postal service; 'prescribed' means prescribed by rules. Section 21 deals with repeal and re-enactment effects.
  • CONFLICT OF LAWS AND REPUGNANCE: Article 254 of the Constitution governs inconsistency between Centre and State laws. If a central law and state law conflict on a Concurrent List subject, the central law prevails unless the state law received Presidential assent. In case of repugnancy, the central law generally overrides the state law.
  • LEGAL MAXIMS — ESSENTIAL FOR CS EXAM: Ubi jus ibi remedium (where there is a right, there is a remedy); Audi alteram partem (hear the other side — natural justice); Nemo judex in causa sua (no one should be a judge in his own cause);actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea (the act does not make a person guilty unless the mind is also guilty); Pacta sunt servanda (agreements must be kept); Res ipsa loquitur (the thing speaks for itself); Stare decisis (stand by what has been decided).
  • APPLIED TO COMPANY SECRETARY PRACTICE: A practising CS must understand jurisprudence to interpret the Companies Act, 2013 and allied statutes correctly. The rules of interpretation directly affect compliance, board resolutions, statutory filings, and legal opinions. Knowledge of maxims aids argumentation before authorities and tribunals.
  • INTERPRETATION IN COMPANY LAW CONTEXT: The Companies Act, 2013 uses the General Clauses Act, 1897 by incorporation. Sections like 2(1)(a)-(zzzz) contain definitions; the Preamble states the objectives; Schedule VII lists charitable activities. Courts read the Act harmoniously, giving effect to the object of promoting corporate governance and transparency.
  • SUBSEQUENT AMENDMENTS AND REPEALS: When a statute is amended, the amending Act is read with the principal Act as one instrument unless the contrary appears. When a statute is repealed and re-enacted, established interpretations of the old provision continue to apply to the new provision (doctrine of assimilated meanings).
  • EQUITY AND GOOD CONSCIENCE: In the absence of a specific legal provision, courts may decide based on equity, justice, and good conscience. This is particularly relevant in company matters where gaps exist. However, equity cannot override clear statutory language.
  • RELATIONS BETWEEN JURISPRUDENCE AND OTHER LAWS: Jurisprudence informs the foundational principles of Contract Act (consensus ad idem, consideration), IPC (mens rea, strict liability exceptions), Evidence Act (burden of proof, relevancy), and Company Law (fiduciary duties, ultra vires doctrine evolution).

Important facts

  • John Austin defined law as 'the command of the sovereign backed by sanction' — the classical positivist formula. His work 'The Province of Jurisprudence Determined' (1832) is seminal.
  • Friedrich Carl von Savigny founded the Historical School and propounded the concept of Volksgeist (spirit of the people) as the source of law.
  • Sir Henry Maine's famous proposition: 'All progressive societies have moved from Status to Contract' — capturing the evolution of legal relationships.
  • Roscoe Pound developed the theory of 'Social Engineering' in jurisprudence, viewing law as a tool to balance competing interests with minimum friction.
  • Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. stated: 'The life of the law has not been logic; it has been experience.' This became a cornerstone of American Legal Realism.
  • Heydon's Case (1584) 76 ER 637 is the foundational authority for the Mischief Rule of statutory interpretation.
  • Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd. (1897) AC 22 established the principle of separate legal personality of a incorporated company.
  • Article 13 of the Constitution of India declares any law inconsistent with fundamental rights as void — basis for judicial review and severability doctrine.
  • Article 254 of the Constitution deals with repugnancy between Centre and State legislations on Concurrent List entries.
  • Section 3 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 provides default definitions applicable to all Central Acts unless the context otherwise requires.
  • Section 21 of the General Clauses Act deals with the effect of repeals — saved rights, liabilities, and pending proceedings continue unless explicitly excluded.
  • The Latin maxim 'Stare decisis et non quieta movere' means 'to stand by decisions and not disturb matters which have been settled.'
  • In Indian jurisprudence, the Preamble to the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) is considered part of the basic structure and can be used to interpret ambiguous provisions.
  • The doctrine of 'colourable legislation' holds that a legislature cannot do indirectly what it cannot do directly — closely linked to legislative competence.
  • Natural Justice comprises two core rules: Audi alteram partem and Nemo judex in causa sua. Breach renders an administrative action voidable.
  • CIT v. Hazarimal Somppa (1972) 86 ITR 330 (SC) laid down that while construing a taxing statute, the court looks to the substance and not merely the form.
  • The term 'company' under Section 2(1)(d) of the Companies Act, 2013 means a company incorporated under this Act or under any previous company law.
  • 'Person' under Section 2(1)(pc) of the Companies Act, 2013 includes an individual, HUF, company, firm, association, body of individuals, or any artificial juridical person.
  • The doctrine of 'ultra vires' — acts beyond the powers conferred by the statute or memorandum — is void and cannot be ratified. For companies, it applies to objects clause.
  • Section 4 of the Companies Act, 2013 requires every company to have a Memorandum and Articles. The Memorandum defines the company's scope; the Articles contain regulations.
  • Under the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 3 defines 'fact' and 'relevant fact'; Section 101-114 deal with burden of proof; Section 114 contains presumptions.
  • IPC Section 6 distinguishes offences as either 'punishment' offences or those involving 'forfeiture of property'; Section 52 provides 'good faith' definition.
  • The term 'good faith' under Section 52 of the IPC means doing or believing something with due care and attention — not merely honest belief alone.
  • In Company Law, 'shadow director' is defined under Section 2(27A) of the Companies Act, 2013 — a person in accordance with whose directions or instructions the directors are accustomed to act.
  • The term 'related party' under Section 2(76) of the Companies Act, 2013 has a detailed definition covering holding, subsidiary, associate companies, directors,Key Managerial Personnel and their relatives.
  • The doctrine of 'piercing the corporate veil' allows courts to look behind the separate legal personality in cases of fraud, evasion of legal obligations, or agency relationships.
  • The phrase 'due diligence' is not defined in the Companies Act but has acquired a well-settled meaning in commercial and legal practice — reasonable steps a prudent person would take.
  • Section 2(1)(za) of the Companies Act, 2013 defines 'listed company' as one whose securities are listed on a stock exchange recognised by SEBI under SEBI Act, 1992.
  • The term 'securities' under Section 2(47) includes shares, bonds, debentures, derivative, deposit receipts, rights or interest in securities, and any other instrument declared securities by the central government.
  • Rule of interpretation: Harmonious construction — when two provisions appear irreconcilable, they should be read together so that each retains meaning and effect.
  • The expression 'may' is generally directory (permissive) while 'shall' is generally mandatory — but context determines the true character; courts examine substance over form.
  • Legal Maxims frequently tested: 'Actus curiae neminem gravabit' (an act of the court shall prejudice no one); 'Ignorantia juris non excusat' (ignorance of law is no excuse); 'Lex retro non agit' (law does not operate retrospectively unless expressly provided).
  • Judicial precedents are binding under Article 141 of the Constitution — the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within India.
  • In the absence of a specific provision, the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 are supplemented by the General Clauses Act, 1897, Indian Contract Act, 1872, and Indian Evidence Act, 1872 as applicable.
  • The term 'board' under the Companies Act, 2013 means the Board of Directors of a company (Section 2(10)).
  • 'Key Managerial Personnel' (KMP) under Section 2(51) includes the Managing Director, Whole-time Director, Chief Executive Officer, Company Secretary, and Chief Financial Officer.
  • The principle of 'beneficial ownership' has gained prominence post-2013 amendments; Section 90 mandates disclosure of significant beneficial owners in listed and large unlisted companies.
  • 'Statutory auditor' under Section 2(20) means a firm or individual eligible and appointed/registered under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 to audit company accounts.
  • The phrase 'financial year' under Section 2(38) means the financial year specified in Section 2(c) of the Companies Act, 2013 or under Section 3 of the Annual Accounts Act, 1984 — being April to March.
  • Section 12 of the Companies Act, 2013 requires every company to have a registered office and to notify its address to the Registrar within 30 days of incorporation.
  • Legal personality confers upon a company the capacity to hold property, enter contracts, sue and be sued — but the company acts through its human agents (directors, officers).
  • When interpreting any provision of the Companies Act, the MCA Guidelines, Circulars, and Notifications issued by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs are relevant extraneous materials.
  • The principle of 'purposive construction' has been consistently applied by the Supreme Court in tax, commercial, and company law matters to give effect to the legislative intent rather than a hyper-technical reading.
  • The 'Rule in Ramsey's Case' (tax law) — though primarily fiscal — illustrates that courts examine the substance of transactions rather than their form, a principle equally relevant in company law compliance scrutiny.
  • The doctrine of 'delegatus non potest delegare' (a delegate cannot further delegate) applies to statutory functions — a body authorised to make rules cannot further delegate rule-making power unless the statute permits.
  • 'Natural person' acquires legal personality at birth and loses it at death. Legal/juristic persons acquire personality upon creation by statute or registration and lose it upon dissolution.
  • Section 7 of the Companies Act, 2013 requires a minimum number of subscribers (2 for public, 1 for private) to incorporate a company, who must subscribe to the Memorandum.
  • The term 'compliant company' under Section 2(61) means a company that has complied with the provisions of the Act, Rules, and regulations applicable to it.
  • When a provision is ambiguous, the court may refer to the Statement of Objects and Reasons accompanying the Bill, Law Commission Reports, and committee reports — these are legitimate aids under the external help category.
  • Legal maxims have been repeatedly relied upon by Indian courts: e.g., 'Audi alteram partem' in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) to expand the scope of natural justice under Article 21.

Memory aids

  • mnemonic for Schools of Jurisprudence: 'N-H-R-S-L' → Natural, Historical, Realist, Sociological, Legal Positivist. Think: 'Naturals Have Really Serious Problems' to recall all five major schools.
  • mnemonic for Rights classification: 'CLPD' → Claim, Liberty, Power, Immunity — Wesley's four types of legal rights (often tested in comparative questions).
  • Mnemonic for maxims: 'A-U-N-P-R' → Audi alteram partem, Ubi jus ibi remedium, Nemo judex, Pacta sunt servanda, Res ipsa loquitur. Imagine a courtroom: 'An Ugly Nurse Punched a Rat'.
  • Maine's phrase 'From Status to Contract' — memorise as a timeline: ancient societies = status-based (family/feudal); modern societies = contract-based (free agreement).
  • For interpretation aids mnemonic: 'PEDRICS' → Preamble, Explanations, Definitions, Schedules, Reports, Illustrations, Cross-references, Statements of Objects. These are the internal and external aids a CS must know.
  • Remember 'SALOMON' for separate legal personality: 'Separate Alien Legal Entity Owned Minus one Owner's liability' — the company is separate from shareholders.
  • For the three main rules of interpretation: 'L-G-M' → Literal, Golden, Mischief. Remember: 'Lawyers Generally Master statutes' — start with literal, modify with golden, go deeper with mischief/purpose.
  • Memorise these three Latin maxims with their English equivalents: 'Ubi jus ibi remedium' = Where there is a right, there is a remedy; 'Audi alteram partem' = No one should be condemned unheard; 'Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea' = The act alone does not make one guilty without a guilty mind.
  • For General Clauses Act Section 3 terms: mnemonic 'DOPPS' → Document, Offence, Person, Year, Service — all common definitions tested directly.
  • Remember the hierarchy: Constitution > Statute > Subordinate Legislation > Custom. Customs are valid only if not inconsistent with statute and meeting the five conditions.
  • For types of persons: 'N-J-P' → Natural, Juristic, Personal (minors, lunatics). Juristic persons include companies, temples, idols, firms, trusts — remember 'CIF-T' → Company, Idol, Firm — Trust.
  • The phrase 'Corporate Veil' can be remembered by the image: a company is like a puppet; the veil is the puppet screen. Courts sometimes pull back the screen to see who is really controlling (fraud/evasion cases).
  • For 'shall' vs 'may': 'SHALL = MUST, MAY = CAN' — but add 'Context is King' as the exception reminder, because courts sometimes interpret 'shall' as directory and 'may' as mandatory based on purpose.
  • To recall Savigny's contribution: think 'SAVIGNY = Spirit Of Volk's Genius' — the Volksgeist concept connects law to the cultural spirit of the people.
  • For Pound's Social Engineering: picture law as an engineer building a dam to channel conflicting interests — controlling water flow with minimum resistance = social engineering with minimum friction.

Exam tips

  • For CS Executive, focus on APPLICATION-based questions — you will be asked to identify which school of jurisprudence a given statement belongs to, or which rule of interpretation applies to a specific scenario.
  • Practice identifying maxims from their Latin phrase and matching them with their English meaning and application area — both are frequently tested in objective and descriptive formats.
  • Learn the exact definitions from the General Clauses Act, 1897 (Section 3) — questions often ask which term is correctly defined and which is incorrectly matched.
  • When answering descriptive questions on interpretation, always structure your answer: (1) State the rule, (2) Give its origin/case law, (3) Explain how it applies, (4) Conclude with the result — this mirrors ICSI's expected format.
  • Differentiate clearly between Internal Aids (preamble, definitions, schedules, provisos, illustrations, marginal notes) and External Aids (travaux préparatoires, dictionaries, law commission reports, precedents) — classify accurately in answers.
  • Remember that for CS exams, the connection between Jurisprudence and Company Law is critical — be prepared to explain how interpretative principles apply specifically to the Companies Act, 2013 provisions.
  • For Rights and Duties, prepare the classification tables (claim/liberty/power/immunity; absolute/relative; personal/proprietary) with one example each — this visual organisation scores well in descriptive answers.
  • Do not confuse the three main interpretative rules — LITERAL (plain words), GOLDEN (avoid absurdity), MISCHIEF (remedy the defect). Each has a distinct trigger and application sequence.
  • When writing about Natural Law vs. Legal Positivism, highlight the key difference: Natural Law subjects law to moral test; Positivism separates law from morality. Mention Austin's command theory vs. Aquinas's divine reason.
  • For the Corporate Veil doctrine, know both the general rule (Salomon) and the exceptions (fraud, agency, group enterprises, statutory provisions) — exam may ask you to list circumstances where the veil is lifted.
  • Revision strategy: First master all maxims (at least 15), then schools of jurisprudence (5 major ones), then interpretation rules (3 main + canons), then General Clauses Act key sections. Build from concepts to application.
  • In the exam, when asked to interpret a Companies Act provision, begin with the literal rule, mention that if ambiguity arises you will resort to purposive interpretation, and cite relevant judicial pronouncements where possible.
  • For 'Person' concept, remember that a company is a 'artificial juridical person' — it can own property, contract, sue, but cannot be punished with imprisonment (only fines) unless specifically provided.
  • Always distinguish between 'void' and 'voidable' acts in the context of ultra vires — ultra vires acts are void ab initio (no legal effect), while voidable acts are valid until set aside.
  • When revising, create a comparison chart of all five schools of jurisprudence: founder, core proposition, view on law-morality relationship, and a representative case or principle — this single chart covers maximum exam ground.

Common traps

  • Confusing 'possessing a right' with 'being a person' — minors and lunatics are persons but their rights are exercised through guardians; they can still own property and sue.
  • Assuming every maxim is a binding rule of law — maxims are principles of interpretation and equity, not statutory law. They aid but do not override clear statutory language.
  • Treating 'Literal Rule' as absolute — in modern Indian jurisprudence, the purposive approach often supersedes strict literalism when literal reading produces absurdity or defeats the legislative object.
  • Thinking that 'General Clauses Act' definitions apply to every law automatically — Section 3 says 'unless the context otherwise requires'; special statutes may define terms differently.
  • Confusing 'Possession' with 'Ownership' — possession is a fact (factual control); ownership is a right (legal title). One can possess without owning (tenant) and own without possessing (landlord).
  • Assuming 'shall' is always mandatory and 'may' is always permissive — the context and purpose of the provision determine the true character; courts have held 'shall' as directory and 'may' as mandatory in specific situations.
  • Believing that 'Precedent' binds all courts equally — only decisions of higher courts bind lower courts; decisions of coordinate benches are persuasive, not binding; SC decisions bind all under Article 141.
  • Forgetting that 'Juristic persons' like companies come into existence only on incorporation (Issue of Certificate of Incorporation under Section 7 read with Section 7 of Companies Act, 2013) and cease to exist only on dissolution.
  • Applying 'Ultra Vires' doctrine too broadly — it applies to acts beyond the memorandum (object clause), not mere irregularities in procedure. Ultra vires acts are void ab initio and cannot be ratified.
  • Mixing up 'Natural Law' with 'Positive Law' — Natural Law derives from morality/reason; Positive Law is enacted by human authority. A law can be positivistically valid yet morally objectionable.
  • Thinking that 'Preamble' is not part of the statute — while not enforceable as an independent right, the Preamble is a vital aid to interpretation and reveals the purpose and spirit of the enactment.
  • Confusing 'Legal Maxim' with 'Legal Principle' — all maxims are principles, but not all principles are maxims. Maxims are short, traditional Latin formulations; principles can be full propositions of law.
  • Assuming that 'Equity' overrides statute — equity fills gaps where law is silent but cannot override clear statutory provisions. In case of conflict between statute and equitable maxim, statute prevails.
  • Treating 'Custom' as equivalent to 'Statute' — customs are secondary sources and must satisfy strict conditions (ancient, certain, reasonable, continuous, not contrary to statute); they yield to express statutory provisions.
  • Forgetting that 'Shadow Director' is a statutory concept under Section 2(27A) — it is not the same as a nominal or pseudo director; the test is whether regular directors are accustomed to act according to the shadow director's directions.
  • Assuming every interpretation technique applies equally — LITERAL, GOLDEN, and MISCHIEF rules are applied hierarchically: first literal, then golden if absurd, then mischief/purposive if ambiguity remains.
  • Confusing 'Beneficial Ownership' with 'Registered Ownership' — beneficial owner enjoys economic benefits but may not be on the register; Section 90 disclosures bridge this gap, especially for listed companies.
  • Using marginal notes as decisive aids — marginal notes are prepared by drafters and are not part of the enacted text; they are only aids of last resort when the substantive text is ambiguous.
  • Treating 'External aids' as equal to 'Internal aids' — internal aids (preamble, definitions, schedules) carry more weight; external aids (travaux préparatoires, law commission reports) are supplementary and used only when internal aids fail.
  • Applying 'Doctrine of Severability' mechanically — the invalid part must be truly separable; if the valid and invalid parts are inextricably mixed, the entire provision may fall.
  • Assuming 'Statement of Objects and Reasons' is part of the statute — it is an external aid explaining the rationale but is not itself enacted law; however, courts frequently rely on it for purposive interpretation.
  • Confusing the 'Rule in Bennet's Case' with general interpretation — Bennet's Case established that punctuation and captions are not part of the enactment; modern practice sometimes treats headings differently but the principle remains relevant.
