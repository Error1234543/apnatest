Definitions

  • Sole Proprietorship: A business owned and managed by a single individual who bears unlimited liability; no separate legal entity exists apart from the owner. Registered under the Shop and Establishment Act or state-specific regulations but not under the Companies Act. The proprietor's personal assets are at risk for business debts.
  • Partnership: A relation between persons who have agreed to share profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. Not a separate legal entity from partners in the same way as a company (per Partner & Co. v. CIT), though the Partnership Act treats it as distinct for certain purposes. Partners bear unlimited joint and several liability.
  • Hindu Undivided Family (HUF): A form of business recognized under Hindu Law where the karta manages family properties and business. The HUF is treated as a separate assessable unit for tax purposes but is not governed by a statute like the Companies Act.
  • Company under Companies Act, 2013: An artificial person created by law, having a separate legal entity, perpetual succession, and a common seal. It can own property, sue and be sued in its own name. Defined under Section 2(20) of the Companies Act, 2013. Its liability may be limited by shares or by guarantee.
  • One Person Company (OPC): A company having only one person as a member (Section 2(62)). Introduced by Companies (Amendment) Act, 2015. The sole member can nominate another person (with consent) who shall become member on death or incapacity of the original member. Governed by Section 2(62) and Sections 45-51 of the Companies Act, 2013.
  • Section 8 Company: A company formed for promoting charitable objects such as commerce, art, science, sports, education, research, social welfare, religion, charity, protection of environment, or any other useful purpose. It applies its profits or income towards promoting its objects and prohibits payment of dividend to its members. Governed by Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013; requires license from the Central Government.
  • Memorandum of Association (MoA): The charter of the company, defining its scope of powers and operations. It is the foundation document filed with the Registrar at the time of incorporation. Governed by Section 4 of the Companies Act, 2013. Contains six clauses: Name, Registered Office, Object, Liability, Capital, and Subscription.
  • Articles of Association (AoA): The bylaws or rulebook of the company regulating management and internal procedures. Filed alongside the MoA at incorporation. Governed by Section 5 of the Companies Act, 2013. It defines the relationship between the company and its members, and among members themselves.
  • Prospectus: Any document described or called a prospectus, inviting subscriptions for shares or debentures of a body corporate (Section 2(70)). Includes lead managers' invitations, red herring prospectus, shelf prospectus, and offer letter. Key document triggering civil and criminal liability for misstatement.
  • Floating Charge: A charge created on fluctuating assets (e.g., stock-in-trade, book debts) which does not attach to the property until some further step is taken (e.g., crystallization). The chargor can deal with the charged assets in the ordinary course of business until crystallization.
  • Fixed Charge: A charge over specific, identifiable assets (e.g., land, building, plant, machinery) that attaches immediately upon creation. The chargor cannot deal with the charged asset without the chargee's consent.
  • Doctrine of Ultra Vires: Acts beyond the powers conferred by the MoA are void and cannot be ratified even by unanimous consent of shareholders. Named company law principle — 'ultra vires' means beyond the powers. Protects shareholders and creditors from risk-taking outside the defined object.
  • Constructive Notice: Every person dealing with a company is deemed to have notice of the contents of its public documents (MoA, AoA, charges, etc.) filed with the Registrar. They are expected to read and understand these documents before contracting with the company.
  • Indoor Management Rule (Turquand's Case): A person dealing with a company is entitled to assume that internal procedural requirements have been duly complied with, provided the transaction appears regular on its face. Protects outsiders from undisclosed irregularities within the company.
  • Employer: Under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, includes any person who has entrusted the management of an establishment to another person, and also includes the manager or managing agent. Specific definitions vary across different labour statutes.
  • Workman: Under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, means any person employed in any industry to do manual, unskilled, skilled, technical, operational, clerical, or supervisory work for hire or reward, drawing wages up to Rs. 18,000 per month (supervisory staff drawing wages up to Rs. 18,000 and exercising either mainly managerial functions or functions of administration). Excludes persons primarily in a managerial or administrative capacity or those drawing above the specified wage limit.

Key concepts

  • Business Forms Comparison — Sole Proprietorship vs Partnership vs Company vs OPC vs Section 8: A sole proprietor has single ownership, unlimited liability, no separate legal personality, and simple compliance. A partnership has 2-20 members (per Partnership Act), unlimited liability, and is governed by mutual agency. A company is a separate legal entity with limited liability, perpetual succession, and statutory compliance. An OPC has one member with limited liability and simplified compliance. A Section 8 company is for charitable objects with prohibition of dividend and special licensing.
  • Process of Incorporation of a Company: (1) Obtain Digital Signature Certificate (DSC) for proposed directors/subscribers. (2) Apply for Director Identification Number (DIN) if not already held. (3) Reserve/propose company name through RUN (Reserve Unique Name) service on MCA portal. (4) Draft MoA and AoA. (5) File incorporation documents including SPICe+ FORM (Part A for reservation, Part B for incorporation) with Registrar. (6) Pay stamp duty and fees. (7) Receive Certificate of Incorporation from ROC. The certificate is conclusive evidence that all requirements have been complied with (Section 11).
  • Six Clauses of MoA (Section 4): (i) Name Clause — company's name ending with 'Private Limited' or 'Limited' as applicable. (ii) Registered Office Clause — state in which registered office is situated. (iii) Object Clause — main objects, objects necessary for attainment of main objects, and other objects. (iv) Liability Clause — whether liability is limited by shares or guarantee. (v) Capital Clause — authorized share capital divided into nominal values of shares. (vi) Subscription Clause — declaration of intention to form company and take minimum shares by subscribers.
  • Doctrine of Ultra Vires and Its Effects: Any act beyond the MoA is void ab initio — it cannot be ratified. Directors personally liable for unauthorized acts. Shareholders can file a derivative suit to restrain ultra vires acts. Money spent on ultra vires acts cannot be claimed against the company. This doctrine protects investors by ensuring the company operates within its defined sphere.
  • Constructive Notice vs Indoor Management Rule: Constructive notice means outsiders are deemed to know contents of public documents. Indoor management (Royal British Bank v Turquand) provides the corollary — outsiders can assume internal procedures are properly followed. Exception: outsider with actual knowledge of irregularity, or where circumstances are suspicious requiring inquiry. BALCI v SIFAT [1967] is a leading case.
  • Alteration of Memorandum: MoA can be altered subject to statutory procedures: Name (Section 13(1)(a)), Registered Office (Section 13(1)(b)), Objects (Section 13(1)(c) — special resolution + Central Govt confirmation, NCLT), Liability (Section 13(1)(d)), Capital (Section 13(1)(e) — special resolution), Subdivision/Consolidation of Shares (Section 43). Each alteration must be filed with ROC within prescribed time. Alteration does not affect rights of members or creditors unless specifically provided.
  • Classification of Companies (Section 2): Private Company (Section 2(68)): minimum 2 members, maximum 200 members, restricts right to transfer shares, prohibits invitation to public. Public Company (Section 2(71)): no upper limit on members. One Person Company (Section 2(62)): single member. Small Company (Section 2(85)): as notified by Central Government. Dormant Company (Section 455): formed for future project or holding asset but not in operation. Producer Company (Section 581A-E): registered under Cooperative Societies Act but governed by Companies Act provisions. National Company: government-owned with 51%+ paid-up capital held by Central/State Government(s).
  • Charge Creation and Registration (Sections 77-79): A charge means an interest or lien created on property or assets of a company as security for a debt. Must be registered with ROC within 30 days of creation (extendable to 300 days with extra fee). Floating charge over undertaking/property requires registration of particulars. Failure to register renders the charge void against a liquidator and creditors. Debenture holders with fixed charge have priority over floating charge holders.
  • Prospectus and Liability for Misstatements: A prospectus inviting subscriptions must disclose material particulars (Section 26). Statement in prospectus that is untrue or misleading renders the issuer, directors, persons named as directors, and experts liable. Civil liability under Section 34 for damages. Criminal liability under Section 35 for false statements. No liability if statement was withdrawn or corrected. Protection available if prospectus was issued without knowledge of misstatement and with consent.
  • Pre-incorporation Contracts: A contract entered into before formation of company is a pre-incorporation contract. Such contracts are not enforceable against the company as it did not exist. Upon incorporation, the company may adopt the contract by novation (fresh agreement between same parties with new terms). Common law principle from Twine v E&N Sanitary Stores.
  • Shelf Prospectus (Section 32): A company may file a shelf prospectus instead of a fresh prospectus when issuing securities from time to time. Required for government securities and high-credit-ratings. Allows flexibility in timing of offers without repeated prospectus filings.
  • Fixed vs Floating Charge — Crystallization: A floating charge remains indefinite over changing assets until crystallization occurs. Crystallization events include: cessation of business, commencement of winding up, giving notice of crystallization, appointment of receiver/manager. Once crystallized, the floating charge becomes a fixed charge over the assets existing at that time. A fixed charge attaches immediately and does not require crystallization.
  • Key Differences — Private vs Public Company: Private company restricts transfer of shares, limits members to 200, prohibits public invitation. Public company allows free transferability (subject to AoA), has no upper member limit, can invite public subscription. Special provisions apply to wholly-owned subsidiaries converting to private companies.
  • Reconstruction vs Amalgamation: Reconstruction involves alteration of share capital or reorganization within the existing company or its merger with another to form a new entity — often by sale of assets to a new company. Amalgamation (Section 23AA, now largely subsumed under Scheme provisions) involves two or more companies merging into one. Both require NCLT approval and satisfy creditor/shareholder interests.
  • Factory Act, 1948 — Key Provisions: Application: every premises where manufacturing process is carried on with 10+ workers (using power) or 20+ workers (without power). Defines 'factory', 'worker', 'young person', 'child'. Covers health (cleanliness, ventilation, lighting, temperature, dust/fumes), safety (fencing machinery, fire precautions), welfare (washing facilities, first aid, canteen, creche), employment of young persons and women, hours of work (max 48 hrs/week, max 9 hrs/day), overtime wages, leave with wages.

Memory aids

  • MoA Six Clauses Mnemonic — NROLCS: Name, Registered Office, Object, Liability, Capital, Subscription. Remember: 'NO ROSLY CAN SEE' — or 'Never Risk Our Company's Loss Seriously' → N-R-O-C-L-S.
  • Ultra Vires Meaning: 'Ultra' = beyond, 'Vires' = powers. Acts beyond the MoA powers are void. Recall: 'If you go beyond your charter, nobody can force the company to honor it — not even all shareholders together.'
  • Constructive Notice vs Indoor Management: Constructive notice = 'The world is presumed to know what is on public record.' Indoor management = 'You can trust internal procedures are proper.' Think: You are notified externally (constructive notice) but shielded internally (indoor management).
  • Type of Charges Mnemonic — FFF: Fixed (on specific immovable/identified assets), Floating (on changing current assets like stock/debts), Fiduciary/Financial (debentures secured). Crystallization converts Floating → Fixed.
  • Companies Classification Quick Map: (2) Private = max 200 members, restricted transfer, no public invite. (71) Public = no restriction. (62) OPC = one member. (85) Small = as per notification limits. (455) Dormant = sleep mode. (8) Section 8 = charitable objects.
  • Labour Laws Pillars — Factory Act: Health, Safety, Welfare, Employment of Young Persons, Hours/Wages. Remember as 'HSWEH': Health, Safety, Welfare, Employment, Hours.
  • INCORPORATION Steps Mnemonic — 'PERSON': Prepare DSC, Obtain DIN, Submit RUN (name), Execute MoA/AoA, Navigate SPICe+ filing, Receive Certificate.

Exam tips

  • For CS Executive descriptive papers, always cite the relevant section number when writing answers — e.g., 'Under Section 4 of the Companies Act, 2013...' — this significantly enhances answer credibility and carries marks.
  • When asked about MoA clauses, structure your answer clause-by-clause with a one-line explanation of each. Never skip any of the six clauses — examiners expect completeness.
  • For ultra vires questions, mention both the doctrine and its consequences (void ab initio, personal liability of directors, shareholder remedy) to secure full marks.
  • In Labour Laws questions, distinguish clearly between the applying thresholds (10/20 workers for Factory Act, 10/20 for EPF Act, etc.) — these numerical thresholds are frequently tested.
  • When comparing forms of business, use a comparative table format in your answer for maximum clarity. Cover: legal status, liability, compliance burden, raising capital, and perpetuity.
  • For Chapter Test style recall: memorize the numeric limits — OPC (1 member), Private company (2-200 members), Public company (min 7, formerly; min 2 now after 2019 amendment), Section 8 (charitable objects, no dividend), Small Company (as per notification).
  • Remember: Certificate of Incorporation is conclusive evidence under Section 11 — no subsequent challenge can invalidate it on grounds of defective incorporation.
  • In answer writing for industrial laws, always mention the year of enactment (e.g., Factories Act, 1948; Industrial Disputes Act, 1947; EPF & MP Act, 1952; Payment of Wages Act, 1936) as this demonstrates knowledge breadth.
  • For question about charge registration: remember the 30-day rule with 300-day extension, and state clearly the consequence of non-registration — void against liquidator and creditors, NOT void between parties.
  • Distinguish carefully between reconstruction (altering capital structure) and amalgamation (merging two or more companies). Both need NCLT approval but follow different procedural routes.

Common traps

  • Confusing Ultra Vires with Intra Vires: Ultra vires = beyond powers (void); Intra vires = within powers (valid). A common trap is thinking ultra vires acts can be ratified by shareholders — they CANNOT. Only intra vires acts can be ratified.
  • Confusing Private and Public Company Limits: Private company maximum members = 200 (excluding employees and ex-employees). Public company has no upper limit. The lower limit for both is 2. Confusing these numbers is a frequent error.
  • Constructive Notice Does Not Protect Everyone: Constructive notice protects the company, not the outsider. The indoor management rule protects the outsider. Students often reverse the direction of protection.
  • MoA vs AoA Confusion: MoA defines the outer boundary of company's powers (external); AoA defines internal rules and regulations (internal). An act violating AoA is intra vires the company but may be irregular internally. An act violating MoA is ultra vires and void.
  • Registration of Charge Time Limit: 30 days from creation, extendable to 300 days with additional fees. For debentures, charge must be registered within 30 days of issue. Failure = charge void against liquidator and creditors. Many students forget the 300-day extension provision.
  • Section 8 Company Cannot Pay Dividend: Section 8 companies apply profits toward charitable objects and cannot distribute dividends. This is a fundamental difference from normal companies. Do not confuse with dormant companies which simply hold assets without active business.
  • Prospectus Definition Breadth: A prospectus includes not just traditional offering documents but also 'lead manager's invitation' and 'offer letter.' Even documents not titled 'prospectus' may attract prospectus liability if they invite public subscription.
  • Floating Charge vs Security Interest: Floating charge covers shifting assets; fixed charge attaches to identified assets. The critical practical distinction is what the company can do with charged assets before crystallization — with a floating charge, it can deal freely; with a fixed charge, it cannot.
  • Workman Definition Limits: Under ID Act, workman excludes those drawing above Rs. 18,000/month or those primarily in managerial/administrative capacity. Do not apply this definition to other statutes — each labour law has its own definition of employee/workman.
  • OPC Conversion Rules: An OPC must convert into a private company if paid-up capital exceeds Rs. 50 lakh or turnover exceeds Rs. 2 crore. These thresholds are revised periodically by notification — check latest figures for exam accuracy.
