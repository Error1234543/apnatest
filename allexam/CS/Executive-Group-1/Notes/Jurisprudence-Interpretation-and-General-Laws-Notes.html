1. Jurisprudence — Meaning, Scope and Schools of Thought

Jurisprudence is the study and philosophy of law — it examines what law is, where it comes from, why it exists, how it operates, and what its ideals should be. For the CS Executive examination, you must understand jurisprudence not as abstract theorising alone but as the conceptual backbone that makes sense of every statute you will later apply as a Company Secretary.

Major Schools of Jurisprudence:

• Natural Law School: Holds that law is derived from reason, morality, divine will or nature; a human law contrary to natural justice is not true law. Key thinkers: Aristotle, Cicero, St. Augustine, Thomas Aquinas. Modern relevance appears in Fundamental Rights under Part III of the Constitution and the principle that 'no one can be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law' — the procedure itself must be fair, just and reasonable.

• Analytical (or positivist) School: Law is the command of the sovereign, backed by sanctions. It focuses on law 'as it is', not 'as it ought to be'. Key thinkers: John Austin, John Salmond, H.L.A. Hart. Austin defined law as 'command of the sovereign backed by threat of sanction'. Modern criticisms note that not all law is command (e.g., conferment statutes), the sovereign concept is vague, and customary law exists without formal command.

• Historical School: Law is not made but found — it grows organically from the Volkgeist (spirit of the people). Key thinker: Friedrich Carl von Savigny. Law develops like language and custom, reflecting national character. This school explains why legal systems differ across nations and why transplanting foreign law often fails without adaptation.

• Sociological School: Law must be studied in its social context and should serve social purposes. Key thinkers: Roscoe Pound, Eugen Ehrlich. Pound spoke of law as social engineering — balancing competing interests (individual, public, social). This school underpins welfare legislation and judicial activism.

• Legal Realism School: Law is what courts actually do, not merely what statutes say. Key thinkers: Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., Jerome Frank. 'The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience.' Realism emphasises judicial discretion, socio-economic background of judges, and unpredictability in outcomes.

• American Realist / Critical Legal Studies / Law and Economics movements have also influenced contemporary jurisprudence, but for CS Executive focus on Natural, Analytical, Historical and Sociological schools in adequate depth.

Contemporary trends include Feminist Jurisprudence (critiquing patriarchal bias in law), Dalit Jurisprudence (examining caste-based legal discrimination), Ecological Jurisprudence (rights of nature, environmental protection), and Post-modern jurisprudence (questioning grand narratives of law).

2. Sources of Law and Evolution of Legal Systems

Sources of law indicate where legal rules derive their authority and binding force. For CS Executive, master these systematically:

• Custom: One of the oldest sources. A long-established practice accepted as binding. Requirements for valid custom: antiquity, continuity, reasonableness, certainty, not opposed to statute or public policy. Custom can be local (applicable to a region) or special (applicable to a particular community or trade). Under the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (Section 35), customs relevant to family, tribe, corporation or locality may be proved.

• Legislation (Statute): The most important modern source. Law enacted by the competent legislative authority. Types: Original (primary legislation by Parliament/State Legislatures), Delegated (subordinate legislation — rules, regulations, bye-laws framed by executive under parent Act), and Constituent legislation (making or amending the Constitution).

• Judicial Precedent (Case Law): Doctrine of stare decisis — courts follow decisions of higher courts. India follows a hierarchical precedent system: Supreme Court decisions bind all courts under Article 141; High Courts bind subordinate courts within their jurisdiction. Distinguish binding precedent from persuasive precedent (e.g., Foreign judgments, Privy Council decisions pre-1950, obiter dicta).

• Equity: Developed to mitigate rigour of common law; principles of conscience, fairness and good faith. Now fused with law in most jurisdictions. Indian courts apply equitable principles in specific remedies — injunctions, specific performance, trusts.

• Justice, Reason and Good Conscience: Used when no explicit rule exists; courts apply universal moral principles.

• Legal Scholarly Writings: Works of recognised jurists are persuasive, especially in areas lacking clear statutory coverage. Salmond, Austin, Dicey, Pollock are frequently cited.

• International Law: Treaties and conventions become part of Indian law when incorporated by legislation (e.g., human rights treaties given effect through domestic statutes).

Evolution of Legal Systems:

• Common Law System: Originated in England; based on judge-made law and precedent. Features: adversarial process, separation of legislature and judiciary in law-making role, case-centric. Countries: UK, USA, India, Australia, Canada.

• Civil Law (Code) System: Based on comprehensive written codes; Roman law heritage. Features: inquisitorial process, code as primary source, judges apply rather than make law. Countries: France, Germany, Japan, most of Latin America.

• Socialist Law System: Law is an instrument of the state to achieve socialist goals; property relations central. Post-Soviet states have largely moved away.

• Islamic Law (Sharia): Derived from Quran, Sunnah, Ijma, Qiyas. Applies in Muslim-majority countries; personal law aspect relevant to Indian Muslims.

• Hindu Law: Ancient dharma shastras; now codified in HMA, HSM, HAA, HIA. Still partially applicable.

• Buddhist, Jewish and other religious legal traditions exist but are less central for the CS syllabus.

3. Nature of Law — Rights, Duties, Ownership, Possession and Legal Personality

These core concepts form the grammar of any legal system and are repeatedly tested.

• Right and Duty: A right is a legally recognised claim enforceable against another. Salmond classified rights into: (i) Perfect rights (enforceable in court — e.g., property rights); (ii) Imperfect rights (not directly enforceable but recognised — e.g., time-barred debts). Every right correlates with a duty. Classifications: (a) Absolute rights (owed to world at large — e.g., right to life, reputation); (b) Relative rights (owed to specific persons — e.g., contractual rights). Corresponding duties: Positive (to act) vs. Negative (to abstain).

• Ownership: The most extensive right in a thing. Salmond described ownership as 'right of property in a corporeal thing existing in one person and capable of indefinite duration'. Essential elements: (i) Vastness of extent — widest possible right; (ii) Absoluteness — no inherent limit on user; (iii) Exclusiveness — excludes all others; (iv) Perpetuity — no time limit; (v) Liability to seizure — subject to state action. Ownership under Indian law is regulated by the Transfer of Property Act, 1882; Indian Easements Act, 1882; Indian Succession Act, 1925; and various state land revenue laws. Differentiate between absolute ownership (common law ideal) and qualified ownership (e.g., government property, ancestral coparcenary property under Hindu law).

• Possession: Factual control over a thing with the intention to exclude others (corpus + animus). Kinds: (i) Possession in law vs. possession in fact; (ii) Visible vs. invisible possession; (iii) Continuous vs. discontinuous; (iv) Actual vs. constructive; (v) Direct vs. derivative; (vi) Legal vs. illegal; (vii) Good vs. bad possession (relevant for adverse possession under Limitation Act). Difference from ownership: possession is a fact, ownership is a right; possessor may not be owner (e.g., bailee, tenant, mortgagee in possession).

• Legal Personality: The capacity to have rights and duties. Natural persons (human beings) and juridical/legal persons (companies, corporations, trusts, partnerships, municipalities) can both be legal persons. A company is a separate legal entity distinct from its members (Salomon v. Salomon & Co.). Capacity varies — minors, lunatics, corporations ultra vires — limitations apply. The Companies Act, 2013 grants companies perpetual succession and a common seal.

• Liability: Civil liability arises from breach of civil obligation (tort, contract); criminal liability from violation of penal law. Vicarious liability holds one person responsible for acts of another (e.g., employer-employee under Section 166 of the Motor Vehicles Act). Strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher) imposes liability without fault for dangerous things escaping.

4. Constitutional Jurisprudence — Preamble, Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

The Constitution of India is the supreme law of the land (Article 13 declares any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights void). Its jurisprudential architecture is central to the CS syllabus.

Preamble: Often called the 'identity card' of the Constitution. It declares India a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic securing Justice (social, economic, political), Liberty (thought, expression, belief, faith, worship), Equality (status and opportunity), and Fraternity. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Supreme Court held the Preamble is part of the Constitution and can be amended but its basic structure cannot be destroyed. Key cases: Berubari Union (1960) initially said Preamble not part of Constitution; Kesavananda reversed; Minerva Mills (1980) affirmed harmony between FRs and DPSPs as basic structure.

Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35): Justiciable, enforceable against the State. Key rights for Company Secretaries:
• Article 14 — Equality before law and equal protection of laws (introduced due process implicitly)
• Article 19 — Six freedoms (speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession)
• Article 21 — Protection of life and personal liberty (expanded to include right to privacy, clean environment, speedy trial, dignity)
• Article 300A — No person deprived of property except by authority of law (post-44th Amendment, no longer a FR but a constitutional right)
Limitations: Reasonable restrictions under Article 19; state of emergency (Article 352); Fundamental Rights suspended during Emergency except Articles 20 and 21. Writs under Article 32 (Supreme Court) and Article 226 (High Courts) — habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto.

Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36–51): Non-justiciable but fundamental in governance. Categories: Socialist (Articles 38, 39 — equal justice, free legal aid, adequate livelihood, equal pay, child protection, workers' participation); Gandhian (Articles 40–48 — panchayats, village panchayats, cottage industries, prohibition of intoxicating drinks, cow slaughter ban, promotion of cottage industries); Liberal-intellectual (Articles 44–49, 51 — uniform civil code, early childhood care, separation of judiciary from executive, promotion of international peace).

FR vs DPSP conflict: Golaknath (1967) gave primacy to FRs; Kesavananda (1973) held neither has priority; Minerva Mills (1980) reaffirmed harmony. Article 31C (added by 25th Amendment) gives precedence to DPSPs under Articles 39(b) and (c) over Articles 14 and 19.

5. Interpretation of Statutes — General Principles and Primary Rules

Statutory interpretation is the art of discovering and expounding the meaning of legislative provisions. For CS Executive, this section requires precise knowledge of rules, maxims and illustrative application.

Why interpretation is needed: Language is imprecise; words have multiple meanings; unforeseen situations arise; ambiguity in legislative drafting; conflicts between provisions; need to reconcile special and general laws.

Primary (Pure) Rules of Interpretation:

• Literal/Grammatical Rule: Give words their plain, ordinary, grammatical meaning even if it leads to some inconvenience. Applied when language is clear and unambiguous. Maxims: 'Expressum facit cessare tacitum' (express mention negates implied); 'Noscitur a sociis' (word knows its companions); 'Ejusdem generis' (general words follow the class of preceding specific words). Example: In statutory lists, 'cars, trucks and other vehicles' — 'other vehicles' restricted to motor vehicles of similar kind.

• Golden Rule: Modification of literal rule to avoid absurdity. Two applications: (i) Where literal meaning leads to absurdity, modify the meaning slightly; (ii) Where a word is ambiguous, choose the meaning that avoids absurdity. Example: A beneficiary under a will who murders the testator cannot inherit (reformed by law but illustrative of golden rule logic).

• Mischief Rule (Heydon's Case, 1584): Look to the mischief or defect the statute intended to remedy. Four questions: (i) What was the common law before the Act? (ii) What mischief did it fail to address? (iii) What remedy did Parliament intend? (iv) How should the remedy be construed? Most purposive in approach.

• Purposive/Modern Rule: Look beyond text to legislative purpose — widely used in contemporary jurisprudence, especially for social welfare legislation. Indian courts increasingly adopt this approach.

Subsidiary (Assistive) Rules:

• Rule of Harmonious Construction: When two provisions conflict, read them harmoniously so both survive; neither is rendered otiose.
• Rule of Harmonious Reading of Exceptions: Exception must be read narrowly; main provision broadly.
• Expressio unius est exclusio alterius: Expression of one thing is exclusion of others.
• Expressum facit cessare tacitum: Express mention excludes implied provisions.
• Noscitur a sociis and Ejusdem generis (already discussed above).
• Reddendo singula singulis: Distributive application — each word applies to its corresponding noun.
• Exclusion of Oral Evidence: When a statute requires writing, oral testimony cannot contradict it (Sections 91–92, Indian Evidence Act).

6. Interpretation of Statutes — Internal and External Aids

Aids to interpretation are tools used when the bare text is ambiguous or unclear. They are classified into internal (intrinsic) and external (extrinsic) aids.

Internal (Intrinsic) Aids — derived from within the statute itself:

• Title and Preamble: The short title gives identification; the preamble states objects, reasons and scope. The preamble may resolve ambiguity in operative provisions. In Re: the Delhi Land Rent Act (1954), the Preamble was used to resolve conflicting clauses.

• Long Title: States the purpose and scope comprehensively.

• Headings and Marginal Notes: Helpful for understanding the scheme but not conclusive (Section 29, General Clauses Act says marginal notes are not part of the enactment but may be consulted).

• Chapter Title: Gives thematic organisation.

• Explanation: Statutory explanations are part of the enactment and clarify ambiguities (Section 29, GCA).

• Illustrations: Explicitly printed examples aid interpretation.

• Saving Clauses: Preserve existing rights or pending proceedings.

• Provisos: Carve out exceptions to the general rule stated in the main provision.

• Definitions (Section 2 of statutes): Defined terms carry that meaning throughout unless context requires otherwise (Section 29, GCA).

• Schedules: Supplementary but integral parts; often contain detailed provisions, forms, tables.

External (Extrinsic) Aids — outside the four corners of the statute:

• Historical Background: Previous legal position, law commission reports, committee reports, white papers.

• Statement of Objects and Reasons: Laid in Parliament alongside the Bill; highly persuasive for ascertaining legislative intent.

• Parliamentary Debates: Generally excluded under traditional English rule but increasingly referenced by Indian courts (Rustom Cavas Jungi v. Country Bankers Association — reference to legislative history permitted in limited circumstances).

• Dictionary and Lexicographical Sources: Ordinary meaning verification.

• Legal Maxims: Doctrines that guide interpretation.

• Judicial Decisions: Prior interpretations of similar provisions.

• Other Statutes: In pari materia statutes (on same subject matter) are read together; consistent interpretation preferred.

• International Treaties and Conventions: Where domestic law incorporates treaty obligations, they guide interpretation (Vizag pattern).

• Subsequent Legislative History: Amendments, explanatory acts. Important: subsequent events cannot change original meaning but can confirm it.

7. General Clauses Act, 1897 — Definitions and Interpretation Rules

The General Clauses Act, 1897 is the interpretative framework for all Central legislation unless expressly excluded. Sections 3 to 5 and related provisions are essential for every legal practitioner.

Key definitions (Section 3):
• 'Document' includes any manuscript or print; 'writing' includes typing, printing, lithography.
• 'Person' includes any company or association — not merely natural persons.
• 'Male' includes female where context permits (Section 3(a)).
• 'Month' means calendar month unless context indicates otherwise.
• 'Year' means calendar year.
• 'Person acting in good faith' — nothing is done in bad faith which is done or intended to be done honestly.
• 'Office' means the place, not the person.

Punctuation, gender and number (Section 4):
Punctuation is indifferent — not decisive of meaning. Words importing the masculine gender include females (where context permits). Words in singular include plural and vice versa.

References to sections and provisions (Section 5):
A reference to a section is a reference to that section of the same Act unless otherwise specified. A reference to a provision includes a reference to sub-sections and clauses.

Service of notices (Section 27):
Notice sent by post is deemed served at the time of delivery to the post office.

Repealing and reenacting (Section 8):
Where an enactment is repealed and re-enacted, references to the repealed enactment apply to the new one. Saved transactions, rights, obligations and liabilities continue.

Delegated legislation (Section 5A):
Power to make rules, orders, notifications includes power to add, amend, vary or rescind.

Important judicial interpretations:
• Secretary, State of Karnataka v. Umadevi (2006): Clarified conditions for regularisation — no arbitrary employment; must meet eligibility criteria; not a right.
• Union of India v. Shrilaxmi (1997): Emphasised that 'shall' is usually mandatory but 'may' is discretionary, though context governs.
• K.T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. Government of Kerala (2011): Context is paramount; grammatical meaning yields to contextual impossibility.

8. Indian Penal Code — General Principles of Criminal Liability (Sections 6–120A)

The IPC, 1860 (now largely replaced by Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 effective July 1, 2024 — check latest ICSI notification for whether syllabus has been updated accordingly). For the CS exam, know both the classic IPC provisions and the transitional position under BNS where applicable.

Foundational Sections (IPC, as traditionally taught):
• Section 6 — Definitions: 'Man' includes female; 'life' means life of human being; 'dishonestly' means doing with intent to cause wrongful gain or wrongful loss (Section 24); 'wrongful gain' (Section 2(1)); 'wrongful loss' (Section 2(2)); 'property' (Section 2(3)).
• Section 8 — Offence committed during pregnancy: Child born alive though mother dead is a homicide offence.
• Section 9 — Offence committed by detached limb: Doing harm to a part of body is same as doing to whole body.
• Section 10 — 'Person': Includes company, association, body of individuals.
• Section 11 — 'He': Includes female as well as male.
• Section 12 — 'Interest': Any legal right or advantage.
• Section 23 — Dishonestly doing act causing wrongful gain/loss: Core of almost all IPC offences.
• Section 24 — Definition of 'dishonestly'.
• Section 25 — Dishonestly breaking open container: Aggravated form.
• Section 26 — Common intention (now Section 34 IPC / corresponding BNS provision): When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of common intention, each is liable for that act.
• Section 27 — Same offence done by several persons: Different intentional acts by different persons — each liable for own act.
• Section 29 — Knowledge: Mental element required; 'knows' includes belief.
• Sections 30–31 — Intention and intent distinguished: Intention = purpose; intent = aim.
• Sections 32–41 — 'Act' and 'Omission': Act includes lawful and unlawful; omission means neglect or refusal to act where there is a legal duty.
• Section 42 — 'Person specially employed': Duty imposed by contract.
• Section 43 — 'Omission': Neglect or refusal to act where duty exists.
• Section 44 — 'Damage': Includes injury, harm, prejudice.
• Section 45 — 'Good faith': Due care and attention; honest belief (Section 3, GCA).
• Section 51 — 'Insanity': Unsoundness of mind.
• Section 52 — 'Bona fide': Good faith.
• Section 53 — Punishments: Death, imprisonment, forfeiture of property, fine.
• Section 54 — 'Firm': As per Partnership Act, 1932.
• Section 56 — 'Death penalty': Now includes death by hanging per CrPC/BNS procedure.
• Section 57 — Fractions of punishments: Tables for computing proportional sentences.
• Section 58 — 'Female': Includes female human of any age.
• Section 59 — 'Life': Means life of human being unless contrary appearance.
• Section 64 — Proof of age: May be inferred from circumstances.
• Section 70 — Joint liability: Several persons jointly convicted.
• Section 71 — Limitation of punishment for several offences: General rule — sentence not more than double.
• Section 72 — Punishment of offender guilty of one of several offences: Court may impose punishment of any one offence proved.
• Section 73 — Distribution of punishment: Court may direct imprisonment to be served partly in one term and partly in another.
• Section 76 — Act done by person bound by mistake of fact: Mistake of fact defence (Section 79 IPC equivalent in BNS).
• Section 77 — Act of Judge: When Judge acting in good faith under bona fide belief of jurisdiction.
• Section 78 — Relief against unconstitutionality: If officer acts in reliance on law subsequently declared invalid.
• Section 79 — Act done by person justified or bound by law: Justified by law though not strictly according to law.
• Section 80 — Accident in doing lawful act: Lack of criminal intention or knowledge.
• Section 81 — Act likely to cause harm but done without criminal intent to prevent other harm: Necessity defence.
• Sections 82–84 — Age and mental capacity defences: Under 7 (absolute), 7–12 (conditional on maturity), sane/insane.
• Section 85 — Intoxication: Against will — no liability; voluntary — liability with knowledge.
• Section 86 — Offence requiring particular intent committed by intoxicated person: Different rules for knowledge vs. intention.
• Section 87 — Act not intended to cause death, done by consent: Consent defence (limited to hurt, not grievous hurt/death).
• Section 88 — Act done in good faith for benefit of person with consent: Medical/surgical procedures.
• Section 89 — Act done in good faith for benefit of child or insane person: Parent/guardian acts.
• Section 90 — Consent when known to be given under fear or misconception: Invalid consent.
• Section 91 — Acts excluded from Sections 88, 89: Harm prohibited by law cannot be excused by consent.
• Sections 93–96 — Private defence: Self-defence (Sections 96–106). Right to private defence of body and property against aggression.
• Section 97 — Extent of right: May defend body and property of self and others.
• Section 99 — Acts against which defence not available: Against public servants acting in good faith; death/grievous hurt unnecessary; force proportionate.
• Section 100 — Grounds for exercising right against body: Reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt.
• Section 101 — Exercises of right against property: Theft, robbery, mischief, house-trespass.
• Section 102–106 — Continuation and proportionality of right.
• Section 107 — Abetment: Instigation, conspiratorial abetment, intentional aid.
• Section 108 — Abettor: Person abetting commission of offence.
• Section 109 — Punishment of abetment when act abetted is done in absence of abettor: Same punishment as principal.
• Sections 110–120 — Conspiracy and criminal conspiracy: Section 120A defines conspiracy — agreement to commit illegal act or legal act by illegal means.

Note: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023 replaces IPC from 1 July 2024. Section numbers change but substantive principles largely continue. ICSI updates notifications — always verify.

9. Law of Contract — Essentials, Validity and Enforceability

The Indian Contract Act, 1872 governs commercial agreements. Section 2(h) defines a contract as 'an agreement enforceable by law'. The chain is: Offer → Acceptance → Promise → Consideration → Agreement → Enforceability → Contract.

Essentials (Section 10):
• Free consent (Sections 13–22): Consent is free when not caused by coercion (Section 15 — threatening unlawful act), undue influence (Section 16 — fiduciary relationship, dominating position), fraud (Section 17 — false representation, active concealment, promise without belief, any other deceitful act), misrepresentation (Section 18 — innocent false statement), or mistake (Section 20 — bilateral; Section 21 — unilaterial mistake of law not void; Section 22 — unilateral mistake of fact not void unless known to other party).
• Capacity to contract (Section 11): Major (18+ years), sound mind, not disqualified by any law. Minors' agreements void (Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose, 1903 — landmark: minor's agreement is void ab initio; minor cannot be estopped; ratification after majority impossible).
• Lawful consideration (Section 2(d)): Consideration must be lawful (Section 23) — not forbidden, not fraudulent, not immoral, not opposing public policy.
• Lawful object: Object must not be illegal.
• Not expressly declared void: Betting agreements (Section 27), restraint of marriage (Section 26), restraint of trade (Section 27 — with exceptions for sale of goodwill), wagering agreements (Section 30 — void, though related bets recoverable under certain conditions), agreements in restraint of legal proceedings (Section 28).

Classification of contracts:
• Valid, Void, Voidable, Illegal, Unenforceable.
• Quasi-contracts (Sections 68–72): Supply of necessities to incapacitated persons; finder of goods; money paid by mistake or under duress; non-gratuitous acts; price paid for non-delivery of goods.

Specific Contracts (Chapters 4–11):
• Indemnity and Guarantee (Sections 124–145): Indemnity — promise to save loss; guarantee — surety for debt of third party. Rights of surety: right of subrogation (Section 140), right of recourse (Section 141), right to compel payment (Section 142).
• Bailment (Sections 148–181): Delivery of goods on condition to return or dispose. Duties of bailor and bailee; right of lien.
• Pledge (Section 172): Bailment as security for payment. Rights of pawnee: lien, suit for debt, sale after notice.
• Agency (Sections 182–238): Creation by agreement, ratification, estoppel, necessity. Termination by act of parties, operation of law. Duties of agent and principal.

Remedies for Breach (Chapter 9, Sections 73–75):
• Compensatory damages — actual loss flowing naturally from breach or known to parties.
• Nominal damages — breach without proof of loss.
• Special damages — specifically proved loss.
• Contemplation test: Hadley v. Baxendale — losses must be reasonably foreseeable.
• Liquidated vs. penalty: Indian law enforces liquidated damages if reasonable (Section 74).
• Contribution and joint promisors (Sections 66–69): Among co-obligors.

10. Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Core Principles

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 regulates transfer of property in goods from seller to buyer for a price.

Key distinctions:
• Sale: Transfer of property immediately; executory.
• Agreement to sell: Future transfer; executory until conditions fulfilled.

Conditions and Warranties (Sections 12–14):
• Conditions — go to root of contract; breach entitles buyer to reject goods and claim damages. Types: Condition as to title (Section 14(a)), condition as to quality/fitness (Sections 14(b), 15), condition as to merchantable quality (Section 16).
• Warranties — collateral to conditions; breach gives claim for damages only.

Transfer of Property (Sections 18–23):
• Specific goods: Property passes when intended to pass. Rules in Section 22.
• Unascertained goods: Property passes when goods are unconditionally appropriated to the contract.
• Goods sent on approval/sale or return: Retention beyond time implies acceptance.

Rights of Unpaid Seller (Sections 45–54):
• Right of lien (Section 47): Retain goods until payment.
• Right of stoppage in transit (Section 43–52): Stop goods while in transit if buyer insolvent.
• Right of resale (Section 54): Resell after notice or if perishable.

Sale by Non-owner (Section 27) and mercantile agency (Section 38): Exceptions to nemo dat quod non habet (no one gives what he does not have) — sale by mercantile agent in possession, sale under voidable title, sale by seller/buyer in possession, sale by auction.

Consumer Protection Act, 2019: Consumer definition, complaint filing, product liability, e-commerce regulation, mediation, penal provisions — briefly note for cross-reference.

11. Negotiable Instruments, Limited Liability Partnerships and Other Commercial Laws

Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881:
• 'Negotiable instrument' (Section 13): Promissory note (Section 4), bill of exchange (Section 5), cheque (Section 6).
• Negotiability: Transfer by delivery (bearer instrument) or delivery + endorsement (order instrument). Holder in due course (Section 9) enjoys protected status — takes free from prior defects.
• Dishonour (Section 32): By non-acceptance or non-payment.
• Notice of dishonour (Section 93): Must be given within reasonable time.
• Crossed cheques (Sections 123–131): General crossing ('& Co.', 'Account Payee') — protects drawer and drawee; banker's cheque has special protections.
• Cheque (Section 6): Drawing order on banker payable on demand; dishonour on insufficient funds attracts liability.

Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008:
• Hybrid entity combining partnership flexibility with corporate limited liability.
• LLP agreement (Section 4): Governs mutual rights and obligations; default provisions apply in absence.
• Designated partners (Section 5): Minimum two, at least one resident Indian.
• Liability: Limited to agreed contribution; partners not personally liable for other partners' misconduct.
• Registration, charges, Annual Return and Financial Statement requirements.

Other Commercial Laws:
• Partnership Act, 1932: Registration optional but advantageous (Section 69 — unregistered firm cannot sue); mutual agency is core characteristic.
• Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996: Arbitration agreement (Section 7), arbitral tribunal composition (Section 10), challenge to appointment (Section 12), grounds for setting aside award (Section 34), enforcement (Section 36). 2015 and 2019 amendments significantly reformed the framework — focus on institutional arbitration, time limits, and reduced judicial intervention.
• Transfer of Property Act, 1882: Modes of transfer — sale, mortgage, lease, gift, exchange. Registration mandatory for documents creating interest in immovable property worth Rs.100 or more (Section 17, Registration Act, 1908). Section 41 (apparent owner), Section 43 (estoppel by deed), Section 53A (part performance doctrine), Section 108 (lessee's liabilities).
• Specific Relief Act, 1963: Specific performance (Sections 10–23), injunctions (Sections 38–42), rectification, cancellation, declaration. 2018 amendment expanded scope.
• Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999: Purpose-oriented regulation of forex transactions; replaced FEMA from 1999 onwards. FDI policy, current account vs capital account transactions.

12. Law of Evidence — Relevant Facts, Admissibility and Burden of Proof

The Indian Evidence Act, 1872 governs proof in Indian courts. It is procedural law, not substantive.

Relevancy of facts (Sections 5–30):
• Section 5: Facts constituting causation, occasion, opportunity, preparation, conduct (including subsequent conduct and statements forming part of same transaction — Section 6, Res gestae).
• Section 11: Facts negative of an alleged fact (proving negative).
• Section 14: Facts showing state of mind or body.
• Sections 15–16: Facts showing existence of relevant right or custom, or course of business.
• Section 18–19: Admissions — statements acknowledging a fact relevant to a proceeding.
• Section 21: Admissions not conclusive proof but may be adopted as evidence.
• Section 29: Confidential communications (spousal privilege under Section 122, 123).
• Section 32: Statements by deceased or unavailable persons (dying declaration — Section 32(1) — no oath requirement, corroboration ideally but not always necessary; statements in course of business — Section 32(2)).
• Section 35: Opinions of experts (Sections 45–51): Opinion of persons specially skilled in science, art, foreign law, handwriting, fingerprint expertise. Expert opinion is prima facie evidence.

Burden of proof (Sections 101–114A):
• Section 101: Whoever desires court to give judgment relies on a fact has burden to prove it.
• Section 102: Burden of proof lies on that party who would lose if no evidence were adduced.
• Section 104: Burden as to fact particularly within knowledge.
• Section 105: Burden of proving exception (e.g., insanity, self-defence) on accused — exception: Sections 305, 306 IPC (now BNS equivalents) where burden shifts to prosecution for certain offences.
• Section 114: Presumptions — court may presume existence of certain facts (e.g., electronic records authentic, common course of business regular, etc.). Illustrations (a)-(m).

Types of evidence:
• Oral evidence (Section 60): Must be direct — if based on hearsay, source must be produced.
• Documentary evidence (Sections 61–90): Primary evidence (original document — Section 62) vs. secondary evidence (Section 63 — certified copies, counterparts, oral accounts). Section 65 specifies when secondary evidence admissible.
• Electronic evidence (Sections 65A, 65B): Certificate of authenticity required for electronic records (Section 65B(4)) — critical for IT Act compliance.

Judicial notices (Section 56):
Court may take judicial notice of: laws in force; public notices; seal signatures; general customs; official gazette notifications. Cannot take judicial notice of private acts or foreign law without proof.

13. Limitation, Wills and Succession — Key Provisions

Limitation Act, 1963:
Purpose: Fixes time limits for instituting suits, appeals and applications; bars relief after prescribed period; promotes certainty and prevents stale claims.

• Section 3: Bar of limitation — suit instituted after period barred;
• Section 4: Expiry of period when court is closed or holiday falls — extends to next working day;
• Section 5: Condonation of delay — sufficient cause may be allowed;
• Section 9: Right to property not barred by expiry of limitation period (only remedy barred);
• Section 14: Dilatory relief — where plaintiff prosecuted in court with due diligence but could not get matter heard due to defect; period of such prosecution excluded;
• Section 15: Deduction in case of decrees, appeals, etc.;
• Section 18: Acknowledgement of debt extends limitation from date of acknowledgement;
• Section 19: Payment on account of principal or interest extends limitation;
• Section 20: Fresh start on part payment;
• Section 23: Suit for compensation for tort — period runs from date of wrong;
• Schedule: First Schedule gives periods for various suits — 3 years for contract, 6 years for decree, 12 years for possession, 30/35/40 years for redemption, 3 years for defamation, 3 years for specific performance, etc.
• Section 27: Extinguishment of right after limitation period.

Wills and Succession:
• Indian Succession Act, 1925: Governs testamentary and intestate succession for Christians, Parsis and those not covered by personal laws. Hindus governed by Hindu Succession Act, 1956 (amended 2005).
• Will (Section 2(h), Succession Act): lawful act by which testator disposing of property takes effect on death.
• Execution (Section 63): Testator signs or marks; two witnesses attest; each witness should see testator sign or acknowledge signature; attestation unnecessary for validity but evidentiary requirement.
• Revocation (Section 70): By obliteration, alteration, subsequent will or destruction.
• revocability: Will is ambulatory and revocable during lifetime.
• Section 119: Will of married person not revoked by marriage (post-2002 amendment).
• Section 122: Will cannot take effect during testator's lifetime.
• Intestate succession: Distribution as per applicable personal law — Hindu Succession Act, 1956 provides Class I and Class II heirs; Muslim personal law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937; Indian Succession Act, 1925 for Christians/Parsis.
• Coparcenary property: 2005 Amendment to HSA gave daughters equal coparcenary rights (Section 6, HSA).

14. Administrative Law, Ombudsman and Tribunal System

Administrative law governs the organisation, powers, procedures and accountability of administrative agencies.

Sources and principles:
• Rule of Law (Dicey): No man is punishable except for breach of law; equals before law; rights determined by courts not statute. Indian adaptation: Maneka Gandhi v. UOI (1978) introduced 'due process' flavour into Article 21.
• Natural Justice: Audi alteram partem (hear the other side) — right to notice, right to present case, right to cross-examine, right to reasoned order. Nemo judex in causa sua (no one should be judge in own cause) — bias disqualifies. Exceptions: Exigency, emergency, statutory exclusion, academic evaluation limited review.
• Wednesbury unreasonableness: Decision so unreasonable that no reasonable authority would make it (Associated Provincial Picture Houses v. Wednesbury Corporation, 1948).
• Proportionality: Measure must be appropriate and necessary to achieve legitimate aim; not excessive relative to objective. Adopted by Indian courts in administrative review.
• Legitimate expectation: Arises when authority makes promise or follows consistent practice; protects reliance interest; procedural protection (hearing) generally required before frustrating expectation.

Tribunals:
• Administrative Tribunals established under Article 323A (1985) for service matters; Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT), State Administrative Tribunals.
• Tribunals under Article 323B for other matters: Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, GST Appellate Tribunal, National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) under Companies Act, 2013, National green Tribunal (NGT) under NGT Act, 2010, Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions, Labour Courts/Tribunals.
• L.S. Ramaprasad v. Andhra Pradesh (1995): Tribunals subject to constitutional validity but within constitutional scheme; judicial review by High Courts under Article 226 and Supreme Court under Article 32 remains.
• Madras Bar Association v. UOI (2014): Struck down provisions giving executive dominance in tribunal appointments; led to Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 unifying appointment and terms.

Ombudsman:
• Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013: Lokpal at Centre, Lokayuktas at State level. Investigates allegations of corruption against public functionaries including PM (with safeguards), Ministers, MPs, Group A/B/C officers.
• Lokayuktas Act varies by state — Gujarat has its own Lokayukta Act, 2011.
• Parliamentary Ombudsman concept originated in Sweden; now in UK, many European countries, India.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL):
• Epitomised by PIL — relaxation of locus standi; judiciary as activist protector of rights. Pioneer:Justice P.N. Bhagwati, Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer. Cases: Hussainara Khatoon (right to speedy trial), Bandhua Mukti Morcha (bonded labour), Vishaka (sexual harassment at workplace). Recent trend: curbing frivolous PILs (Suresh Kumar Koulal v. State of Kerala, 2021).

15. Civil and Criminal Procedure — Jurisdiction, Pleadings and Trial Essentials

CPR provides the machinery for enforcing rights. Company Secretaries must understand jurisdictional basics and procedural flow.

Civil Procedure — Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC):
• Section 3: Definition of Court — includes Judge and Court; any officer empowered to act judicially.
• Section 9: Civil courts have jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature except those expressly or impliedly barred.
• Section 15–21: Pecuniary and territorial jurisdiction. Principal District Court at each district level; subordinate courts below.
• Section 22: Removal of suits — court may transfer when proper venue exists elsewhere.
• Section 23: Frame of suit — plaint must contain material facts, not evidence.
• Order VII: Plaint — contents, rejection for non-compliance (Order VII Rule 11: preliminary dismissal for jurisdiction, res judicata, absence of cause of action).
• Order VIII: Written statement — Defence, set-off, counter-claim.
• Order IX: Ex parte proceedings — default by plaintiff (dismissal) or defendant (decree ex parte).
• Order X: Examination of parties.
• Order XI: Discovery and inspection.
• Order XVI: Summoning and attendance of witnesses.
• Order XVII: Adjournments.
• Order XVIII: Hearings and arguments.
• Order XX: Judgment and decree.
• Order XXI: Execution of decrees and orders.
• Section 10: Res sub judice — suit not to be tried when matter directly and substantially in issue is also pending in previously instituted suit between same parties.
• Section 11: Res judicata — matter directly and substantially in issue shall not be re-agitated; final decision on merits bars subsequent suit. Exceptions: Fraudulent decree, jurisdictional error.
• Section 14: Time spent prosecuting another suit in bad faith excluded.
• Order XLIII: Appeals from orders.
• Order XLIV: Appeal from indigent person (appeal in forma pauperis).
• Section 151: Inherent powers — retained by court to secure ends of justice or prevent abuse of process. Cannot override express provisions.

Criminal Procedure — Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC) / Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), 2023:
• cognizable vs. non-cognizable offences (Section 2(c), (l) CrPC).
• FIR, investigation, charge sheet process.
• Warrants (Section 70), summons (Section 74), bailable vs. non-bailable (Section 2(a)).
• Compounding of offences (Section 320).
• Plea bargaining (Sections 265A–265L).
• Anti-social Activities (Prevention) Act provisions where relevant.
• Bail provisions — Section 436 (bailable), 437 (non-bailable by Magistrate), 438 (anticipatory bail).

Note: BNSS 2023 replaces CrPC from 1 July 2024. BNS (criminal substantive law), BNSS (procedure), BSA (evidence) collectively replace IPC, CrPC, IEA respectively. Always check ICSI circulars for syllabus transition status.

Important facts

  • Jurisprudence studies the philosophy and theory of law; major schools include Natural Law, Analytical (Positivist), Historical, Sociological and Realist.
  • Natural Law: law flows from reason/morality/divine will; a law contrary to natural justice is not true law (Aquinas, Cicero).
  • Legal Positivism (Austin): law is command of sovereign backed by sanction; separates law as it is from law as it ought to be.
  • Historical School (Savigny): law grows organically from Volkgeist (spirit of the people); like language, evolves naturally.
  • Sociological School (Pound): law is social engineering; must balance competing individual, public and social interests.
  • Realism (Holmes): law is what courts do in fact; experience matters more than formal logic.
  • Preamble to Indian Constitution is part of Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973) but cannot be amended to destroy basic structure.
  • Fundamental Rights (Part III) are justiciable; Directive Principles (Part IV) are non-justiciable but fundamental in governance.
  • Article 14 guarantees equality before law; Article 19 guarantees six freedoms subject to reasonable restrictions; Article 21 guarantees life and personal liberty.
  • Section 2(h), Indian Contract Act: contract is an agreement enforceable by law — the five pillars: offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, lawful object.
  • Minor's agreement is void ab initio (Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose, 1903) — minority cannot be ratified.
  • Doctrine of part performance (Section 53A, TPA): protects buyer in possession despite unregistered sale deed.
  • Nemo dat quod non habet: no one can transfer better title than they possess; exceptions include mercantile agent (Section 38, SOGA), apparent owner (Section 27, TPA).
  • Holder in due course under NI Act enjoys protected status — takes freely from prior defects in title.
  • LLP: hybrid entity with limited liability; designated partners; annual return and financial statement mandatory.
  • Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996: 2015 and 2019 amendments introduced institutional arbitration, strict timelines, reduced judicial intervention.
  • General Clauses Act, 1897: Section 3 definitions apply unless context otherwise requires; applies to all Central legislation.
  • Sections 24–25 IPC define dishonesty, wrongful gain, wrongful loss — foundation of almost all IPC offences.
  • Section 34 IPC (common intention) differs from Section 35 (same offence by several persons) — one requires shared intention, other requires individual intentional acts towards common goal.
  • Res judicata (Section 11 CPC) and res sub judice (Section 10 CPC) prevent re-litigation and multiplicity of suits.
  • Section 151 CPC: inherent powers of court to secure ends of justice — cannot be invoked where express provision exists.
  • Natural justice: audi alteram partem (hear other side) and nemo judex in causa sua (no one should be judge in own cause).
  • Wednesbury unreasonableness: administrative decision so unreasonable no reasonable authority would make it.
  • Proportionality: state action must be suitable, necessary and not excessive relative to objective achieved.
  • PIL: relaxation of locus standi pioneered by justices Bhagwati and Krishna Iyer; Vishaka guidelines arose from PIL.
  • Limitation Act, 1963: Section 5 allows condonation of delay on sufficient cause; Section 18 extends limitation on acknowledgment; Schedule first gives limitation periods.
  • Indian Succession Act, 1925 governs wills for non-Hindus, non-Muslims; HSA, 1956 governs Hindus; Muslim personal law governed by Shariat Application Act, 1937.
  • BNS, BNSS, BSA replaced IPC, CrPC, IEA respectively effective 1 July 2024; ICSI notification determines examination applicability.
  • Evidence: dying declaration (Section 32(1)) is an exception to hearsay; Section 65B certificate mandatory for electronic evidence.
  • Ombudsman institution: Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 establishes Lokpal at Centre and Lokayuktas at State level.
