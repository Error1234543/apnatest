1. Introduction and Evolution of Companies Law in India

The Indian company law framework has undergone significant transformation over the past two centuries. The first legislation was the Joint Stock Companies Act of 1850, which was primarily aimed at regulating joint stock companies but did not provide for incorporation. The Companies Act of 1860 introduced the concept of limited liability and registration, followed by the Companies Act of 1882 which consolidated several earlier enactments. The Companies Act of 1913 was a comprehensive piece of legislation that served as the basis for subsequent reforms. The current Companies Act, 2013 replaced the Companies Act, 1956 after nearly five decades, reflecting changes in the economic landscape, globalisation, and the need for stronger corporate governance.

The Companies Act, 2013 received the assent of the President on 29 August 2013, but most of its provisions came into force gradually. Section 469 of the Act provides for its repeal of the 1956 Act. The 2013 Act drew heavily from the Jenkins Committee recommendations (1952), the Ayyangar-Modi Committee report (1961), and the Nanavati Committee report (1978), along with international best practices and the needs of a developing economy. It is significantly more detailed than the 1956 Act, incorporating stricter compliance requirements, enhanced corporate governance norms, and broader provisions for shareholder protection.

2. Incorporation of Company and Its Effects

Incorporation refers to the process by which a company is legally formed and registered under the Companies Act, 2013. The primary procedure involves promotion, naming, subscription, registration, and obtaining the Certificate of Incorporation.

Promotion: A promoter is a person who conceives the idea of establishing a company and takes the necessary steps to bring it into existence. Promoters owe fiduciary duties to the company and must disclose any personal interests or secret profits. They must not make undisclosed profits from their position.

Naming: Under Section 4, a company may be incorporated with any name provided it is not identical or deceptfully similar to an existing company or registered trademark. The name must end with 'Private Limited' or 'Limited' as appropriate, and cannot be offensive or prohibited without prior approval from the Central Government under Section 4(2).

Subscription: For a public company, at least seven persons subscribe to the memorandum; for a private company, at least two persons. Each subscriber must add their name, address, description, and the number of shares taken.

Registration: Under Sections 7 and 8, the promoters file the incorporation documents including the Memorandum of Association (MOA), Articles of Association (AOA), consent of proposed directors, registered office details, and a declaration of compliance with the Act in Form INC-8. The Registrar of Companies (ROC) examines the documents and, if satisfied, registers the company and issues the Certificate of Incorporation under Section 7(1).

Certificate of Incorporation: This is conclusive evidence that all requirements of the Act have been complied with. Under Section 11, the ROC may initiate proceedings for penalty if incorrect information was provided.

Effects of Incorporation: Upon registration, the company becomes a separate legal entity distinct from its members (Lee v Lee's Air Farming Ltd principle). It can own property, sue and be sued in its own name, has perpetual succession, and its shares are transferable. The doctrine of lifting the veil under Section 447 (fraud) and other provisions may pierce this separateness in specific circumstances, such as reducing membership below the minimum or fraudulent activities.

3. Memorandum of Association and Articles of Association

The Memorandum of Association (MOA) under Section 2(56) and 4 defines the company's constitution and scope of operations. It contains six clauses: (1) Name Clause — the registered name of the company; (2) Registered Office Clause — the State in which the registered office is situated; (3) Objects Clause — the main objects and objects ancillary or incidental to the attainment of main objects. Since the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2015, the ultra vires doctrine has been substantially modified — acts beyond the MOA are no longer void ab initio as against third parties, though directors may be liable internally; (4) Liability Clause — the extent of liability of members, whether limited by shares or by guarantee; (5) Capital Clause — the authorised share capital and division into nominal values of shares; (6) Subscription Clause — the declaration of subscribers and their share subscriptions.

Under Section 6, a company may alter its MOA by special resolution, subject to confirmation by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) in certain cases. Changes include alteration of name, registered office within a city/town/village, objects, authorised capital, and sub-division or consolidation of shares.

The Articles of Association (AOA) under Section 2(5) and 5 contain the rules and regulations for the internal management of the company. It governs the relationship between the company and its members, and among members themselves. For a company limited by shares, the provisions of Table F in Schedule I of the Act apply unless excluded or modified by the AOA. The AOA is subordinate to the MOA — any provision inconsistent with the MOA is void.

Alteration of AOA under Section 14 requires a special resolution. However, alterations must not be unlawful, beyond the powers conferred by the Act, or oppressive to any class of members. Under Section 5(2), if a company does not have its own AOA or has not fully adopted one, the relevant Tables in Schedule I apply by default.

4. Prospectus, Allotment and Offer to Public

A prospectus under Section 2(70) is any document inviting offers from the public for subscription or purchase of securities of a body corporate. The definition is broad and includes reference to a red herring prospectus (Section 31), shelf prospectus (Section 32), and offer letter (Section 41).

Prospectus Requirements: Under Section 29, every prospectus must state the matters and contain the explanations specified in Schedule XIII. Section 30 requires that every prospectus be dated and that liability for misstatement lies with every person who authorised the issue. Section 31 deals with the red herring prospectus — a prospectus that does not state the capital structure and details of the offer, which must be filed subsequently. Section 32 allows listed companies to use a shelf prospectus for issues spread over time.

Allotment: Section 39 requires that no allotment of securities shall be made unless a minimum subscription of 5% of the issued capital has been received. If the minimum subscription is not received, all money received must be refunded within 15 days. If not refunded, promoters become jointly and severally liable to pay interest at 15% per annum from the expiry of the 15th day. Section 40 requires a statement in lieu of prospectus to be filed by a public company before any allotment.

Offer to Public: Section 42 restricts private placements. No company shall make any offer to the public for subscription or purchase of its securities except through a prospectus or in compliance with Section 42. Section 42(2) specifies conditions for private placement — the offer must be made to a select group of persons through a letter of offer, and the maximum number of invitees in a financial year cannot exceed 200, excluding employees, ex-employees, and existing members.

Underwriting: Section 33 permits a company to pay remuneration or commission to any person for subscribing or agreeing to subscribe for securities, provided it is disclosed in the prospectus. Section 35 provides liability for untrue or misleading statements in the prospectus — civil liability on the part of the company, promoters, directors, and experts who authorised the prospectus.

5. Members and Share Capital

Membership: Under Section 3, every company must have members. A person whose name is entered in the register of members is a member. The register of members must be kept at the registered office and must contain the names, addresses, occupations, and the shares held by each member. Under Section 88, the company must maintain a register of members and update it promptly. Sections 45-49 deal with transfer and transmission of shares. Transfer requires a proper instrument of transfer unless shares are dematerialised. Transmission occurs by operation of law (e.g., death or insolvency of a member).

Share Capital: Section 2(58) defines share capital. A company limited by shares must have an authorised share capital stated in its MOA. Types of share capital include equity shares (with voting rights) and preference shares (with preferential rights to dividend and repayment of capital). Section 43 lists the types of preference shares — cumulative/non-cumulative, redeemable/irredeemable, convertible/non-convertible.

Redemption of Preference Shares: Section 55 provides for redemption of preference shares. A company limited by shares may issue redeemable preference shares if authorised by its MOA. Redeemable preference shares can only be redeemed out of: (a) the proceeds of a fresh issue made for the purposes of redemption; (b) the profits which would otherwise be available for dividend; or (c) the proceeds of a fresh issue made for the purposes of redemption, together with one or both of the others. Before redeeming, a premium if any must be provided for out of the profits of the company or out of the company's securities premium account. A company must create a Capital Redemption Reserve Account equal to the nominal amount of shares redeemed out of profits.

Alteration of Share Capital: Under Section 61, a company having a share capital may alter its MOA to: increase authorised capital by fresh capital; sub-divide or consolidate shares; cancel shares which have not been taken or agreed to be taken; reconvert debentures into shares; reduce share capital (subject to Sections 66-68).

Share Premium Account: Under Section 52, the premium received on issue of shares must be credited to the Securities Premium Account and may be used for writing off preliminary expenses, expenses of issue, or discount on issue of shares/debentures, or for providing for premium payable on redemption of debentures/preference shares.

6. Directors and Key Managerial Personnel

Directors: Section 2(34) defines a director as a director appointed to the board of a company. Under Section 149, every company must have a minimum number of directors — a public company requires at least three, a private company at least two, and a One Person Company at least one. A woman director is mandatory for every listed company and other prescribed classes of companies.

Appointment and Qualification: Directors may be appointed as alternate, additional, independent, or nominated directors. Section 152 deals with qualifications, and the requirement of a Director Identification Number (DIN) under Section 153 is mandatory. A person cannot be appointed as a director if they are of unsound mind, undischarged insolvent, convicted of certain offences, or disqualified under Sections 164 and 165. Section 164 sets disqualifications including non-filing of financial statements for three consecutive years, failure to pay calls, and conviction of offences.

Duties and Liabilities: Under Section 166, directors must act in good faith, exercise due and reasonable care, avoid conflict of interest, and not achieve undue gain. Section 167 provides for vacation of office, and Section 168 allows resignation. Related party transactions are regulated under Section 188. Section 184 requires directors to disclose their interests in contracts or arrangements.

Independent Directors: Chapter X of the Act deals with independent directors. Under Section 149(6), listed companies and other prescribed companies must have at least one-third of total directors as independent directors. Section 150 lays down qualifications and criteria, and Section 151 requires independence as defined therein.

Key Managerial Personnel (KMP): Under Section 2(51), KMP includes the Whole-Time Director/Manager, Company Secretary, and Chief Financial Officer. Every listed company and other prescribed companies must appoint a Company Secretary as KMP. Section 203 requires appointment of KMP within six months of commencing business or incorporation.

Company officer: Under Section 2(60), an officer who is in default is one who is knowingly responsible for an act, omission, or failure, or where the act or omission is attributable to his guidance or control.

7. Meetings of Board and Committees

Board Meetings: Section 173 governs board meetings. The first board meeting must be held within 30 days of incorporation. Thereafter, at least four board meetings must be held in each financial year with a maximum gap of 120 days between two meetings. Section 174 deals with notice of board meetings — at least seven days' written notice is required. Section 175 requires quorum — one-third of total strength or two directors, whichever is higher. Section 176 prohibits a director from being counted in quorum for discussions concerning his own appointment. Section 177 requires every company to have an Audit Committee.

Audit Committee: Under Section 177, every listed company and other prescribed companies must constitute an Audit Committee. The committee must have at least three directors, a majority being independent directors, and the chairperson must be an independent director. Functions include oversight of financial reporting, internal controls, and statutory audit.

Nomination and Remuneration Committee: Under Section 178, listed companies and other prescribed companies must constitute a Nomination and Remuneration Committee consisting of at least three independent directors to formulate criteria for appointment of directors and KMP and to determine remuneration.

Women Director: Section 178 also mandates the appointment of at least one woman director in certain categories of companies.

Other committees: Listed companies must also have a Stakeholders Relationship Committee, Risk Management Committee, and a committee for related party transactions. The composition and functions of these committees are specified in the Act and the SEBI LODR Regulations for listed entities.

8. General Meetings and Resolutions

General Meetings: Section 92 requires every company to file an annual return with the ROC. Under Section 96, every company must hold an Annual General Meeting (AGM) each year. The first AGM must be held within 9 months from the closure of the first financial year, and subsequent AGMs within 6 months from the date of closure of the financial year. The maximum gap between two AGMs cannot exceed 15 months. Section 100 specifies who can call general meetings — the board, members holding 10% of voting power, or the NCLT.

Notice: Under Section 100 and rules, 21 days' clear notice is required for general meetings. Additional requirements exist for calling extraordinary general meetings.

Quorum: Section 103 specifies quorum — for a public company, 5 members personally present if more than 1000 members; 2 members if up to 1000 members. For a private company, 2 members personally present. For a One Person Company, 1 member.

Resolution: Section 2(74) defines resolution. Ordinary resolutions require a simple majority of votes. Special resolutions under Section 106 require a majority of not less than 75% of votes. Section 108 provides for electronic voting through remote e-voting or voting at the meeting.

Class Rights: Section 48 provides that variations in class rights require consent of holders of three-fourths of the issued shares of that class.

Records of proceedings: Section 118 requires the company to keep minutes of proceedings of every general meeting and board meeting in bound books with particulars of matters dealt with.

9. Accounts, Audit and Financial Reporting

Accounts of Company: Section 128 requires every company to prepare and maintain proper books of account and other relevant books and papers in accordance with Section 128 and the Companies (Accounting Standards) Rules, 2006. Proper books of account must contain records of sums of money received and expended, assets and liabilities, and parts dealing with sales and purchases.

Financial Statements: Section 129 requires every company to prepare an annual financial statement including a balance sheet, profit and loss account, cash flow statement, and notes. For holding and subsidiary companies, consolidated financial statements must also be prepared. The financial statements must be approved and signed by the Chairperson of the Board, Managing Director, CEO, CFO, and Company Secretary.

Board's Report: Under Section 134, the Board's Report must be attached with the financial statements. It must include: (a) a true and fair view of company affairs; (b) matters specified under sub-sections (3) to (8); (c) a statement on development of children's education; (d) a report on environmental conservation. The report must be laid before the AGM.

Audit: Section 139 mandates the appointment of auditors. Every listed company and other prescribed companies must have a Chartered Accountant as a statutory auditor. The term of office of an auditor is five years, and they can be reappointed. Section 140 provides for removal, resignation, and filling of casual vacancies. Section 143 lays down the powers and duties of auditors, including the duty to report fraud under sub-section (12).

Secretarial Audit: Section 204 requires every listed company and other prescribed companies to conduct a secretarial audit. The secretarial auditor must report in Form MR-3 and make additional reports if there are any qualifications or observations.

10. Corporate Governance and Disclosure Requirements

Corporate governance encompasses the system by which companies are directed and controlled. The Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI LODR Regulations prescribe a comprehensive framework.

Related Party Transactions: Section 188 regulates transactions with related parties. Any contract or arrangement between a company and its related parties requires prior approval of the board or shareholders, as applicable. Material related party transactions above thresholds specified in the Act require shareholder approval. Section 189 requires disclosure of contracts or arrangements with interested parties.

Disclosure Requirements: Section 137 requires every company to file its financial statements and board's report with the ROC within 30 days of the AGM (or within the extended period). Section 138 requires the company to file the auditor's report with the ROC.

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR): Under Section 135, every company satisfying the thresholds (net worth of ₹500 crore or more, turnover of ₹1,000 crore or more, or net profit of ₹5 crore or more during the immediately preceding financial year) must constitute a CSR Committee and spend at least 2% of the average net profits of the immediately preceding three years on CSR activities. Section 135 specifies the contents of the CSR policy and reporting requirements under Section 134(5)(g).

Whistleblower: Under Section 177(9), every company must establish a mechanism for employees to report concerns regarding unethical conduct, actual or suspected fraud, or violation of the company's code of conduct. This is to be included in the Audit Committee's terms of reference.

11. Loans, Investments and Guarantees

Loans and Investments: Section 180 restricts the power of a company to: (a) sell, lease, or dispose of the whole or substantially the whole of its undertaking; (b) invest the funds otherwise than in specified investments; and (c) borrow money beyond the limits, unless approved by a special resolution. These restrictions apply to public companies and prescribed private companies.

Loans to Directors: Section 185 prohibits a company from giving any loan to a director or director of its holding company or to any body corporate in which a director is a partner, director, or member, except in the ordinary course of business and on arm's length terms. This prohibition also extends to persons connected with a director.

Inter-company Loans: Section 186 allows a company to lend money and provide guarantees or security in connection with loans to other persons, subject to prescribed limits. The total of such loans, investments, and guarantees cannot exceed 60% of the aggregate of the paid-up capital and free reserves or 100% of the free reserves and securities premium, whichever is more. Prior board or member approval is required as per prescribed limits.

Substantial Interest and Disclosure: Section 188 governs related party transactions requiring disclosure and approval. Section 203 requires KMP to disclose any direct or indirect interest in any body corporate.

12. Company Secretarial Practice and Compliance Procedures

Company secretarial practice involves ensuring that a company complies with all statutory and regulatory requirements. A practicing company secretary plays a critical role in governance and compliance.

Registrars of Companies (ROC): Under Sections 387-391, ROCs are established for different states. Every company must file various forms with the ROC, including incorporation documents, annual returns, financial statements, charges, changes in directors, and other statutory filings.

Annual Return: Section 92 requires every company to prepare an annual return in Form MGT-7, containing details of members, directors, KMP, shareholding, charges, and other prescribed particulars. The annual return must be completed within 60 days from the date of the AGM.

Registers and Records: Section 112 requires the company to maintain registers including the register of members, register of directors and KMP, register of contracts, register of investments, and other registers as prescribed. These must be kept at the registered office and made available for inspection.

Charges: Sections 77-78 require registration of charges. Every charge created by a company must be registered with the ROC within 30 days of creation. Defaulters face penalties.

Inspection: Section 119 allows inspection of registers and documents by members and others, subject to fees.

Practicing Company Secretary: Under Section 24 and the CS Act, 1985, company secretaries in practice are entitled to perform various secretarial services including secretarial audits, certification of compliance, and other statutory advisory work.

13. Winding Up of Companies

Winding up or liquidation is the process of bringing the affairs of a company to a close. The Companies Act, 2013 and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) provide the framework.

Modes of Winding Up: The Tribunal may order winding up under Section 270 when the company is unable to pay debts, passes a special resolution for voluntary winding up, or for just and equitable reasons.

Voluntary Winding Up: Under Sections 271-285, a company may be wound up voluntarily when it passes a resolution for voluntary winding up. If the company can pay its debts in full within a specified period not exceeding three years, it is a members' voluntary winding up; otherwise, it is a creditors' voluntary winding up.

Liquidator: Under Section 277, in a voluntary winding up, the General Meeting may appoint a liquidator. In a winding up by the Tribunal, the NCLT appoints a liquidator. The liquidator takes custody of company assets, Realises assets, pays liabilities, and distributes surplus to members.

Consequences of Winding Up: Under Section 280, upon an order for winding up, all proceedings against the company are stayed. Under Section 281, the property of the company devolves on the liquidator.

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code: The IBC provides an alternative framework for insolvency resolution and liquidation. It introduces the concept of resolution plans and prioritises secured creditors. The NCLT adjudicates applications under the IBC.

Preliminary and Final Report: The liquidator must submit a preliminary report under Section 285 and a final report under Section 286 before applying to the Tribunal for dissolution.

14. Penalty, Offences and Adjudication

The Companies Act, 2013 categorises offences and penalties into compounding, adjudication, and prosecution.

Compounding of Offences: Under Sections 441-448, certain offences can be compounded by the Regional Director, the Appellate Tribunal, or the NCLT. Compounding is an alternative to prosecution and involves payment of a compounding fee. An application for compounding can be made either before or after the institution of prosecution.

Adjudication: Under Section 447, fraud is a cognisable offence punishable with imprisonment and fine. For other offences, adjudication officers can impose penalties under Section 451. Penalties range from small fines to substantial amounts depending on the nature and gravity of the offence.

Penalties on Companies and Officers: Under Section 450, where a company commits an offence, every officer who is in default is also liable. The penalty structure distinguishes between first offence and repeat offences.

Fraud: Section 447 defines fraud as any act, omission, concealment of any fact, or abuse of position committed with intent to deceive or to gain undue advantage. Punishment for fraud includes imprisonment of not less than six months extending to ten years and a fine of not less than the amount involved in the fraud extending to three times that amount.

Prosecution Procedure: Under Section 454, when a company is convicted of an offence, the court may also prosecute officers in default. Provisions regarding limitation for taking cognisance are contained in Section 455.

Important facts

  • The Companies Act, 2013 received Presidential assent on 29 August 2013, replacing the Companies Act, 1956.
  • Minimum directors: public company—3, private company—2, OPC—1. A woman director is mandatory for listed companies and other prescribed classes.
  • Memorandum of Association (MOA) under Section 4 contains six clauses: name, registered office, objects, liability, capital, and subscription. Alteration requires confirmation by NCLT in most cases.
  • Articles of Association (AOA) under Section 5 govern internal management and are subordinate to the MOA. Tables in Schedule I apply if no AOA exists.
  • Certificate of Incorporation under Section 7 is conclusive evidence that all incorporation requirements have been complied with.
  • Ultra vires doctrine: Post-2015 amendment, acts beyond MOA are no longer void against third parties but directors may face internal liability.
  • Minimum subscription: 5% of issued share capital must be received before allotment can proceed (Section 39).
  • Private placement under Section 42 limits invitations to 200 persons per financial year, excluding employees and existing members.
  • Share capital: authorised capital is the maximum a company can issue. Redeemable preference shares can only be redeemed out of fresh issue proceeds or distributable profits (Section 55).
  • Capital Redemption Reserve Account must be created equal to the nominal amount of redeemed preference shares if redeemed out of profits.
  • Board meetings: at least 4 per year with maximum 120 days gap. Notice of 7 days is mandatory (Section 173).
  • AGM: first AGM within 9 months of financial year-end; subsequent AGMs within 6 months. Maximum gap between AGMs is 15 months (Section 96).
  • Special resolution requires 75% majority of votes cast (Section 106). Ordinary resolution requires simple majority.
  • Independent directors: listed companies must have at least 1/3 of total directors as independent directors (Section 149(6)).
  • Key Managerial Personnel (KMP) under Section 2(51) includes MD/CEO, CS, and CFO. CS is mandatory for listed companies (Section 203).
  • Secretarial audit under Section 204 is mandatory for listed companies and other prescribed companies.
  • CSR mandate under Section 135: companies meeting threshold (net worth ₹500cr / turnover ₹1,000cr / net profit ₹5cr) must spend 2% of average net profits of preceding three years.
  • Related party transactions under Section 188 require board/shareholder approval depending on materiality thresholds.
  • Section 186 limits inter-corporate loans and investments to 60% of paid-up capital + free reserves or 100% of free reserves + securities premium.
  • Fraud under Section 447 is punishable with imprisonment (6 months to 10 years) and fine (amount involved to 3× that amount). It is a cognisable offence.
  • Winding up: voluntary (members' or creditors') or by Tribunal. Liquidator takes control of assets, settles claims, and distributes surplus.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the MOA (external/constitutional document) with the AOA (internal management document). The MOA defines the company's powers; the AOA defines its internal rules.
  • Forgetting that the ultra vires doctrine was modified by the 2015 amendment — acts beyond the MOA are no longer automatically void against third parties.
  • Assuming that a special resolution requires 2/3 majority — it actually requires 75% of votes cast (Section 106).
  • Not knowing that the first AGM must be held within 9 months (not 6 months) while subsequent AGMs are within 6 months from financial year-end.
  • Confusing 'minimum subscription' (5% of issued capital) with 'minimum share application money' (5% of nominal value of shares mentioned in prospectus).
  • Overlooking that redeemable preference shares can ONLY be redeemed out of profits or fresh issue — not out of capital (Section 55).
  • Forgetting that a Capital Redemption Reserve Account must be created when redeeming preference shares out of profits.
  • Not distinguishing between compounding (administrative settlement of offence) and adjudication (penalty determination by officer).
  • Assuming Section 185 prohibition applies universally — it specifically targets loans to directors and their related parties, not all loans.
  • Missing that CSR threshold is based on any ONE of the three criteria (net worth, turnover, or net profit), not all three.
  • Confusing the quorum for board meetings (1/3 of directors or 2, whichever is higher) with the quorum for general meetings (5 or 2 members for public company).
  • Not realising that a company can alter its MOA but only with NCLT confirmation in most cases, whereas AOA alteration by special resolution alone is sufficient.
  • Forgetting that every company must file its annual return (Form MGT-7) within 60 days from the date of the AGM.
  • Not understanding that Section 447 fraud provisions apply even if no actual loss is caused — intent to deceive is sufficient for the offence.
  • Mixing up the timelines: notice for board meeting is 7 days, but for general meeting it is 21 clear days.
