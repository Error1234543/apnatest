1. Introduction to Accounting: Meaning, Objectives, Functions and Users

["Accounting is the art of recording, classifying, and summarising financial transactions in a systematic manner, and interpreting the results thereof. It provides quantitative financial information about an enterprise to various stakeholders for decision-making. The Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) defines accounting as 'the art of recording, classifying, and summarising in a significant manner and in terms of money, transactions and events which are, in part at least, of a financial character, and interpreting the results thereof.'", 'Objectives of accounting include: (a) maintaining systematic records of all business transactions to avoid errors and omissions; (b) ascertaining the results of operations — whether profit or loss has been made during a period through the Preparation of Trading and Profit & Loss Account; (c) determining the financial position of the business at any given date through the Balance Sheet; (d) providing relevant financial information to various users for decision-making; (e) safeguarding the assets of the business by maintaining proper records and internal controls; (f) complying with legal requirements such as the Companies Act, Income Tax Act, GST laws, etc.', 'Functions of accounting are often described under three headings: (i) Recording — daily, systematic recording of transactions in the books of original entry (Journals); (ii) Classifying — grouping similar transactions together in Ledgers; (iii) Summarising — preparing Trial Balance, Final Accounts (Trading A/c, P&L A/c, Balance Sheet) for easy understanding. Beyond these, accounting also involves communicating results to users and interpreting financial data through tools like ratio analysis, cash flow statements, and funds flow statements.', 'Difference between Book-keeping and Accounting: Book-keeping is the mechanical process of recording transactions in the books of account — it is routine, clerical, and deals with the day-to-day recording. Accounting goes beyond book-keeping — it involves classifying, summarising, analyzing, interpreting, and communicating financial information. Book-keeping is a subset of accounting. All book-keepers are not accountants, but all accountants must understand book-keeping. Book-keeping ends with the preparation of Trial Balance, while accounting continues with final accounts, adjustments, interpretation, and reporting.', 'Users of accounting information are classified into Internal Users and External Users. Internal users include owners/managers who use accounting information for planning, controlling, and decision-making within the enterprise. External users include shareholders/investors (for assessing profitability and risk), creditors/suppliers (for evaluating creditworthiness), banks and financial institutions (for loan appraisal), government and tax authorities (for tax assessment and compliance), employees (for wage negotiations and job security), customers (for continuity of supply), and the general public (for understanding the contribution of the business to the economy).']

2. Accounting Principles, Conventions and Standards

['Accounting Principles are the guidelines and rules that govern the practice of accounting. They are broadly classified into Accounting Concepts, Accounting Conventions, and Accounting Standards. These principles provide the framework within which financial statements are prepared, ensuring consistency, reliability, and comparability.', 'Key Accounting Concepts: (a) Business Entity Concept — the business and its owners are separate entities. Only business transactions are recorded, not personal transactions of the owner. (b) Going Concern Concept — it is assumed that the business will continue to operate for the foreseeable future; hence assets are recorded at book value and not liquidation value. (c) Accounting Period Concept — the life of the business is divided into equal intervals (usually one year) for periodic evaluation of performance. (d) Money Measurement Concept — only those transactions that can be expressed in monetary terms are recorded in the books; qualitative aspects like employee skill or management quality are not recorded. (e) Cost Concept — assets are recorded at their original purchase cost and not at market value; depreciation is charged on historical cost. (f) Dual Aspect Concept — every transaction has two aspects (debit and credit); the fundamental accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Capital. (g) Accrual Concept — revenue and expenses are recognized when they are earned or incurred, not when cash is received or paid. (h) Matching Concept — expenses incurred to earn revenues of a particular period must be matched against the revenues of that same period. (i) Duality Principle — the basis of double-entry bookkeeping where every debit has a corresponding credit.', "Key Accounting Conventions: (a) Convention of Consistency — accounting policies and methods should be followed consistently from one period to another so that results are comparable across periods. Any change should be disclosed. (b) Convention of Conservatism (Prudence) — 'anticipate no profit but provide for all possible losses.' Expenses and liabilities are provided for even if not yet certain; assets and income are not overstated. (c) Convention of Materiality — only material (significant) items are separately disclosed; immaterial items may be grouped or expensed immediately without affecting decision-making. (d) Convention of Disclosure — full and true disclosure of all material information in financial statements to enable users to make informed decisions. Any change in accounting policy should be disclosed.", 'Accounting Standards are uniform guidelines issued by professional bodies to ensure that financial statements are prepared in a consistent and standardized manner. In India, the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) issues Accounting Standards (AS). The MCA has notified some Accounting Standards as Ind AS (Indian Accounting Standards) converged with IFRS. Important Accounting Standards for CS Foundation include: AS-1 (Disclosure of Accounting Policies), AS-2 (Valuation of Inventories), AS-3 (Cash Flow Statements), AS-4 (Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date), AS-5 (Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items and Changes in Accounting Policies), AS-10 (Accounting for Fixed Assets), AS-15 (Employee Benefits), AS-29 (Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets), and AS-30/31/32 (Financial Instruments). Accounting Standards override accounting conventions in case of conflict.']

3. Accounting Equation and Double Entry System

["The Accounting Equation is the fundamental principle underlying double-entry bookkeeping: Assets = Liabilities + Capital. This equation must always remain in balance after every transaction. Assets are resources owned by the business (cash, debtors, inventory, fixed assets). Liabilities are obligations owed to outsiders (creditors, loans). Capital is the owner's residual claim on assets after deducting liabilities. The equation reflects that every asset has a source of finance — either from owners (capital) or from outsiders (liabilities).", 'Demonstration of the Accounting Equation: When an individual starts a business by investing Rs. 1,00,000 cash, the equation becomes: Assets (Cash Rs. 1,00,000) = Liabilities (Rs. 0) + Capital (Rs. 1,00,000). If he purchases goods worth Rs. 20,000 on credit from Rohan, Assets (Cash Rs. 1,00,000 + Goods Rs. 20,000) = Liabilities (Rohan Rs. 20,000) + Capital (Rs. 1,00,000). Each transaction maintains the equality. This equation helps verify the arithmetical accuracy of entries and forms the basis of the balance sheet structure.', 'Double Entry System is the modern method of bookkeeping based on the dual aspect concept. It states that every transaction has a double effect — a debit aspect and a credit aspect. For every debit entry, there is an equivalent credit entry. The system was first systematically described by Luca Pacioli in 1494. Its advantages include: (a) complete record of all transactions; (b) automatic error detection through Trial Balance; (c) reliable basis for preparing financial statements; (d) fraud prevention due to cross-verification; (e)科学性 (scientific and logical basis).', 'Golden Rules of Double Entry: There are three categories of accounts, each with its own golden rule: (a) Personal Accounts (accounts of persons/entities) — Debit the receiver, Credit the giver. Example: If goods are purchased from Mohan, Mohan is credited as he is the giver of goods, and Purchases A/c is debited. (b) Real Accounts (accounts of assets/properties) — Debit what comes in, Credit what goes out. Example: Cash received is debited as cash comes in; machinery sold is credited as it goes out. (c) Nominal Accounts (accounts of incomes, expenses, gains, losses) — Debit all expenses and losses, Credit all incomes and gains. Example: Salary paid is debited (expense), Rent received is credited (income).', 'Modern Approach (Analytical Method) to Double Entry: Instead of golden rules, accounts are analyzed based on their nature: (a) Assets — increase debited, decrease credited; (b) Liabilities — increase credited, decrease debited; (c) Capital — increase credited, decrease debited; (d) Income — increase credited, decrease debited; (e) Expenses — increase debited, decrease credited. This approach is more intuitive and widely used in practice. Memorisation tip: DEEP (Debit: Expenses,drawings,Assets) and CLIC (Credit: Liabilities,Income,Capital).']

4. Bookkeeping Process: Journal, Ledger, and Cash Book

['Journal (Book of Original Entry) is the first book where transactions are recorded in chronological order with dates, particulars, debit and credit amounts. It serves as the legal evidence of transactions. The format includes Date, Particulars, Ledger Folio (LF), Debit Amount, and Credit Amount. The narration (brief explanation) is written under each entry in brackets. Journal entries follow the golden rules/modern approach. Important types of journal entries include: opening entries (on commencement), transfer entries (closing one account and opening another), closing entries (at the end of the year), and adjusting entries (for accruals, prepayments, etc.).', "Important Journal Entries to remember: Purchase of goods on credit — Purchases A/c Dr. to Supplier's A/c; Sale of goods on credit — Debtor's A/c Dr. to Sales A/c; Cash received from debtor — Cash A/c Dr. to Debtor's A/c; Goods purchased for cash — Purchases A/c Dr. to Cash A/c; Payment of rent — Rent A/c Dr. to Cash A/c; Salary paid — Salary A/c Dr. to Cash A/c; Introduction of capital by owner — Cash/Bank A/c Dr. to Owner's A/c; Purchase of machinery for cash — Machinery A/c Dr. to Cash A/c; Bought furniture — Furniture A/c Dr. to Cash/Creditor's A/c; Received commission — Cash/Bank A/c Dr. to Commission A/c; Paid insurance — Insurance A/c Dr. to Cash A/c; Interest received — Debtor's A/c Dr. to Interest A/c; Goods taken by proprietor for personal use — Drawings A/c Dr. to Purchases A/c (or Goods A/c).", "Ledger is the principal book of account where journal entries are posted (classified) into respective accounts. Each account in the ledger is called a 'T-account' with debit and credit sides. The process of posting involves transferring amounts from the journal to the relevant ledger accounts along with the date and LF number. The balance of each account is ascertained by finding the difference between total debits and credits. Closing balance is brought down (c/d) and opened as opening balance (b/d) for the next period. Important ledger accounts include: Cash A/c, Bank A/c, Purchases A/c, Sales A/c, Salaries A/c, Rent A/c, and personal accounts of debtors/creditors.", "Cash Book is a special journal that records all cash and bank transactions. It serves as both a book of original entry and a ledger (cash account). It has three columns: Cash, Bank, and Discount. There are different types of cash books: (a) Simple Cash Book — single column for cash only; (b) Double Column Cash Book — cash and bank columns; (c) Triple Column Cash Book — cash, bank, and discount columns. A Cash Book is never balanced; the balance appears directly in the Trial Balance as the balance carried forward on the credit side (if bank balance is credit, it represents an overdraft). Contra entries are recorded in the Cash Book when there is a transaction between cash and bank (e.g., cash deposited into bank, cash withdrawn from bank) — marked with 'C' in the LF column.", 'Petty Cash Book (Imprest System): A small business maintains a Chief Cashier who distributes a fixed amount (imprest amount) to a Petty Cashier for daily small expenses. The Petty Cash Book records small expenditures. At the end of the period, the Petty Cashier submits vouchers and is reimbursed to restore the imprest amount to its original fixed level. The Imprest System ensures control over petty cash and prevents misuse. The reimbursement entry in the main Cash Book is: Petty Cash A/c Dr. to Cash/Bank A/c.']

5. Trial Balance, Final Accounts and Adjustments

['A Trial Balance is a statement prepared to check the arithmetical accuracy of ledger postings. It lists all ledger balances (debit and credit) on a given date. If the totals of debit and credit columns agree, it indicates that double entry has been correctly applied (though it does not guarantee absence of all errors). It is prepared before final accounts.Formats: (a) Columnar format with separate debit and credit columns; (b) Balance format with balances shown on their respective sides. Items shown on the debit side include: assets, expenses, losses, drawings. Items shown on the credit side include: liabilities, capital, incomes, gains.', 'Final Accounts consist of the Trading Account, Profit and Loss Account, and Balance Sheet. The Trading Account determines gross profit or loss — it is prepared by comparing opening stock, purchases, direct expenses (carriage inward, wages, freight, octroi) with closing stock and sales. Gross Profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold. The Profit and Loss Account determines net profit or loss — it considers indirect expenses (rent, salary, postage, insurance, depreciation, bad debts) and indirect incomes (discount received, commission, interest received). Net Profit = Total Incomes − Total Expenses.', "Balance Sheet is a statement showing the assets and liabilities of a business on a particular date. It follows the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital. The right side shows sources of funds (Liabilities + Capital), and the left side shows application of funds (Assets). Assets are classified as Fixed Assets (permanent use), Current/Current Assets (short-term, convertible to cash), Fictitious Assets (no real value — prepaid expense, outstanding expense), and Non-current Investments. Liabilities are classified as Capital Liability (owner's equity), Long-term Liabilities (loans > 1 year), and Current Liabilities (payable within 1 year).", 'Important Adjustments at the time of preparing Final Accounts: (a) Outstanding/accrued expenses — expense incurred but not paid; debit to P&L A/c, show as liability in Balance Sheet; (b) Prepaid expenses — expense paid in advance; credit to P&L A/c (reduce expense), show as asset in Balance Sheet; (c) Outstanding/receivable income — income earned but not received; debit to Receivable A/c (asset), credit to P&L A/c; (d) Prepaid/received in advance income — income received in advance; credit to Advance Income A/c (liability), reduce income in P&L A/c; (e) Depreciation on fixed assets — reduce asset value, charge to P&L A/c; (f) Bad debts written off — reduce debtors, charge to P&L A/c; (g) Provision for bad debts — additional provision increases the provision account (liability side), charge to P&L A/c; (h) Provision for discount on debtors — reduce debtors, charge to P&L A/c; (i) Stock undervalued/overvalued — adjust closing stock accordingly; (j) Goods taken by proprietor for personal use — reduce purchases, increase drawings; (k) Goods given away as free samples — treat as advertisement expense.']

6. Rectification of Errors, Depreciation, Provisions and Reserves

['Errors in accounting are of three types: Errors of Omission (transaction completely omitted — no entry in any book); Errors of Commission (wrong amount entered or wrong account posted — but dual aspect maintained); Errors of Principle (violation of accounting principles — e.g., treating revenue expenditure as capital expenditure, or vice versa). Errors of Omission and Commission do not affect the Trial Balance agreement, but Errors of Principle also do not affect the Trial Balance since they involve wrong classification but still maintain double entry.', "Errors that DO affect the Trial Balance agreement include: (a) partial omission of credit entry; (b) wrong totaling of a subsidiary book; (c) wrong casting (addition/subtraction) of a ledger account; (d) posting to the wrong side; (e) posting a single entry. The difference in the Trial Balance is temporarily adjusted through a 'Suspense Account' pending rectification. Rectification of errors uses the reverse of the original incorrect entry: if an amount was debited to the wrong account, credit that wrong account and debit the correct one; if an amount was wholly omitted, pass the complete entry; if the wrong amount was recorded, pass a supplemental entry for the difference.", "Depreciation is the permanent, gradual, and continuous decrease in the book value of a fixed asset due to usage, passage of time, obsolescence, or exhaustion. Reasons for providing depreciation: (a) true profit ascertainment — matching cost with revenue; (b) true financial position — assets shown at realistic values; (c) fund adequacy for replacement; (d) compliance with legal requirements. Methods of depreciation: (a) Straight Line Method (Fixed Installment Method) — equal depreciation every year on original cost; useful when asset's efficiency remains constant. (b) Diminishing Balance Method (Written Down Value Method) — depreciation is calculated on the reducing book value each year; useful when repair costs increase as the asset ages. Formula for WDV: Rate of Depreciation = 1 − (S/C)^(1/n), where S = Scrap value, C = Cost, n = Useful life.", "Provisions and Reserves: A Provision is an appropriation of profit made to meet a known liability or to reduce the book value of an asset (e.g., provision for depreciation, provision for bad debts, provision for tax). A Reserve is an appropriation of profit kept for future use — it strengthens the financial position. Reserves are classified as: (a) Revenue Reserve — created out of revenue profits (general reserve, reserve for specific purposes like dividend equalization); (b) Capital Reserve — created out of capital profits (profit on sale of fixed assets, premium on shares) — can only be used for capital purposes. 'Specific Reserve' refers to reserves created for a specific purpose (e.g., debenture redemption reserve). 'General Reserve' is unappropriated profit shown under Reserves and Surplus in the Balance Sheet."]

7. Bills of Exchange and Promissory Notes

['A Bill of Exchange is a written instrument containing an unconditional order, signed by the drawer, directing a certain person (drawee) to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person (payee) or to the bearer of the instrument. It requires acceptance by the drawee before it becomes enforceable. Key parties: Drawer (maker who creates the bill), Drawee (person directed to pay — becomes Acceptor on accepting), Payee (person to whom payment is to be made — may be the drawer himself). A promissory note, by contrast, is an unconditional promise made by the maker to pay a certain sum to the payee — it does not require acceptance.', 'Important differences between Bill of Exchange and Promissory Note: (a) Bill is an order; Note is a promise. (b) Bill requires acceptance; Note does not. (c) In a bill, two parties are primarily liable (drawer and acceptor); in a note, only one party (maker) is liable. (d) A bill can be drawn on a person; a note is made by a person. (e) A bill may be addressed to more than one drawee; a note is signed by one maker. However, both are negotiable instruments under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881.', "Accounting for Bills of Exchange: When a bill is accepted by the drawee, the Acceptee A/c is debited and Bill Receivable A/c is credited (from the drawer's perspective). When the bill is dishonoured (not paid on maturity), the creditor's original A/c is revived — Creditor A/c Dr. to Bill Receivable A/c. Notes of Dishonour are prepared. When a bill is discounted with the bank, Bank A/c is debited with the amount received (after deducting discount charges), Discount A/c is debited with the discount charges, and Bill Receivable A/c is credited. If the discounted bill is later dishonoured, the Bank A/c is debited with the full face value, and the account of the person from whom the bill was discounted is debited with a note of dishonour.", "Renewal of Bills: When a bill is renewed, the old Bill Receivable A/c is closed by transferring it to the new Bill Receivable A/c (or to the Acceptee's A/c). If a new bill is accepted in place of the old one, Old Bill Receivable A/c Dr. to New Bill Receivable A/c. If the acceptor pays a part amount and accepts a new bill for the balance, the part payment is recorded separately. Any allowance/rebate granted is debited to the Acceptee's A/c."]

8. Consignment and Joint Venture Accounts

['Consignment is a dispatch of goods by one person (Consignor) to another (Consignee) for sale, where the consignor retains ownership until the goods are sold. The consignee acts as an agent and earns a commission. Expenses may be borne by either party. Key features: ownership remains with consignor; risk is borne by consignor; consignor can resume goods at any time; normal loss (breakage, spillage) is absorbed by consignor; abnormal loss (fire, theft) is treated separately.', "Accounting treatment for Consignment: The Consignment A/c is a personal account of the consignee (nominal account in nature for profit/loss calculation). It is debited with: cost of goods sent, non-recurring expenses (freight, insurance, cartage, warehousing), and valued abnormal loss. It is credited with: sales proceeds, recurring expenses allowed by consignee, and abnormal loss (at market value). The difference is Gross Profit/Loss. Normal loss is not valued — it increases the per-unit cost of remaining goods. Abnormal loss is valued at selling price (if undetected at the time) or cost plus proportional profit. Commission to the consignee is usually: (a) ordinary commission (percentage on sales); (b) del credere commission (extra percentage for guaranteeing credit customers — only if explicitly stated). For valuing closing stock, if 'normal loss' is given, units available for sale = Goods sent − Normal loss.", 'Joint Venture is a temporary partnership formed for executing a particular project or undertaking. Each venturer contributes capital and shares profit/loss in an agreed ratio. Unlike partnership, a joint venture does not create a separate legal entity; it is dissolved once the venture is completed. Accounting can be maintained by: (a) keeping separate books — each venturer maintains their own records and an equal set of books is maintained jointly; (b) not keeping separate books — each venturer records their own transactions and adjusts through a Joint Venture A/c in their individual books. The Joint Venture A/c is a nominal account — debited with all expenses/losses and credited with all incomes/gains; the balance represents profit or loss to be shared in the agreed ratio.', 'Key difference between Consignment and Joint Venture: In consignment, the consignee is merely an agent (does not share profit/loss); in joint venture, each venturer shares profit/loss. In consignment, ownership of goods remains with consignor; in joint venture, each venturer owns their contributed portion. Consignment is ongoing/recurring; joint venture is temporary/single-purpose.']

9. Audit: Meaning, Objectives, Types and Procedures

["Audit is the independent examination of financial information of an entity, whether profit-oriented or not, and of any other information related thereto, to express an opinion thereon. According to the ICSI Guidance Note on 'Audit Objectives and Procedures', audit is a systematic and independent examination of books, accounts, and documents to ascertain whether they faithfully reflect the transactions recorded therein. The primary aim of audit is to enable the auditor to make a report to the members/shareholders about whether the financial statements give a 'true and fair view' of the state of affairs and the profit/loss for the period.", 'Objectives of Audit: (a) Primary Objectives — (i) to ascertain whether financial statements disclose a true and fair view of the state of affairs; (ii) to detect and prevent errors and frauds. (b) Secondary Objectives — (i) assisting management in improving operational efficiency; (ii) providing evidence in legal disputes; (iii) helping in tax assessments. An audit does not guarantee detection of all errors and frauds — it provides reasonable assurance, not absolute assurance.', 'Types of Audit: (a) Statutory Audit — mandatory under the Companies Act for companies; conducted by a qualified chartered accountant. (b) Departmental Audit — conducted by government departments for public sector undertakings. (c) Concurrent Audit — conducted simultaneously with the occurrence of transactions, mainly in banking. (d) Stock Audit — verification of stock-in-hand by a specialist auditor. (e) Tax Audit — under the Income Tax Act, section 44AB, when turnover exceeds specified limits. (f) Special Audit — ordered by tax authorities or court under specific provisions. (g) Internal Audit — examination of internal controls and systems by employees or external specialists. (h) Cost Audit — verification of cost records under the Companies (Cost Records) Rules.', "Auditor's Duties and Responsibilities: The auditor must examine the books and accounts, verify assets and liabilities, check compliance with statutory requirements, report on the truth and fairness of financial statements, and communicate findings to those charged with governance. The auditor has a duty to exercise reasonable care, skill, and diligence. Key procedures include: Vouching (checking supporting documents for transactions recorded) and Verification (confirming the existence, ownership, and valuation of assets and liabilities shown in the Balance Sheet). Vouching relates to the Profit and Loss Account (revenue items), while verification relates to the Balance Sheet (capital/asset items).", "Vouching vs Verification: Vouching is the process of examining original documents (invoices, receipts, vouchers) to verify the authenticity and accuracy of transactions recorded in the books. It tests the 'occurrence' and 'accuracy' assertions. Verification is the process of confirming the existence, ownership, valuation, and disclosure of assets and liabilities at the balance sheet date. It tests 'existence', 'rights and obligations', 'valuation', and 'presentation' assertions. Vouching is done before final accounts; verification is done after final accounts. Both are essential and complementary — neither replaces the other."]

10. Important Exam Topics: Accounting Standards Summary and Common Areas of Confusion

['Summary of Important Accounting Standards for CS Foundation: AS-1 — Disclosure of Accounting Policies (requires disclosure of significant accounting policies adopted); AS-2 — Valuation of Inventories (inventories valued at lower of cost or net realizable value; cost formulas: FIFO or Weighted Average); AS-3 — Cash Flow Statements (cash flows classified into operating, investing, financing activities); AS-4 — Contingencies and Events After Balance Sheet Date (contingent liabilities disclosed but not recognized; events after BS date classified as adjusting/non-adjusting); AS-5 — Net Profit or Loss and Prior Period Items (prior period items disclosed separately below extraordinary items); AS-10 — Accounting for Fixed Assets (initial measurement at cost, subsequent measurement); AS-15 — Employee Benefits (defined contribution and defined benefit plans); AS-29 — Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets (provision recognized when present obligation exists, reliable estimate possible); AS-30/31/32 — Financial Instruments (recognition, measurement, hedge accounting, consolidation).', 'Common areas of confusion for students: (a) Outstanding expense vs. Prepaid expense — Outstanding means not yet paid (liability); Prepaid means paid in advance (asset). Both appear in the Balance Sheet — outstanding on the liability side, prepaid on the asset side. (b) Accrued income vs. Received in advance — Accrued income is earned but not received (asset); Received in advance is received but not earned (liability). (c) Provision vs. Reserve — Provision is a charge against profit (known liability/asset reduction); Reserve is an appropriation of profit (retained earnings). Provision reduces profit; reserve does not. (d) Error of Omission vs. Error of Commission — Omission means entire transaction missed; Commission means wrong posting but dual aspect maintained. (e) Bill Receivable vs. Bill Payable — Bill Receivable is an asset (bill accepted by us); Bill Payable is a liability (bill accepted by us payable to others). (f) Consignment vs. Sale — In consignment, goods can be returned; in sale, goods are transferred with no right of return unless agreed.', 'Exam weightage trends: This chapter carries significant weightage in CS Foundation exams. Most questions are based on: (a) Journal entries (including bills of exchange); (b) Treatment of adjustments in Final Accounts; (c) Accounting for errors and suspense account; (d) Accounting standards identification; (e) Basic concepts and terminology; (f) Audit types and objectives. Students should practice numerical problems on Journal, Ledger, Trial Balance, Final Accounts, and Bills of Exchange, while also memorizing key definitions and standards for MCQ theory questions.']

Important facts

  • Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital — must always balance after every transaction
  • Golden Rules: Personal — Debit the receiver, Credit the giver; Real — Debit what comes in, Credit what goes out; Nominal — Debit all expenses/losses, Credit all incomes/gains
  • Double Entry System: Every transaction has a dual aspect — debit and credit of equal amounts
  • Trial Balance checks arithmetical accuracy only — errors of omission, principle, and compensating errors are not detected
  • AS-2: Inventories valued at lower of cost or Net Realizable Value (NRV)
  • AS-4: Contingent liabilities are disclosed but NOT recognized; adjusting events after balance sheet date require adjustment; non-adjusting events require disclosure only
  • AS-29: Provision recognized when (a) present obligation exists, (b) probable outflow, (c) reliable estimate possible
  • Outstanding/accrued expenses increase liability and are added to expense in P&L; Prepaid expenses decrease expense and are shown as asset
  • Normal loss is not valued and increases per-unit cost of remaining goods; Abnormal loss is valued and shown separately
  • In Consignment, the Consignor retains ownership; the Consignee is merely an agent earning commission
  • Joint Venture is temporary — dissolved upon completion; each venturer shares profit/loss in agreed ratio
  • Statutory Audit is mandatory under Companies Act; Tax Audit under Section 44AB of Income Tax Act
  • Vouching checks transactions against documentary evidence (P&L items); Verification confirms asset/liability existence and value (Balance Sheet items)
  • Provision is a charge against profit; Reserve is an appropriation of profit — fundamental distinction
  • Suspense Account is used temporarily when Trial Balance does not tally, pending rectification of errors
  • DEEP rule: Debit Expenses, Drawings, Assets; CLIC rule: Credit Liabilities, Income, Capital

Formulas and relationships

Gross Profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold, where COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses − Closing Stock
Net Profit = Gross Profit − Indirect Expenses + Indirect Incomes
Rate of Depreciation (WDV method) = 1 − (Scrap Value / Original Cost)^(1/n), where n = useful life in years
Per Unit Cost (with Normal Loss) = (Total Cost − Value of Normal Loss, if any) / (Total Units − Normal Loss Units)
Closing Stock Value = Unsold Units × Per Unit Cost
Abnormal Loss (at Selling Price) = (Units Lost / Total Units Available) × Sales Value of Goods Sent on Consignment
Abnormal Loss (at Cost + Proportionate Profit) = Abnormal Loss at Cost + (Abnormal Loss Units / Available Units) × Total Profit
Ordinary Commission = Agreed Percentage × Total Sales (or Net Sales depending on terms)
Del Credere Commission = Agreed Percentage × Credit Sales
Discount on Bill Discounted = Face Value × Rate × Time (in years)

Common mistakes

  • Confusing outstanding expense (liability, add to expense) with prepaid expense (asset, deduct from expense) — remember: outstanding = owed but unpaid; prepaid = paid but not yet consumed
  • Treating all errors as affecting the Trial Balance — errors of omission, principle, and compensating errors do NOT affect Trial Balance agreement
  • Forgetting that abormal loss on consignment should be valued at selling price if it cannot be detected before stock verification — this overstates profit if ignored
  • Recording depreciation as a provision for value fall instead of recognizing it as a systematic allocation of cost over useful life
  • Mixing up Bill Receivable (asset, we are the creditor) with Bill Payable (liability, we are the debtor) — remember: Receivable means we expect to receive money
  • Valuing closing stock at market price when market price is higher than cost — AS-2 mandates LOWER of cost or NRV; never value above cost
  • Ignoring the dual aspect in rectification of errors — every correction must maintain Assets = Liabilities + Capital
  • Not distinguishing between vouching (verifying recorded transactions) and verification (confirming asset/liability existence) — these serve different purposes
  • Assuming that agreed Trial Balance means no errors — errors of omission, principle, and compensating errors remain undetected
  • Treating a Joint Venture as a Partnership — Joint Venture has no separate legal identity and is temporary; Partnership creates an ongoing relationship
  • Recording goods sent on consignment as sales in the Books of Consignor — consignment is NOT a sale until the consignee sells to a third party
  • Forgetting that ordinary commission may sometimes be calculated on Net Sales (Sales − Returns) rather than Gross Sales — always check the terms
