Drafting, Pleadings and Appearances — Professional Group 1 (CS)

CSEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Drafting: Principles, Structure and Professional Standards

Drafting is the skill of presenting facts, law and prayer in a precise, legally acceptable form so that a tribunal or authority can understand the issue, appreciate the claim and pass an effective order. For a practicing Company Secretary, drafting is not creative writing — it is regulated by statute, procedural codes, rules of the concerned forum and precedent. The core principles of good drafting are: clarity (each sentence must convey one proposition), accuracy (dates, section numbers, parties' names and cause titles must be verified), relevance (every paragraph should advance the prayer), brevity (tribunals prefer concise submissions over voluminous narration), and compliance (the draft must satisfy the formal requirements of the applicable rules — format, annexures, verification, affidavit, fees). A well-drafted pleading anticipates objections; it does not merely repeat facts. In the CS Professional context, drafting is tested through both theoretical questions and practical papers, and marks are awarded for adherence to format, completeness of necessary averments, correct citation of provisions and logical sequencing of prayers.

Every application, petition or appeal follows an identifiable structure:
- Cause title: identifies the forum, the parties (petitioner/applicant versus respondent), and the nature of the proceeding.
- Number and date: assigned by the registry; the draft must leave a blank for this or note that it will be filled on filing.
- Introduction/preliminary: brief statement of who is filing, in what capacity, and under which statutory provision(s).
- Facts of the case: chronological narration divided into numbered paragraphs, each dealing with a single fact or group of closely related facts. Avoid argumentative language in the facts section.
- Grounds: the legal basis for the relief sought, each ground numbered and tied to a specific provision or rule.
- Prayer: the specific relief(s) requested, clearly enumerated and within the forum's jurisdiction.
- Annexures: list of documents relied upon, with brief description.
- Verification and affidavit: mandatory in most applications before NCLT/NCLAT/SEBI to ensure truthfulness.

Common structural errors that lead to rejection or remand include: missing verification, incorrect cause title (naming wrong party or wrong forum), non-compliance with mandatory format prescribed under the relevant rules, failure to file supporting affidavit, and prayer that exceeds the statutory powers of the tribunal.

2. Statutory Framework Governing Drafting Before NCLT and NCLAT

The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) derive their powers and procedures primarily from Sections 408 to 449 of the Companies Act, 2013, read with the National Company Law Tribunals Rules, 2016 (as amended), and the NCLT Regulations, 2016. A Company Secretary must know the procedural backbone before attempting any draft because the rules determine format, limitation, jurisdiction and the consequences of defectiveness.

Key provisions to internalize:
- Section 408: Appointment of Chairperson and Members — establishes the constitutional validity of the tribunal.
- Section 420(1): Application of the Act and rules — NCLT is bound by the Act, rules and regulations made thereunder; residual procedural gap-filling draws on principles of natural justice.
- Section 424: Jurisdiction — exclusive jurisdiction over matters listed in Schedule VI; transfer and invocation provisions.
- Section 425: Exercise of jurisdiction and powers — tribunals may exercise powers suo motu or on application; no civil court has jurisdiction over matters falling within Schedule VI.
- Section 429: Appeals to NCLAT — time limit of forty-five days from the date of the order (Section 429(2)); extension for sufficient cause.
- Section 432: Procedure and powers of NCLT — summary procedure where appropriate, discovery and inspection, summoning, commission appointments, interim orders.
- Section 435: Right of audience — advocates enrolled under the Advocates Act, 1961, and professionals recognized under any law (including Company Secretaries) may appear.
- Section 448: Penalty for frivolous or vexatious claims — deterrence against misuse of drafting privilege.

Under the NCLT Rules, the standard format for applications is governed by Rule 17 of the National Company Law Tribunals Rules, 2016, which prescribes the form and contents of applications. The Limitation Act, 1963 applies to applications before NCLT where the underlying right is Time-barred if not filed within the prescribed period; Section 14 (exclusion of time spent prosecuting another bona fide proceeding) is frequently invoked in drafting.

Before drafting any application, always confirm: (a) whether the matter falls within Schedule VI, (b) whether the limitation period is expiring, (c) whether prior approval or notice is statutorily required (e.g., Section 241-242 petitions require the petitioner to demonstrate locus standi under Section 244), and (d) whether the cause title correctly reflects the substantive role of each party. NCLT practice has evolved through numerous judicial decisions; citations such as V.B. Ruddar Ltd. v. CIT and Madhavilata Singhal v. NCLT establish that the tribunal is not a subordinate court and retains inherent powers, but these cannot override express statutory bars.

3. Drafting under Sections 241-242: Oppression and Mismanagement Petitions

Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act, 2013 provide remedies against oppressive and prejudicial conduct by promoters or majority shareholders. These are among the most frequently drafted petitions in corporate dispute practice, and their complexity demands rigorous attention to procedural prerequisites and prayer alignment.

Preliminary requirements before filing:
- Locus standi (Section 244): A petition may be presented by not less than 100 members or members holding not less than 10% of the paid-up share capital, or by any member(s) holding shares consequent upon transmission. For a private company, the threshold is one-fifth of the number of its members. If the company is being wound up, half the members or half the paid-up capital applies.
- Prior application to the Board (Section 241(2)): Before presenting the petition, the applicant must represent to the Board of Directors requesting them to take steps to remedy the Grievance, and the Board must have failed to do so within sixty days — unless the Tribunal permits waiver on the grounds that the representation would be futile or that delay would prejudice the applicant.
- Limitation: The petition should ordinarily be filed within three years from the date the cause of action arose; delay must be explained with cogent reasons.

Structure of the oppression/mismanagement petition:
1. Cause title with precise designation: 'In the matter of [Company Name] and In the matter of Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act, 2013'.
2. Particulars of the petitioner(s) and their shareholding to establish eligibility.
3. Description of the company: incorporation details, registered office, nature of business, share capital structure, and management history.
4. Factual matrix: detailed chronology of events giving rise to the allegations — exclusion from management, diversion of assets, non-payment of dividends, dilution of shareholding, siphoning of funds, irregular allotments, abuse of controlling position.
5. Specific allegations of oppression and mismanagement with references to documents and board resolutions.
6. Legal grounds: invocation of Section 241 read with Section 432, reliance on judicial precedents (e.g., Reliance Industries Ltd. v. Securities and Exchange Board of India; Hari Prasad Aggarwal v. M/s HP Agencies).
7. Prayer: list specific reliefs — declaration that certain acts are oppressive, direction for purchase of shares at fair value, regulation of future conduct, appointment of director(s), restoration of documents, and costs.
8. Interim relief prayer: maintain status quo, restrain alienation of assets, stay board resolutions challenged, under Section 241(3) and Section 432(3).

Important drafting nuance: the prayer must be tailored to what the Tribunal can actually grant under Section 242. Common excesses to avoid include praying for winding up (unless alternatively prayed under Section 243), claiming damages (the Tribunal awards compensation in a different form), or seeking directions outside the company's affairs. The petition must distinguish between past misconduct (for which compensation may lie) and continuing oppression (for which injunctive relief is warranted).

4. Drafting Applications under Sections 39, 42, 74, 75 and Related Provisions

A practicing Company Secretary encounters several recurring application types arising from the Companies Act, 2013. Mastery of these standard formats is essential because the examination tests ability to produce correct drafts under pressure.

Application for waiver under Section 39 (public issue prospectus): When a company seeks exemption from the requirement of issuing a prospectus in certain public issue scenarios, the application must address the statutory conditions, the nature of exemptions claimed, and support with documents like the project report, board resolution authorizing the issue, and certification by the CS. Key contents: identification of the sponsor/promoter group, justification for reliance on Section 39(2) or (3), disclosure of the mode of issue (rights, preferential, placement), and undertaking to comply with SEBI ICDR Regulations.

Application under Section 42 (private placement): This requires drafting a Board Resolution, a Letter of Offer (Form MGT-05), and compliance with the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014. Essential elements: name and details of proposed allottees, price and consideration, valuation report under Section 243, return of allotment under Section 39, and records maintenance. The application to the Board must specify: class of security, number, price, mode of payment, and justification for selecting particular persons.

Application for enforcement of charge under Section 74: When a company defaults in repayment of debenture monies, debenture holders may apply for enforcement. The draft must include: details of the debenture issue, amount outstanding, terms of repayment, default particulars, and the relief sought (appointment of receiver, sale of charged assets, or direction for payment). Annexure requirements are critical — trust deed, debenture certificate, repayment schedule and demand notices must be exhibited.

Application under Section 75 for revalidation of forfeited/dormant shares: Where share certificates are lost or destroyed, or shares become dormant, the company must apply under Section 75 for revalidation of issued share capital. The draft requires: board resolution, indemnity bond from the shareholder, confirmation of payment of full face value, gazette notification proof, and NOC from the company secretary in practice.

Each of these applications follows a common skeleton but diverges in substantive requirements dictated by the governing section. Always verify the latest form number prescribed under the Companies (Forms) Rules, 2014, as amendments are frequent. For instance, Form INC-25 (now superseded or modified) was historically used for NCLT applications; current practice requires using the generic application format under the NCLT Rules with reference to the specific section.

5. Drafting Corporate Governance and Compliance Applications (Meetings, Resolutions and Disclosures)

Beyond litigation-style petitions, a Company Secretary drafts numerous routine governance applications and filings. These test precision with statutory timelines, form numbers and procedural correctness.

Board Meeting requisition and agenda drafting:
- A member under Section 168 may requisition the Board to call a meeting. The draft requisition must state: purpose of the meeting, business to be transacted, and supporting member signatures holding requisite percentage.
- Agenda items must reference the applicable section, rule or accounting standard, and include requisite disclosures (e.g., related party transactions under Section 188 require prior Board approval and, in some cases, shareholder approval under Section 189).

Special Resolution and Ordinary Resolution drafting:
- Every resolution must contain the exact operative language: 'BE IT RESOLVED THAT...' for special resolutions and 'RESOLVED AS AN ORDINARY RESOLUTION THAT...' for ordinary resolutions.
- Special resolutions (Section 106) require 75% majority and 21-day notice; include objects clause, effective date and authorization for CS to file Form MGT-14 within 30 days.
- Frequently tested scenarios: alteration of memorandum (Section 13), reduction of share capital (Section 66), appointment of alternate director (Section 167), donation to political party (Section 182), and investment beyond 60% of free reserves (Section 180).

Compliance certificates and reports:
- CSR report under Section 135: require drafting of annual report on CSR activities including spill-over amounts, unspent CSR funds treatment, and governance mechanisms.
- Secretarial Audit Report (Form MR-3): under Section 204, draft must address every clause in the prescribed format, with qualification and explanatory notes where compliance is deficient.
- Internal Financial Controls report: required for listed companies, must reflect testing methodology, scope and findings.

Drafting these documents demands awareness that errors in form numbers, deadlines or resolution wording can result in penalties under Section 447 (fraud) or Section 454 (default in filing). A good practice is to maintain a drafting checklist for each form type.

6. Drafting Before SEBI: Applications, Submissions and Compliance Filings

When a company enters the capital markets, the Company Secretary's drafting portfolio expands to include submissions before the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI). Though the ICSI syllabus emphasizes statutory drafting under the Companies Act, modern practice requires familiarity with SEBI regulatory filings, especially for listed entities and those pursuing IPOs, rights issues, or buybacks.

IPO/SEO prospectus drafting:
- Governed by SEBI ICDR Regulations, 2018. The CS must ensure the draft prospectus contains: risk factors, object of the issue, mode and pricing, use of proceeds, promoter background, financial statements prepared under Ind AS, grievance redressal mechanism and key managerial personnel details.
- The CS in Practice certifies compliance with disclosure requirements; the draft must include the CS certificate under Regulation 7 of ICDR.
- Substantial modification after filing requires resubmission with a copy marked 'modified'.

Submission under SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015:
- Regular disclosures include quarterly results, shareholder meetings, material events under Regulation 30, and insider trading compliance under SEBI (PIT) Regulations.
- The CS drafts board presentations, committee reports and explanations for non-compliance when invited by SEBI.
- Complaint response drafting: when SEBI issues show-cause or inquiry notices, the response must be structured chronologically, address each allegation separately, cite documentary evidence and rely on favorable precedents where applicable.

Takeover Code (SEBI (SAST) Regulations, 2011):
- Drafting of open offer triggers, public announcement, and shareholding pattern computations is within the CS domain. Key drafting elements: acquirer's intention, consideration per equity share, escrow arrangements, and timeline compliance.
- Volume-based disclosures under Regulation 31 of SAST must be drafted with precision because missing a threshold even by a small percentage can attract penal action.

SEBI also entertains applications for relaxation, exemption and clarification. Each such draft must be supported by a factual matrix, legal grounds and a clear prayer. The tone should be respectful but firm, acknowledging regulatory concern while establishing the company's compliance position.

7. Drafting Before the Registrar of Companies and Other Authorities

The Registrar of Companies (ROC) remains the primary regulator for companies incorporated under the Act. A Company Secretary drafts numerous applications, returns and correspondence addressed to the ROC throughout the company's lifecycle.

Applications requiring ROC approval:
- Change of name (Section 13): draft includes board resolution, shareholder resolution (special), justification for name change, confirmation of no objections from concerned agencies, and undertaking regarding pending litigations.
- Registration of charge (Sections 77-78): draft mortgage/deed of assignment must accompany the application in Form CHG-1; the CS must ensure the charge description is precise, property schedule complete and creation/enforcement/variation/modification dates accurate.
- Reduction of share capital (Section 66): requires NCLT sanction first, followed by ROC registration. The draft application to ROC must exhibit the NCLT order and confirm creditor protection measures.
- Conversion of public to private company or vice versa: multiple steps involving special resolution, amendment of memorandum, and fresh registration.

Show-cause response and reply drafting:
- ROC may issue notices for failure to file annual returns (Form MGT-7), financial statements (AOC-4), or for irregular allotments. The response must: admit or deny each allegation, explain the reason for delay, cite force majeure or genuine inability where applicable, propose remedial steps already taken and pray for condonation of delay under Section 403.
- Always enclose the belated documents together with the delayed filing fee plus additional fees as per the Companies (Registration Offices and Fees) Rules.

Other authorities:
- Income Tax Department: responses to assessment notices, refund claims and appeal drafts under the Income Tax Act.
- NCLC (National Company Law Cell): applications for review and rectification under Section 438.
- Provincial Governments/MCA portals: e-form filings under the MCA21 system require careful attention to data fields, digital signature validity and OTP-based authentication.

Drafting convention for all ROC communications: use formal language, reference the specific section and subsection, quote the notice number and date, and request specific relief rather than making general submissions.

8. Appearance Before Tribunals: Rights, Procedures and Practical Conduct

A Company Secretary's authority to appear before tribunals is defined by law and cannot be presumed. Understanding the scope of representation prevents unauthorized practice complaints and strengthens professional standing.

Statutory right of appearance:
- Section 435(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 grants the right of audience to: (a) advocates enrolled under the Advocates Act, 1961; (b) any other person authorized by any law to appear before a tribunal; (c) any officer, employee or director of the company; (d) any person employed by the company in administrative or legal capacity; and (e) any Company Secretary in whole-time practice certified by ICSI.
- Section 435(2) clarifies that nothing in sub-section (1) empowers a non-lawyer to appear on behalf of another person except in the capacities listed. Thus, a CS in practice may appear for the company they serve but not for unrelated third parties.
- The ICSI Certificate of Practice is the foundational document establishing eligibility. Its scope limits practice to companies, LLPs and allied entities.

Procedure for appearance:
- Vakalatnama or written authorization: the tribunal requires a signed authorization from the client appointing the CS as representative. The form must contain: client details, representative details, scope of authority (to file, appear, withdraw, compromise), date and place of execution, and notary attestation.
- Memo of appearance: filed with the registry along with theCertificate of Practice, enrollment number and chamber address.
- Court room etiquette: address the bench as 'My Lord' or 'Madam'; refer to opponents as 'learned counsel' or 'representative'; rise when entering or leaving; submit documents in triplicate; keep electronic devices silenced.

Practical conduct during hearings:
- Brief note of facts: prepare a one-page summary highlighting dates, key documents, legal provisions and desired relief.
- Index of books: tabular index of all bound volumes with page references and document descriptions.
- Case law compilation: extract leading propositions from cited judgments; do not read entire judgments.
- Cross-examination preparation: if examining witnesses, prepare a tight questioning sequence; avoid open-ended queries.

Common procedural failures: failing to file the memo of appearance before the hearing date, producing unauthorized persons as representatives, omitting the original Certificate of Practice, and failing to serve copies on opposite parties as required by the rules of procedure. The tribunal may refuse to hear the matter if these formalities are not complied with.

9. Drafting Appeals, Review and Revision Applications

Post-decision drafting is as important as initial application drafting. An improperly framed appeal can result in dismissal on technical grounds even when the merit is strong.

Appeal to NCLAT (Section 429):
- Filed within 45 days from the date of the NCLT order; condonation of delay requires a separate application under Section 5 of the Limitation Act with compelling reasons (illness, postal delay, misconception about jurisdiction, suppression of material by opposite party).
- Structure: appellant-appellee cause title, cross-reference to the NCLT order (date, bench, case number), brief facts of the NCLT proceeding, grounds of appeal (each numbered), prayer for setting aside or modifying the impugned order, and prayer for stay of operation pending disposal.
- Mandatory annexures: certified copy of the NCLT order, memo of appearance, vakalatnama, index of documents, and copy of the limitation condonation application (if applicable).
- A critical distinction: NCLAT has appellate jurisdiction over orders of NCLT but cannot revisit findings of fact unless they are perverse or based on no evidence. Grounds should focus on legal error, jurisdictional excess or procedural irregularity rather than re-agitating facts.

Review Application (Section 438 read with NCLT Rules):
- Maintainable only on: (a) discovery of new and important evidence, (b) mistake or error apparent on the face of the record, or (c) any other sufficient reason.
- Time limit: thirty days from the date of the order or discovery of error.
- Drafting tip: do not use review as an avenue for rehearing; articulate precisely what error is patent and how it affects the outcome.

Revision Application:
- Limited jurisdiction under Section 438 — the Tribunal may call for records to correct a miscarriage of justice.
- Rarely granted; the draft should demonstrate that no alternative remedy exists and that the error is jurisdictional or illegal.

Appeal against NCLAT to Supreme Court (Section 431):
- Only on substantial question of law. The draft petition must frame the question precisely; vague statements of grievance are dismissed at the admission stage.

Common drafting pitfalls: filing the appeal in the wrong forum (NCLAT instead of SC or vice versa), mixing grounds with facts, praying for relief beyond the tribunal's appellate jurisdiction, and failing to obtain a certified copy of the impugned order.

10. Practical Drafting Exercises, Common Errors and Examination Strategy

The CS Professional examination evaluates drafting through scenario-based questions requiring candidates to produce a complete application, petition, resolution or response within a time-constrained setting. Success depends on systematic preparation and recognition of recurring question patterns.

Frequently tested drafting areas:
- Petition under Sections 241-242 alleging oppression and mismanagement.
- Application for condonation of delay in filing.
- Drafting of Board Resolution and Special Resolution.
- Application for change of name, reduction of capital or amalgamation approval.
- Response to show-cause notice from ROC or SEBI.
- Draft petition for investigation under Section 21.
- Memo of appearance and vakalatnama.
- Appeal to NCLAT with grounds.
- Application for review before NCLT.
- Compliance certificate draft under Section 204.

Common errors examined candidates repeatedly commit:
- Missing verification clause and affidavit — this is fatal and attracts immediate disqualification of the draft.
- Incorrect limitation calculation — the clock starts from the date of the impugned act or knowledge, not from discovery in all cases; applicants must explicitly address delay.
- Vague or over-broad prayer — each relief must be specific, justiciable and within jurisdiction.
- Omitting antecedent approvals — e.g., applying for reduction of capital without showing NCLT sanction or creditor protection.
- Failing to exhibit mandatory documents — annexure lists must match the documents actually produced.
- Mis-naming parties — the petitioner must be the real party in interest; using a nomina brand or unrelated entity invalidates locus.
- Ignoring alternative remedies — some statutes require exhausting internal grievance mechanisms before approaching the tribunal; failure to aver this weakens the draft.
- Using argumentative language in the facts section — facts should be neutral; arguments belong in the grounds.

Examination strategy:
- Read the question twice to identify the exact document to be drafted, the authority before which it is filed, and the relief sought.
- Allocate time proportionally: 5 minutes planning, 25-30 minutes drafting, 5 minutes checking for missing essentials.
- Use headings and numbering liberally — it aids both comprehension and marking.
- Include all boilerplate clauses even when the question seems to focus on substance; examiners reward completeness.
- Where a format is prescribed (e.g., Form MR-3, Form MGT-14), reproduce the structure accurately.
- Do not invent section numbers; if unsure, write 'under the relevant provisions of the Companies Act, 2013' rather than citing incorrectly.
- Practice produces speed: draft at least ten different application types under timed conditions before the examination.

Important facts

  • Sections 241-244 of the Companies Act, 2013 govern oppression and mismanagement petitions; Section 244 specifies locus standi thresholds (100 members or 10% paid-up capital for public companies; one-fifth of members for private companies).
  • A prior representation to the Board of Directors under Section 241(2) is a mandatory precondition before filing an oppression petition, unless the Tribunal grants waiver on stated grounds.
  • Section 435 confers the right of appearance before NCLT/NCLAT to Company Secretaries in whole-time practice holding a valid Certificate of Practice from ICSI.
  • Appeals to NCLAT under Section 429 must be filed within 45 days; condonation of delay requires a separate application with sufficient cause established.
  • Review applications before NCLT are maintainable only on discovery of new evidence, error apparent on the face of the record, or other sufficient reason, within 30 days.
  • The Limitation Act, 1963 applies to NCLT proceedings; Section 14 allows exclusion of time spent pursuing bona fide proceedings in courts lacking jurisdiction.
  • Section 448 imposes penalty for frivolous or vexatious claims, deterring misuse of the drafting and filing process.
  • Verification and affidavit are mandatory components of most NCLT/NCLAT applications; omission is a fatal defect leading to rejection.
  • NCLAT's appellate jurisdiction is confined to substantial questions of law and perversity of findings; it does not act as a factual appellate court.
  • SEBI ICDR Regulations, 2018 govern prospectus drafting for public issues; the CS in Practice certifies disclosure compliance under Regulation 7.
  • ROC applications for change of name, charge registration and capital reduction require specific forms and antecedent approvals that must be exhibited in the draft.
  • Vakalatnama must clearly define the scope of authorization — including filing, appearing, compromising and withdrawing — and requires notarization.
