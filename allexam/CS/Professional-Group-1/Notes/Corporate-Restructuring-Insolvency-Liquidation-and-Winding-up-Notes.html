Corporate Restructuring, Insolvency, Liquidation and Winding-up

CSEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Corporate Restructuring — Meaning, Types and Legal Framework

Corporate restructuring refers to the reorganization of a company's organizational structure, ownership, operations, or debts, primarily to make the business more profitable or better positioned for future growth. For a practicing Company Secretary, restructuring is encountered most often through schemes under Sections 230-232 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with the Companies (Compromise, Arrangement and Amalgamation) Rules, 2016.

The principal forms of restructuring include:

- Amalgamation: The legal merger of two or more companies into one. Under Section 2(1B), amalgamation means the fusion of an enterprise belonging to different persons into a single enterprise, involving transfer of property, liabilities and/or business undertakings of one company to another or to a new entity.

- Merger: A broader economic concept where one company absorbs another; in statutory terms, it is effected through the same scheme mechanism as amalgamation.

- Demerger: Transfer of undertaking(s), property, assets, liabilities or any one or more of them of the transferor company to the transferee company, provided the shareholding of shareholders in the transferee company is proportionate to their shareholding in the transferor company, as required under Section 2(19A).

- Scheme of Arrangement/Compromise: Any arrangement where a company proposes to enter into agreements with its creditors or members, or classes thereof, for compromise or arrangement of their rights, including reconstructions, share buy-backs, and reorganizations.

- Financial Restructuring: Involves modification of debt terms, debt-to-equity swaps, and debt restructuring outside the normal course.

- Takeover/Acquisition: Acquisition of control or shares in a target company by an acquirer, regulated also by SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011.

The legal framework governing restructuring in India comprises multiple statutes:

- Companies Act, 2013 — Sections 230-232 and related rules for court/tribunal-approved schemes.
- Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) Regulations — for listed entities, especially takeover and disclosure norms.
- Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Guidelines — for financial restructuring.
- Competition Act, 2002 — requires CCI approval for combinations above thresholds.
- Income Tax Act, 1961 — Sections 47, 155, 158 for tax implications of schemes.
- Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999 — for cross-border restructuring.

The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) exercises jurisdiction under Section 394 and Part X of the Companies Act to approve schemes after ensuring procedural fairness and protection of stakeholder interests.

2. Scheme of Arrangement — Procedure under Sections 230 to 232 of the Companies Act, 2013

Section 230 provides the foundational procedure for compromise or arrangement between a company and its creditors or members, or a class of them. The key steps are:

Step 1 — Application to NCLT:
The company (or creditor/member/class thereof) may file an application before the NCLT seeking directions for convening a meeting of creditors or members. The application must be supported by an explanatory statement disclosing all material facts including purpose, effect, and impact on stakeholders.

Step 2 — Ordering Meeting:
The NCLT, if satisfied that a meeting should be convened, issues an order directing the holding of the meeting of creditors or members or class thereof. The NCLT may also appoint a chairperson for the meeting and fix the date, time, venue and mode (including video conferencing). Class composition is critical — the NCLT determines whether creditors/members constitute a single class or multiple classes based on similarity of rights and interests.

Step 3 — Notice and Disclosure:
Not less than one month's notice of the meeting must be given to all concerned. The explanatory statement must be sent along with the notice, fully disclosing all material information so that stakeholders can take informed decisions.

Step 4 — Conduct of Meeting and Voting:
The meeting is conducted in accordance with the NCLT order. As per the Companies (Compromise, Arrangement and Amalgamation) Rules, 2016, approval requires at least ¾ in value of creditors/members present and voting (in person or by proxy) who vote in favour of the scheme. The chairperson must prepare a report of the meeting's outcome.

Step 5 — Sanction by NCLT:
After the meeting, the company applies to the NCLT for sanction. The NCLT examines:
- Whether statutory requirements have been complied with.
- Whether the scheme is fair, reasonable and not contrary to public policy.
- Whether all stakeholders have received full disclosure.
- Whether the scheme is legally valid.
- Whether dissenting creditors/members have been treated fairly.

The NCLT may impose conditions it deems fit before sanctioning the scheme. Upon sanction, the order is binding on all creditors/members, including dissenters.

Section 231 provides for a special provision when a scheme involves the transfer of undertaking, property, assets, liabilities, etc., to another company — it allows the NCLT to make an order giving effect to the transfer without requiring compliance with certain formalities if the conditions specified are met.

Section 232 deals with provisions for safeguarding interests of shareholders/creditors in case of scheme. It empowers the NCLT to call for report from the Registrar or SEBI and to pass such orders as it deems fit. It also requires that the scheme must not result in prejudice to the interests of shareholders/creditors.

3. Amalgamation and Demerger — Detailed Provisions and Tax/Regulatory Consequences

Amalgamation (Section 2(1B)) requires that the undertaking of the transferor company be transferred to the transferee company. The essential elements are:

- There must be a transfer of the entire undertaking or part thereof.
- Property, assets, rights, liabilities must vest in the transferee company.
- The consideration is usually shares, debentures, cash or a combination.
- The scheme requires NCLT approval.

In an amalgamation in the nature of merger, the conditions under Section 13(1)(b) of the Income Tax Act require that:
- All properties and liabilities of the transferor must be transferred to the transferee.
- Shareholders holding not less than 90% in value of shares of the transferor (excluding shares already held by the transferee) must become shareholders of the transferee by virtue of the scheme.
- The business of the transferor must be carried on by the transferee.
- No cash payment shall be made to shareholders except in adjustment of fractional shares.

Demerger (Section 2(19A)) has specific statutory requirements:
- The undertaking of the transferor company is transferred to the transferee company.
- The ownership of shares of the transferee is transferred to the shareholders of the transferor company.
- The proportion of shareholding of shareholders in the transferee must be identical to their proportion in the transferor company immediately before the demerger.
- The transfer of shares must be proportionate to the value of net assets transferred.
- If shares are not issued proportionately, the value transferred must be adjusted.
- Tax exemptions are available only when the conditions under Section 2(19A)(b) and Section 13 of the IT Act are satisfied.

Important distinctions:
- In amalgamation, the transferor company may or may not get dissolved — in a merger, dissolution is mandatory; in a subsidiary merger, the subsidiary survives.
- In demerger, the shareholders of the transferor receive shares in the transferee proportionately.
- Neither amalgamation nor demerger requires shareholder approval separately under the Companies Act since it is effected through the scheme mechanism, but the explanatory statement must detail the impact.

Regulatory filings post-scheme approval include filing the NCLT order with the Registrar within 30 days, alteration of memorandum/articles if required, and compliance with SEBI LODR for listed entities.

4. Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) — Framework under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016, established a time-bound insolvency resolution framework with the objective of maximizing the value of assets of the corporate debtor, promoting entrepreneurship, increasing availability of credit, and balancing the interests of all stakeholders.

Key institutions under IBC:
- Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI): Regulator overseeing the process.
- Insolvency Professionals (IPs): Licensed professionals who manage the insolvency process.
- Information Utilities (IUs): Maintain records of financial information and corroborate default.
- Adjudicating Authorities: NCLT for corporate persons and NCLAT for appeals.

Threshold and initiation:
- A corporate debtor is anyone incorporated under the Companies Act carrying on business.
- A default means failure to pay operational debt or financial debt. Minimum threshold for financial debt is Rs. 1 lakh (as amended from time to time).
- The creditor (financial, operational or corporate) may initiate CIRP by filing an application under Section 7 (financial creditor), Section 9 (operational creditor) or Section 10 (corporate debtor/members) with the NCLT.
- On admission, a moratorium under Section 14 comes into force, prohibiting any suits, executions, or proceedings against the corporate debtor.

Composition of Committee of Creditors (CoC):
- Section 21 requires constitution of the CoC comprising all financial creditors of the corporate debtor. The CoC elects an interim Resolution Professional (RP) who continues as RP if appointed within 30 days.
- Voting share of each financial creditor is calculated as the proportion of the debt owed to them out of total assessed financial debt.

Resolution Professional's role:
- Takes over management of the corporate debtor's affairs.
- Publishes an invitation for expressions of interest.
- Evaluates resolution plans and submits to the CoC.

Timeframe:
- CIRP must be completed within 180 days from the admission date, extendable by up to 90 days (total maximum 270 days), subject to extension by vote of 66% of CoC members.

Resolution Plan:
- Must be approved by at least 66% of voting share of CoC members.
- Once approved by NCLT under Section 31, it is binding on all stakeholders including employees, government authorities, and dissenting creditors.
- Priority of payment under Section 53 applies in liquidation but the resolution plan may provide a different waterfall subject to CoC approval and NCLT sanction.

Grounds for rejection of resolution plan by NCLT include: non-compliance with IBC/Companies Act/others, fraud, unenforceability, or not satisfying criteria under Section 30(2).

5. Liquidation Process under the IBC — Grounds, Procedure and Waterfall Mechanism

Liquidation is initiated when no resolution plan is approved, a sanctioned plan is not implemented, or the corporate debtor is found guilty of fraud, among other grounds specified in Section 33.

Section 33 — Circumstances for Liquidation:
- The NCLT rejects the resolution plan and no further plan is submitted within 90 days of rejection.
- The resolution plan approved by NCLT is not implemented by the resolution applicant within the stipulated time.
- The resolution process is terminated due to obstruction by the corporate debtor.
- The corporate debtor is found guilty of fraud under Section 66 (during the resolution period, a finding of fraud mandates liquidation).

Section 34 — Appointment of Liquidator:
The NCLT orders liquidation and appoints a liquidator from a panel maintained by IBBI. The liquidator takes custody/control of all assets of the corporate debtor.

Section 35 — Functions of Liquidator:
- Determine the company's affairs through verification of claims.
- Sell the corporate debtor's assets as a going concern or otherwise.
- Distribute proceeds in accordance with Section 53.
- Apply to NCLT for closure of liquidation proceedings.

Section 36 — Moratorium during Liquidation:
No suits or proceedings can be instituted against the corporate debtor once the liquidation order is passed.

Section 53 — Waterfall Mechanism (Priority of Payment):
The proceeds from liquidation are distributed in the following order of priority:

1st: Insolvency resolution process costs and liquidation costs (paid first in full).
2nd: Workmen's dues for a period of 24 months preceding liquidation and debts due to secured creditors who have relinquished security in the interim period.
3rd: Employees' wages and salaries for a period of 12 months preceding liquidation.
4th: Financial debts owed to unsecured creditors.
5th: Government dues (central/state) for the last 2 years.
6th: Any remaining dues to secured creditors (if security was realized). Any balance after 1st priority goes to Secured Creditors.
7th: Any remaining amount to equity shareholders/partners.

Secured creditors may either realize security independently or relinquish it to participate in the distribution according to Section 53.

Termination of liquidation (Section 59): The liquidator applies to NCLT for an order of termination when the assets are exhausted or when all distributions are made. The company is then dissolved.

6. Winding-up under the Companies Act, 2013 — Modes, Grounds and Procedures

Winding-up (also called liquidation under the Companies Act) means the process of bringing a company's life to an end and distributing its assets to its stakeholders. It is governed by Chapter XXI of the Companies Act, 2013 (Sections 270-324) and is distinct from, though overlapping with, IBC liquidation.

Modes of Winding-up:

(a) Voluntary Winding-up (Section 270):
- Members' Voluntary Winding: Declared by a declaration of solvency. Directors make a sworn affidavit stating the company can pay all debts in full within a specified period (not exceeding 3 years). Requires special resolution.
- Creditors' Voluntary Winding: Where the company cannot pay its debts in full. A creditors' meeting is convened, and the winding-up is conducted with creditors' supervision.
- Both are supervised by the NCLT and a liquidator is appointed.

(b) Winding-up by the Tribunal (Compulsory Winding-up, Section 271):
The NCLT may wind up a company on grounds including:
- Special resolution by the company that it be wound up by the Tribunal.
- Where the company has subjected itself to an enterprise insolvency resolution process under the IBC.
- Default in delivering statutory report or holding statutory meeting.
- Reduction of membership below statutory minimum (2 for public, 2 for private).
- Where the company is unable to pay its debts.
- Where the Tribunal is of opinion that it is just and equitable to wind up.

(c) Winding-up subject to supervision of the Court (Section 442, though largely obsolete now).

Grounds for being unable to pay debts (Section 43):
- Creditor to whom the company owes more than Rs. 1 lakh has served a demand and the company has failed to pay/secure/compound within 3 weeks.
- Execution or other process issued on a decree/judgment remains unsatisfied.
- Proof of inability to pay debts to the satisfaction of the Tribunal.

Consequences of Winding-up:
- On appointment of a liquidator, the management vests in the liquidator (Section 448).
- No alterations can be made in the articles without Tribunal permission (Section 449).
- Avoidance of transfers, dispositions and alienations after commencement of winding up (Sections 431-433).
- Dissolution follows completion of winding-up.

Effect of IBC over winding-up: Under Section 35 and Schedule VII of IBC, once CIRP is initiated under the Code, the provisions of the Companies Act relating to voluntary winding up do not apply.

7. Comparison of Schemes under Companies Act and IBC — Overlap and Jurisdictional Clarity

A critical area for professional practice is understanding how the scheme route under Sections 230-232 of the Companies Act interacts with the IBC framework. The judiciary has addressed this overlap through several landmark judgments.

Key principles:

- When a company is under active CIRP, a scheme under Sections 230-232 cannot be proposed independently because the resolution professional controls the company's affairs. However, the resolution plan itself functions as a scheme.

- A company in liquidation cannot use the scheme route; the IBC provides a self-contained code for liquidation.

- If a scheme is pending before the NCLT at the time CIRP is initiated, the NCLT may abate or stay the scheme proceeding in favour of the IBC process, as clarified in the Supreme Court judgment in Rajesh Garg v. Shyam Metal and Alloys Ltd. and subsequent decisions.

- An amicable settlement between parties cannot override the statutory scheme of the IBC; even consent settlements must conform to IBC procedures.

- The NCLT retains discretion to allow a scheme under Section 230-232 alongside the IBC in limited circumstances where the CIRP is not actively underway, but this is exceptional.

Difference between a resolution plan and a scheme:
- A resolution plan is submitted by a resolution applicant and approved by the CoC with 66% majority; it binds all stakeholders regardless of individual consent.
- A scheme under Sections 230-232 is approved by ¾ majority of creditors/members in each class and then by NCLT; it is more consensual and allows greater freedom in structuring the compromise.

Tax and regulatory clearances: Under IBC, clearance from tax authorities and regulatory bodies (like SEBI, RBI) must be sought before approving a resolution plan. Under the Companies Act scheme route, consent of relevant authorities is obtained at the NCLT stage.

8. Fraudulent Trading and Personal Liability — Provisions under IBC and Companies Act

Both the IBC and the Companies Act contain provisions penalizing fraudulent trading and enabling personal liability against directors/officers in insolvency scenarios.

Under IBC — Section 66 (Fraudulent Trading):
If, during the course of CIRP, it appears that any business of the corporate debtor has been carried on with intent to defraud creditors of the corporate debtor or creditors of any other person, or for any fraudulent purpose, the NCLT, on application by the IP or liquidator, may declare that any persons who were knowingly parties to the carrying on of the business in the manner above shall be personally responsible, without any limitation of liability, for all or any of the debts of the corporate debtor.

Elements of Section 66:
- Business must have been carried on with intent to defraud.
- Knowledge and participation of the person must be established.
- Liability is unlimited and personal.

Under Companies Act — Section 447 (Fraud):
Fraud is defined broadly and includes any act, omission, concealment of any fact or abuse of position committed by any person or any other person with the connivance in any connection with the company, with intent to deceive or to gain advantage. Penalties include imprisonment (6 months to 10 years) and fine commensurate with the fraud amount (up to three times the amount involved).

Section 448 (False Statements):
Providing false information to obtain condonation of default or approval of scheme attracts similar penalties.

Other relevant provisions:
- Section 244 of Companies Act: Piercing the corporate veil in cases of fraud.
- Section 451: Property of the company vesting in the liquidator.
- Section 246: Investigation of affairs of a company.
- Provision of preferential and undervalued transactions (Section 439 of Companies Act and Section 43-45 of IBC) allowing the liquidator/RP to challenge transactions entered into before insolvency to restore value to the estate.

Preference transactions under IBC (Section 44): Transactions entered into within 2 years (related party) or 1 year (other) before the commencement of insolvency, which put the counterparty in a more favourable position than liquidation would, are voidable.

9. Cross-Border Insolvency — Status in India and Regulatory Considerations

India currently does not have a fully enacted cross-border insolvency regime comparable to the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency, 2004. However, several developments are significant:

- The IBC contains no specific provisions addressing cross-border insolvency, creating a gap for companies with international operations or assets abroad.

- The RBI has issued master directions on foreign exchange matters applicable to distressed cross-border transactions, and FEMA regulations govern outward remittances and overseas investments.

- Bilateral cooperation: India has signed Memoranda of Understanding with jurisdictions like Singapore, UK, and others for cooperation in insolvency matters, though these are facilitative rather than binding.

- NCLT has, in certain cases, shown willingness to recognize foreign proceedings comity-based, particularly where the foreign proceeding is under a developed insolvency regime.

- Proposed legislation: The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) has recommended and drafts for a cross-border insolvency regulation have been circulated, mirroring the UNCITRAL Model Law's approach of granting relief to foreign representative and coordinating proceedings.

Practical issues faced by Company Secretaries:
- Determining the centre of main interests (COMI) of a corporate debtor with multi-jurisdictional presence.
- Recognition of insolvency proceedings commenced abroad and enforcement of foreign orders in India.
- Asset recovery across borders and coordination with foreign liquidators/administrators.
- Double recovery risk — ensuring a creditor does not recover from both Indian and foreign proceedings.

For CS examination purposes, candidates should note the absence of a formal cross-border insolvency law in India and the reliance on FEMA, RBI guidelines, and common law principles of comity.

10. Practical Considerations for Company Secretaries — Compliance, Filings and Ethical Duties

A Company Secretary plays a pivotal role in every restructuring, insolvency, or winding-up proceeding. Key responsibilities include:

Pre-proceeding compliance:
- Verify whether the proposed scheme/restructuring requires CCI approval under the Competition Act (combination notification thresholds).
- Assess tax implications under the Income Tax Act and ensure appropriate disclosures.
- Check SEBI LODR and SAST compliance for listed companies.
- Ensure accuracy of financial statements and auditors' reports.
- Verify that board and member meetings are duly convened and resolutions validly passed.

During CIRP:
- Facilitate communication between the resolution professional and the company.
- Ensure proper maintenance of records, minutes and statutory registers.
- Assist in preparation of information memorandum and responses to resolution applicants.
- Monitor compliance with NCLT directions.

Post-approval compliance:
- File the NCLT order with the Registrar within 30 days (Section 39 of Companies Act).
- Update the memorandum and articles if altered by the scheme.
- Intimate banks, registries, and regulators as required.
- Handle employee-related compliances during restructuring.

Ethical duties:
- Maintain confidentiality of commercially sensitive information received during the process.
- Avoid conflicts of interest, especially if acting for multiple stakeholders with competing interests.
- Ensure transparency and full disclosure to all creditors and members.
- Act in the best interests of the company as a whole while protecting weaker stakeholders, particularly workers and small creditors.

Common errors to avoid:
- Missing the 30-day filing deadline for NCLT orders.
- Inadequate classification of creditors in scheme meetings leading to challenging the order.
- Failure to obtain requisite clearances (tax, sectoral regulator) before sanction.
- Incorrect calculation of voting share in CoC or scheme meeting.
- Non-compliance with the waterfall mechanism during liquidation.

Important facts

  • Amalgamation is defined under Section 2(1B) of the Companies Act, 2013 as the fusion of enterprises involving transfer of undertaking, property, assets, liabilities or any one or more of them.
  • Demerger under Section 2(19A) requires that the shareholding of shareholders in the transferee company be exactly proportionate to their shareholding in the transferor company.
  • Schemes under Sections 230-232 require ¾ majority by value of creditors/members present and voting, followed by NCLT sanction.
  • CIRP timeframe under IBC is 180 days, extendable by 90 days (maximum 270 days), with a possible extension beyond 270 days only through judicial intervention in rare cases.
  • Moratorium under Section 14 of IBC prohibits all suits, executions and proceedings against the corporate debtor from the date of admission of CIRP.
  • Committee of Creditors (CoC) approves a resolution plan by 66% of voting share of financial creditors.
  • Section 66 of IBC provides for personal liability without limitation for persons knowingly parties to fraudulent trading during CIRP.
  • Waterfall mechanism under Section 53 of IBC gives first priority to insolvency resolution process costs and liquidation costs, followed by workmen's dues (24 months), employees' dues (12 months), unsecured financial debts, government dues (2 years), and finally equity shareholders.
  • Winding-up by the Tribunal under Section 271 of the Companies Act can be ordered on grounds including inability to pay debts, just and equitable ground, and special resolution.
  • Declaration of solvency is mandatory for members' voluntary winding-up; without it, the winding-up defaults to creditors' voluntary winding-up.
  • Section 447 of the Companies Act defines fraud and prescribes imprisonment of 6 months to 10 years and fine up to three times the amount involved.
  • When CIRP is admitted under IBC, voluntary winding-up provisions under the Companies Act are superseded by the IBC scheme.
  • India currently lacks a formal cross-border insolvency law; existing gaps are filled by FEMA, RBI guidelines and common law comity principles.
  • CCI approval under the Competition Act, 2002 is required for amalgamations and acquisitions crossing combination thresholds.
  • NCLT order approving a scheme must be filed with the Registrar within 30 days under Section 39 of the Companies Act.
