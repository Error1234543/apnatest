Environmental, Social and Governance Principles and Practice — Complete Study Notes

CSEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to ESG: Concept, Evolution and Significance

ESG stands for Environmental, Social and Governance — three broad categories of factors used to measure the sustainability and ethical impact of an investment in a company or business. The ESG framework emerged as a response to the limitations of traditional financial analysis, which often ignored non-financial risks and long-term value creation drivers.

The concept gained traction in the early 2000s following the publication of the first UN Global Compact principles and the Greenhouse Gas Protocol. In 2004, the former UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan called for the development of an integrated sustainability reporting framework. The term 'ESG' was formally coined in a UNEP-initiated report titled 'Who Cares Wins' (2004), which argued that incorporating ESG factors into financial analysis leads to better investment decisions.

The significance of ESG lies in its ability to capture risks and opportunities that conventional financial metrics miss. Environmentally, companies face climate-related liabilities, regulatory penalties for pollution, and resource scarcity risks. Socially, poor labor practices can lead to reputational damage, strikes, and legal action. Governance failures — such as weak board oversight or lack of transparency — have historically been root causes of corporate scandals and financial crises.

For Company Secretaries, ESG competence is essential because they serve as compliance officers, governance advisors, and corporate secretary — roles increasingly tied to ESG disclosure obligations, board-level sustainability committees, and stakeholder communication.

2. The Environmental Pillar: Key Issues, Metrics and Regulatory Landscape

The Environmental pillar examines how a company performs as a steward of the natural environment. Key areas of assessment include:

- Climate Change and Greenhouse Gas Emissions: Companies are evaluated on their carbon footprint (Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions under the GHG Protocol), climate risk exposure, and transition strategies toward net-zero. The science-based targets initiative (SBTi) provides a framework for setting emissions-reduction targets consistent with climate science.

- Energy Management: Efficiency measures, renewable energy adoption, and energy sourcing mix are critical metrics. Companies using significant energy in operations face both cost risks and regulatory compliance obligations.

- Water and Waste Management: Water stress in operational regions, wastewater treatment practices, circular economy approaches, and hazardous waste handling are assessed. Water scarcity poses physical risks to operations in certain geographies.

- Pollution and Environmental Degradation: Air emissions, toxic discharge, deforestation, biodiversity impact, and soil contamination are tracked. Regulatory fines and remediation costs represent material financial risks.

- Natural Resource Use: Sustainable sourcing of raw materials, land use practices, and supply chain environmental impact are evaluated, especially for resource-intensive industries.

In India, the environmental regulatory landscape includes the Environment Protection Act, 1986; the Climate Change Action Plan; the Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT) scheme for energy efficiency; and SEBI's mandate for top 1,000 listed entities to file Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reports (BRSR). Internationally, the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) framework has become a global reference for climate risk reporting.

3. The Social Pillar: Labor Practices, Human Rights and Community Engagement

The Social pillar evaluates how a company manages relationships with employees, suppliers, customers, and communities. Key areas include:

- Labor Practices and Decent Work: This covers fair wages, working hours, freedom of association, collective bargaining rights, elimination of forced and child labor, and occupational health and safety. The International Labour Organization (ILO) core conventions provide the international benchmark.

- Diversity, Equity and Inclusion (DEI): Representation of women and underrepresented groups at board and management levels, pay equity, inclusive workplace policies, and anti-discrimination measures are assessed. The 'Glass Ceiling Index' and gender pay gap are common metrics.

- Human Rights: Respect for human rights throughout operations and supply chains, including adherence to the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs), due diligence processes, and remediation mechanisms for violations.

- Product Responsibility and Consumer Welfare: Product safety, data privacy and cybersecurity, responsible marketing, accessibility, and customer complaint resolution are evaluated. The GDPR (EU) and DPDP Act, 2023 (India) are relevant data protection frameworks.

- Community Engagement and Social Impact: Community investment, local employment, indigenous rights, and social license to operate are assessed. Companies must demonstrate meaningful engagement with affected communities, not merely philanthropic donations.

- Supply Chain Social Responsibility: Modern slavery legislation, supplier code of conduct enforcement, and traceability of labor conditions in global supply chains are increasingly scrutinized. The UK Modern Slavery Act and California Transparency in Supply Chains Act are pioneering examples.

4. The Governance Pillar: Board Structure, Ethics, Transparency and Accountability

Governance is often considered the most actionable pillar because it directly reflects management quality and corporate integrity. Key governance dimensions include:

- Board Composition and Independence: The proportion of independent directors, diversity of the board, separation of CEO and Chair roles, and presence of specialized committees (audit, remuneration, nomination, sustainability/ESG committee) are evaluated. Good governance requires boards capable of effective oversight and challenge.

- Executive Compensation: Linkage of executive pay to ESG performance metrics, say-on-pay provisions, transparency of compensation structures, and alignment with long-term shareholder interests. Excessive or poorly structured compensation linked to short-term targets is a red flag.

- Shareholder Rights and Engagement: Protection of minority shareholders, voting rights, transparency in related-party transactions, and mechanisms for shareholder proposals and engagement. Coordinated investor initiatives (e.g., Climate Action 100+) exert significant governance pressure.

- Business Ethics and Anti-Corruption: Anti-bribery and anti-corruption policies, Whistleblower protection mechanisms, lobbying and political contribution transparency, and compliance with laws like the UK Bribery Act and India's Prevention of Corruption Act.

- Tax Transparency: Public country-by-country reporting, tax avoidance strategies, and alignment between effective tax rates and economic substance. The BEPS (Base Erosion and Profit Shifting) framework by OECD addresses aggressive tax planning.

- Data Privacy and Cybersecurity Governance: Board-level oversight of data protection, incident response protocols, and compliance with data privacy regulations.

- ESG Integration in Strategy: Whether ESG factors are embedded in corporate strategy, risk management, and capital allocation decisions — moving beyond siloed CSR to integrated value creation.

5. Major ESG Frameworks, Standards and Rating Agencies

A pluralistic ecosystem of frameworks, standards, and rating agencies shapes ESG practice. Understanding their differences is essential for compliance and strategic ESG management.

GLOBAL REPORTING INITIATIVE (GRI):
The most widely adopted sustainability reporting framework globally. GRI Standards cover economic, environmental, and social topics across three series: Universal Standards (GRI 1, 2, 3), Sector Standards, and Topic-Specific Standards (e.g., GRI 300 series on environmental topics). GRI emphasizes stakeholder inclusiveness and materiality.

SUSTAINABILITY ACCOUNTING STANDARDS BOARD (SASB):
Focuses on financially material ESG issues specific to 77 industry sectors. SASB standards help companies disclose information that investors need to assess risk and return. SASB standards have been merged into the IFRS Foundation's ISSB.

INTERNATIONAL SUSTAINABILITY STANDARDS BOARD (ISSB):
Established by the IFRS Foundation in 2021, ISSB issues IFRS S1 (general sustainability-related disclosures) and IFRS S2 (climate-related disclosures). These converge TCFD recommendations into a global baseline for sustainability disclosures.

TASK FORCE ON CLIMATE-related FINANCIAL DISCLOSURES (TCFD):
Recommends four thematic areas for disclosure: Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, and Metrics and Targets. The TCFD framework has been endorsed by over 1,500 organizations and is being incorporated into mandatory disclosure regimes worldwide.

CDP (formerly Carbon Disclosure Project):
A global environmental disclosure system where companies report climate change, water security, and forest conservation impacts. Often used by investors as a scoring mechanism.

UN GLOBAL COMPACT:
A voluntary initiative based on ten principles in the areas of human rights, labor, environment, and anti-corruption. Companies report progress through Communication on Progress (COP).

BRRS (BUSINESS RESPONSIBILITY AND SUSTAINABILITY REPORTING) — INDIA:
Mandated by SEBI for top 1,000 listed entities, BRSR follows a principles-based approach aligned with GRI. It includes three parts: Part A (organizational details), Part B (principles performance), and Part C (disclosure indicators). The BRSR Core requires assured data for select disclosures.

RATING AGENCIES:
MSCI ESG Ratings, Sustainalytics, FTSE Russell, and CDP assess companies using proprietary methodologies. These ratings influence institutional investment decisions and asset allocation. Investors increasingly treat ESG ratings as material inputs for portfolio construction.

6. ESG Materiality Assessment and Double Materiality

Materiality is the cornerstone of ESG reporting and strategy. An ESG matter is material if it could substantially impact a company's financial performance, enterprise value, or ability to create value over time.

FINANCIAL MATERIALITY (OUTSIDE-IN):
Assesses how ESG factors affect the company's financial position — 'how sustainability issues impact the business.' This perspective dominates traditional ESG investing and SASB standards. For example, water scarcity in a beverage company's operating region represents a financially material risk.

IMPACT MATERIALITY (INSIDE-OUT):
Assesses how the company's activities impact the environment and society — 'how the business impacts the world.' This is central to GRI's approach and European regulatory developments.

DOUBLE MATERIALITY:
Combines both perspectives. A company must disclose issues that are material from both the financial perspective and the impact perspective. The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) mandates double materiality, making it the first major jurisdiction to require it legally. For Indian companies with European operations or investors, understanding double materiality is increasingly important.

MATERIALITY ASSESSMENT PROCESS:
1. Identify a comprehensive list of potential ESG topics (typically 20-40 topics spanning environmental, social, and governance themes)
2. Survey internal and external stakeholders to rate importance (financial impact and impact magnitude)
3. Plot results on a materiality matrix (impact on business vs. impact on stakeholders)
4. Prioritize topics falling in the high-materiality quadrant
5. Validate findings with board/senior management
6. Review and update annually or when significant changes occur

The process should be documented, repeatable, and involve cross-functional input from legal, compliance, operations, finance, and sustainability teams.

7. ESG Integration in Corporate Governance: Role of the Board and Company Secretary

Effective ESG management requires board-level ownership and active Company Secretary involvement. The evolving role of the Company Secretary encompasses several critical functions:

BOARD OVERSIGHT OF ESG:
- The board must establish clear ESG governance structures, including a dedicated sustainability/ESG committee or assign ESG oversight to an existing committee (audit, nomination, or a standalone committee)
- Board members need ESG literacy — understanding key frameworks, regulatory developments, and sector-specific risks
- ESG performance should be integrated into risk management frameworks and connected to strategic planning and capital allocation
- The board should set ESG targets, monitor progress, and ensure adequate resource allocation

ROLE OF THE COMPANY SECRETARY:
- Advising the board on ESG governance best practices and regulatory obligations
- Facilitating ESG reporting processes and ensuring compliance with BRSR, Companies Act CSR provisions, and other disclosure requirements
- Coordinating with internal stakeholders (departments, subsidiaries) and external stakeholders (auditors, rating agencies, regulators)
- Managing board and committee meetings on ESG matters, including agenda-setting and minute-taking
- Ensuring proper documentation of ESG decisions and maintaining audit trails for compliance purposes
- Acting as a bridge between the board, management, and stakeholders on ESG issues
- Monitoring evolving ESG regulations domestically and internationally
- Supporting ESG due diligence in M&A, joint ventures, and partnerships

SHAREHOLDER ENGAGEMENT ON ESG:
Company Secretaries facilitate investor relations around ESG matters, including responding to investor ESG questionnaires (CDP, EcoVadis), participating in investor days focused on sustainability, and managing proxy voting on ESG-related resolutions.

8. ESG in the Indian Context: SEBI BRSR, Companies Act CSR, and Related Regulations

India has developed a robust ESG regulatory framework with unique characteristics reflecting its developmental context and governance tradition.

SEBI BRSR MANDATE:
- Applicable to the top 1,000 listed entities (by market capitalization)
- Requires annual reporting on Business Responsibility and Sustainability Performance
- Part A covers organizational profile and governance
- Part B covers the nine Business Responsibility Principles (BRPs) with qualitative disclosures
- Part C contains quantitative disclosure indicators organized by environmental, social, and governance themes
- BRSR Core requires limited assurance for specified high-impact disclosures, with full assurance progressing over time
- Aligns with GRI Standards and incorporates TCFD recommendations

COMPANIES ACT, 2013 — CORPORATE SOCIAL RESPONSIBILITY (CSR):
- Section 135 mandates CSR spending for companies meeting specified thresholds (net worth ₹500 crore or more; turnover ₹1,000 crore or more; or net profit ₹5 crore or more)
- Companies must spend at least 2% of average net profits of the preceding three years on CSR activities
- CSR must cover Schedule VII activities (e.g., education, healthcare, environmental sustainability, rural development)
- The CSR Committee of the board comprises three or more directors, including at least one independent director
- Unspent CSR amounts must be transferred to a specified fund within 30 days of the financial year-end (unless the project is in progress)

OTHER RELEVANT INDIAN REGULATIONS:
- The National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC) issued by MCA, aligning with OECD guidelines and UNGP principles
- The Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) regulations for large energy consumers
- The Wildlife (Protection) Act, Forest (Conservation) Act, and Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act relevant for environmental compliance
- The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act and POSH Act for social compliance
- The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 for data governance

9. ESG Risks, Due Diligence, and Supply Chain Management

ESG risks manifest differently across industries and geographies but share common patterns of financial, reputational, and legal consequences.

IDENTIFICATION OF ESG RISKS:
- Physical risks: Direct impacts from climate change (flooding, drought, extreme weather) on assets and operations
- Transition risks: Policy, legal, technology, and market shifts associated with moving to a lower-carbon economy (carbon pricing, stranded assets, changing consumer preferences)
- Liability risks: Lawsuits related to environmental damage, labor violations, discrimination, or product harm
- Reputational risks: Brand damage from ESG controversies affecting customer loyalty, talent attraction, and investor confidence
- Supply chain risks: ESG failures by suppliers (forced labor, environmental degradation) creating cascading reputational and compliance exposure

ESG DUE DILIGENCE:
- Pre-investment/transaction: Assessing target company's ESG risks, compliance gaps, and potential liabilities through ESG audits and rating reviews
- Ongoing monitoring: Tracking ESG performance of suppliers and business partners using questionnaires, site audits, and third-party assessments
- Continuous improvement: Working with suppliers to address identified gaps and build capacity

SUPPLY CHAIN ESG MANAGEMENT:
- Establishing a supplier code of conduct covering labor, environment, ethics, and health & safety
- Conducting risk-based supplier assessments (high-risk suppliers receive more frequent and deeper audits)
- Building traceability, especially for commodities linked to deforestation, conflict minerals, or forced labor
- Supporting supplier capacity building rather than merely terminating relationships
- Leveraging technology (blockchain, IoT) for supply chain visibility

The EU's Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) and Germany's Supply Chain Due Diligence Act represent the leading regulatory approaches to supply chain ESG obligations, with implications for Indian exporters.

10. ESG Reporting, Assurance, and Emerging Trends

High-quality ESG reporting is essential for credibility, investor confidence, and regulatory compliance.

REPORTING BEST PRACTICES:
- Align reports with recognized frameworks (GRI, SASB/ISSB, TCFD) and disclose framework correspondence explicitly
- Apply double materiality to determine report scope and content
- Use quantitative, comparable data alongside qualitative narrative
- Ensure data consistency year-over-year and clearly explain any methodological changes
- Include forward-looking information (targets, roadmaps) alongside historical performance
- Address negative performance honestly — omitting or selectively disclosing poor results undermines credibility
- Make reports accessible (digital format, plain language summaries, multilingual versions where relevant)

ASSURANCE OF ESG REPORTS:
- Limited assurance provides reasonable but lower confidence; reasonable assurance offers higher confidence akin to financial statement audits
- BRSR Core requires limited assurance for specified indicators, progressing toward reasonable assurance
- The International Audit and Assurance Standards Board (IAASB) has issued ISAE 3000 (revised) for assurance engagements on sustainability information
- Indian Chartered Accountants provide ESG assurance under the ICAI's guidance
- Assurance scope, criteria, and qualifications should be clearly disclosed

EMERGING TRENDS:
- Mandatory ESG disclosure: Over 30 jurisdictions have moved toward or implemented mandatory sustainability reporting
- ESRS (European Sustainability Reporting Standards): Under CSRD, these provide detailed topic-specific requirements
- ISSB adoption: Countries are increasingly adopting or converging with IFRS S1 and S2 as their national baseline
- Scope 3 emissions: Growing pressure to disclose and reduce indirect value chain emissions
- Nature and biodiversity: The Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD) framework emerged in 2023, parallel to TCFD for climate
- Greenwashing regulation: Increasing enforcement actions against misleading ESG claims; the EU's Greenwashing Directive and SEC climate disclosure rules in the US are notable developments
- AI and digital tools: Technology enabling real-time ESG data collection, analytics, and reporting automation
- Just transition: Focus on ensuring ESG transitions do not disproportionately harm workers and vulnerable communities
