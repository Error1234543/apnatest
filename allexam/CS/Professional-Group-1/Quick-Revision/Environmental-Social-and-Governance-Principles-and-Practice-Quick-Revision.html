ESG Principles and Practice — Quick Revision (CS Professional)

CSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • ESG (Environmental, Social and Governance) refers to the three central factors used in measuring the sustainability and ethical impact of an investment in a company, used by investors to screen and select investments.
  • ESG Principles are the guiding framework that integrates environmental stewardship, social responsibility, and sound governance into corporate strategy, operations, and disclosures.
  • ESG Reporting is the systematic disclosure of a company's performance against environmental, social, and governance criteria, enabling stakeholders to assess non-financial risks and impacts.
  • BRSR (Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report) is the mandatory ESG reporting framework prescribed by SEBI for top listed entities under Regulation 34(2)(f) read with SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015.
  • TCFD (Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures) is a framework for disclosing climate-related financial risks and opportunities across governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics/targets.
  • SASB (Sustainability Accounting Standards Board) provides industry-specific sustainability disclosure standards focused on financially material ESG issues for investors.
  • GRI (Global Reporting Initiative) is the most widely adopted independent international organization that sets comprehensive sustainability reporting standards covering economic, environmental, and social dimensions.
  • UN PRI (United Nations Principles for Responsible Investment) is a globally recognized investor initiative providing a six-principle framework for incorporating ESG into investment practice and ownership.
  • Carbon Neutrality means achieving net-zero carbon emissions by balancing emitted carbon with an equivalent amount removed or offset.
  • Materiality in ESG refers to information that could substantively affect a company's financial condition, operating performance, or stakeholder assessments; double materiality considers both financial impact on the company and the company's impact on society/environment.

Key concepts

  • ESG extends traditional financial analysis by incorporating non-financial risk and value drivers. Investors and regulators use ESG to evaluate long-term resilience, reputation risk, regulatory exposure, and cost of capital. A strong ESG posture can reduce capital costs, improve operational efficiency, attract institutional investors, and enhance brand value. For a Company Secretary, ESG knowledge is essential because it directly intersects with compliance, board governance, stakeholder communication, and fiduciary duties under the Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI LODR.
  • Environmental ESG covers: (1) Climate Change — GHG emissions (Scope 1 direct, Scope 2 indirect from purchased energy, Scope 3 other indirect value chain emissions), carbon intensity, transition risk, and physical risk from extreme weather. (2) Energy Management — renewable energy adoption, energy efficiency targets, carbon pricing exposure. (3) Pollution & Waste — air emissions, wastewater discharge, solid and hazardous waste management, circular economy adoption. (4) Water Stress — water usage efficiency, basin-level water risk, discharge quality. (5) Biodiversity & Land Use — deforestation risk, ecosystem impact, sustainable sourcing. (6) Environmental Compliance — adherence to CPCB/SPCB norms, EIA compliance, penalties and litigation history.
  • Social ESG covers: (1) Labor Practices — fair wages, working hours, non-discrimination, freedom of association, gig/workforce vulnerability. (2) Human Rights — supply chain due diligence, child labor prohibition, forced labor elimination, indigenous rights. (3) Occupational Health & Safety — accident rates, safety training, zero-harm initiatives. (4) Diversity, Equity & Inclusion — gender representation at board and management level, pay equity, minority representation. (5) Community Engagement — CSR spending alignment with Schedule VII, community impact assessment, conflict resolution. (6) Product Responsibility — data privacy, cybersecurity, product safety, advertising ethics, customer grievance redressal.
  • Governance ESG covers: (1) Board Structure & Independence — proportion of independent directors, diversity on board, board evaluation, committee effectiveness (Audit, Nomination & Remuneration, CSR, Stakeholders' Relationship). (2) Ethics & Conduct — code of conduct, whistle-blower protection under Section 177(9)/184(6), related-party transaction governance, anti-bribery and corruption policies. (3) Shareholder Rights — equitable treatment, voting rights, dividend policy, buyback governance. (4) Transparency & Disclosure — timely and accurate financial reporting, ESG disclosure quality, audit quality. (5) Risk Management & Internal Controls — ERM framework, ESG risk integration, internal audit coverage of ESG areas. (6) Executive Compensation — pay-for-performance linkage, ESG-linked incentive metrics.
  • GRI Standards: Comprehensive multi-impact framework; topics range from general disclosures (GRI 1) to sector-specific and topic-specific (e.g., GRI 205 on anti-corruption, GRI 302 on energy, GRI 303 on water). Primarily stakeholder-inclusive. SASB Standards: Industry-tailored, investor-focused disclosures identifying financially material sustainability topics; useful for comparing peers within the same sector. TCFD Recommendations: Four pillars — Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, Metrics & Targets — focused specifically on climate-related financial disclosures; now incorporated into ISSB's IFRS S2. UN PRI: Six principles for institutional investors covering ESG integration, active ownership, disclosure, promotion, collaboration, and reporting on implementation. ISSB Standards: IFRS S1 (general sustainability disclosures) and IFRS S2 (climate disclosures) set a global baseline; ICSI syllabus requires awareness of their existence and relevance to corporate reporting.
  • SEBI BRSR: Mandatory for top 1000 listed entities (by market capitalization); includes BRSR Core (with auditable/d assured metrics alongside qualitative disclosures) and basic BRSR. Covers 10 Principles aligned with NCFR (National Council for Farmer's Rights) / UNGP. Companies Act, 2013: Section 135 mandates CSR (2% of avg. net profits), Schedule VII defines eligible CSR activities, Section 166(d) requires directors to promote objectives including protecting the environment. National Green Tribunal Act, 1998: Provides for environmental adjudication and compensation. EPIDZ Act, 2023 (Epidemics Diseases): Relevant for pandemic preparedness disclosures. RBI/IRDAI/AMFI: Sectoral ESG/sustainable finance guidelines for banks, insurers, and mutual funds. State Pollution Control Boards: Enforce consent-based emission/water discharge regulations. ICSI examines the intersection of these legal provisions with a Company Secretary's advisory and compliance role.
  • Double materiality has two dimensions: (1) Impact materiality — how the company's operations impact people and the environment. (2) Financial materiality — how ESG issues affect the company's financial position and performance. EU's CSRD adopts double materiality explicitly; India's BRSR also implicitly requires it through its principle-based disclosures on impacts and governance. For exam purposes, a Company Secretary must understand that materiality assessments drive what gets disclosed, and selective greenwashing avoidance requires rigorous, documented materiality analysis rather than cherry-picked positive narratives.
  • ESG must move beyond compliance checklists to influence board strategy. Key integration points: (1) Board oversight — designated board-level ESG/sustainability committee or explicit agenda items at full board meetings. (2) Policy embedding — procurement policies with ESG supplier criteria, investment appraisals with ESG adjustment factors, M&A due diligence including ESG risks. (3) Risk registers — formal inclusion of ESG risks (climate physical risk, reputational risk from labor disputes, governance scandal risk). (4) KPI setting — board-approved ESG targets (e.g., carbon reduction, diversity benchmarks, safety TRIR). (5) External assurance — voluntary or mandatory assurance of ESG data improves credibility. A CS often serves as the bridge between the board, management, and external stakeholders on ESG matters.
  • Greenwashing refers to making misleading or unsubstantiated claims about environmental or social benefits. Indicators include vague language ('eco-friendly'), lack of data, hiding trade-offs (promoting one green attribute while ignoring a larger harm), using unverified third-party certifications, and disproportionate marketing versus actual performance. Mitigation: Ensure all ESG claims are fact-based and measurable; align disclosures with recognized frameworks; obtain limited/reasonable assurance where possible; maintain internal cross-functional review before publication; avoid superlatives unsupported by evidence. For the CS, greenwashing can expose the company to investor litigation, regulatory action, and reputational damage.

Important facts

  • SEBI made BRSR mandatory for the top 1000 listed entities (by market cap) w.e.f. FY 2022-23, with BRSR Core (assurable metrics) phased in from FY 2024-25.
  • BRSR covers 10 Principles (aligned with UNGP) encompassing business integrity, governing ethics, preventing harm to environment, safeguarding workers' rights, promoting underserved communities, ensuring product responsibility, protecting consumer interests, preserving biodiversity, and engaging stakeholders transparently.
  • Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013 requires companies with nil turnover ≥ Rs. 500 crore OR net worth ≥ Rs. 1,000 crore OR net profit ≥ Rs. 5 crore to constitute a CSR Committee and spend at least 2% of average net profits on CSR.
  • Under Companies Act, 2013, every company having one or more independent directors shall have a Whistle Blower mechanism under Section 177(9), and directors shall report violations to the Audit Committee.
  • Scope 1 emissions = direct GHG emissions from owned/controlled sources; Scope 2 = indirect emissions from purchased electricity/steam/heat; Scope 3 = all other indirect emissions in the value chain (often the largest and hardest to measure).
  • The TCFD framework recommends disclosure across four themes: Governance (board oversight of climate risks), Strategy (real and potential impacts on business), Risk Management (processes to identify, assess, and manage climate risks), and Metrics & Targets (GHG emissions and energy use metrics, carbon targets used).

Memory aids

  • ESG pillars mnemonic — "Sea Guard": SEA = Social + Environmental (nature/ocean); GUARD = Governance (protecting the corporation). Recite: "SEA GUARD the future."
  • Three Scopes of Carbon Emissions — "1 Direct, 2 Electricity, 3 Everywhere else" — Scope 1 = your own flames/fuel; Scope 2 = bought power; Scope 3 = everything upstream and downstream.
  • TCFD Four Pillars — "GRIT": Governance, Risk, Impact (Strategy), Targets (Metrics). This anchors the framework in an easy word.
  • CSR threshold reminder — "500-1000-5" = Turnover 500 cr, Net Worth 1000 cr, Profit 5 cr — any one triggers Section 135 obligation.
  • BRSR Structure — "Basic → Core": Basic covers qualitative 10 Principles; Core adds quantitative, auditable metrics. Think of Core as the "hard data" layer built on the "soft narrative" base.

Exam tips

  • For CS Professional descriptive answers, always anchor ESG discussion in Indian legal/regulatory provisions (Companies Act, SEBI LODR/BRSR) before expanding into international frameworks — examiners expect domestically grounded analysis.
  • When writing about ESG risks, use the TCFD structure (Governance → Strategy → Risk Management → Metrics) as an implicit organizing tool; it demonstrates structured thinking and covers the framework's key expectations.
  • In case-study-type questions, identify the material ESG issue first (what is actually at risk — reputational, regulatory, financial, operational), then recommend board-level actions a Company Secretary would realistically propose.
  • When differentiating frameworks, use a comparison grid in your mind: GRI (comprehensive/stakeholder), SASB (investor/industry), TCFD (climate/financial), UN PRI (investor principles), BRSR (India/mandatory). State the purpose explicitly.
  • For governance-focused questions, connect ESG to the CS's core functions: board committee structuring, secretarial audit scope expansion potential, stakeholder grievance redressal, and compliance monitoring.
  • Use current terminology — refer to 'Sustainability Reporting', 'BRSR Core', 'double materiality', and 'net-zero commitments' rather than outdated terms; examiners reward contemporary accuracy.

Common traps

  • Confusing GRI with SASB: GRI is stakeholder-inclusive and comprehensive; SASB is investor-focused and industry-specific. They serve different audiences — do not conflate them in answers.
  • Assuming BRSR is only for large firms: BRSR applies only to top 1000 listed entities by market cap — do not overgeneralize to all listed companies.
  • Mistaking CSR spending for ESG compliance: CSR is a narrow spending obligation under Section 135; ESG is a broader governance and risk discipline. Fulfilling CSR does not imply strong ESG performance.
  • Ignoring Scope 3: Many candidates fixate on Scope 1 and 2 but overlook Scope 3, which often represents the majority of a company's carbon footprint and is a growing disclosure requirement.
  • Treating greenwashing only as an environmental issue: Greenwashing can apply to social claims (diversity figures) and governance claims (independence assertions) too — it is an umbrella term for any ESG misrepresentation.
  • Forgetting the Companies Act 2013 governance linkages: ESG questions often expect the answer to reference Sections 166 (directors' duties), 177 (Audit Committee), 184 (disclosure of interests), and 135 (CSR) — limiting the answer to generic ESG theory may lose marks.
