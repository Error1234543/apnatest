Corporate Restructuring, Insolvency, Liquidation and Winding-up — Quick Revision

CSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Amalgamation/Scheme of Arrangement: A statutory mechanism under Sections 230-232 of the Companies Act, 2013 whereby a company enters into a compromise or arrangement with its creditors or members (or classes thereof) for reconstruction, amalgamation, merger, demerger, or any other reorganization, sanctioned by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT).
  • Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP): The statutory process initiated under Section 4 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 when a corporate debtor fails to pay its financial debt and a NCLT admits an application for commencing CIRP. During CIRP, a moratorium takes effect, an Interim Resolution Professional (IRP)/Resolution Professional (RP) is appointed, and an Operational Creditor may seek debt recovery separately in appropriate forums.
  • Financial Debt: Under Section 5(8) of the IBC, 2016, includes any loan against which interest is payable and encompasses any amount raised under any financial instrument, and any amount in respect of which a right to receive money has arisen under a lease but only to the extent of the obligation of the lessee to pay such amount as rent in accordance with the terms of the lease agreement.
  • Operational Debt: Under Section 5(21) of the IBC, 2016, means a claim in respect of the provision of goods or services (including employment) owed to an operational creditor by a corporate debtor.
  • Liquidation: Under Section 5(19) of the IBC, 2016, means a process by which the affairs, business, property, and assets of a corporate debtor are dealt with and the corporate debtor is wound up; the NCLT passes an order directing that the corporate debtor be liquidated after either no resolution plan is approved or a resolution plan approved is not implemented.
  • Voluntary Winding Up: Under Section 304 of the Companies Act, 2013, when a company is able to pay its debts in full (within a period specified — solvency declaration required), it may pass a special resolution for voluntary winding up, known as Members' Voluntary Winding Up; where a company cannot pay its debts, it passes a special resolution for creditors' voluntary winding up.
  • Debtor: In IBC terminology under Section 5(7), 'debtor' means a person who owes a debt and includes a joint debtor, but does not include a financial creditor, operational creditor, or any person from whom an operational creditor is seeking recovery.
  • Morphium: Not applicable here; replaced in current understanding by the moratorium concept — under Section 14 of the IBC, 2016, upon admission of an application for CIRP, no suit or proceeding in respect of the debt owed to the corporate debtor shall be instituted or continued in the NCLT or any other court.

Key concepts

  • Amalgamation — Jurisdiction and Procedure (Sections 230-232, Companies Act 2013): An application can be made to the NCLT by any party — the company, any creditor, member, or contributory. The NCLT may convene a meeting of creditors/members or class of creditors/members to consider the scheme. If the majority in number representing ¾ in value of those present and voting agree, the NCLT may sanction the scheme. For mergers involving listed companies, SEBI's SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations and the listing regulations must also be complied with. For schemes involving foreign companies, Sections 234 and 235 apply. Cross-border mergers are governed by Section 234 read with the relevant rules.
  • Schemes — Conditions and Types: Amalgamation (merger and absorption); Demerger under Section 2(19AA) — the new entity or transferred undertaking is distributed among shareholders; Compromise — settlement with creditors; Reconstruction — including financial restructuring (Section 230A — permitted under certain conditions), share buyback, and conversion of debt into equity. Key condition: the scheme must not be opposed to public interest. The NCLT has power to enquire into the bona fides of the promoters and directors. Tax implications under the Income Tax Act may also apply.
  • CIRP — Initiation and Admission (Section 4, IBC): A financial creditor, operational creditor, or the corporate debtor itself can initiate CIRP. The threshold is 'default' — failure to repay a minimum debt of ₹1 crore (as per current notification, subject to amendments). Upon filing, the NCLT examines whether default exists and whether the application is complete. Admisison is not automatic. Once admitted, an IRP takes over management from the board, a moratorium under Section 14 takes effect, and a Resolution Professional (RP) is appointed. CRITP runs for 180 days, extendable by 90 days. Extension requires approval of creditors constituting 66% or more of voting share.
  • CIRP — Operational Creditor vs Financial Creditor: Financial creditors have voting rights in the Committee of Creditors (CoC) based on the amount of financial debt owed. Operational creditors do not participate in CoC decisions but can challenge the admission of CIRP applications. The IBC distinguishes sharply between the two categories. A financial creditor may initiate CIRP for a default of ₹1 lakh (subject to amendments); operational creditors face higher threshold scrutiny.
  • Moratorium (Section 14): Upon admission of CIRP, Section 14 imposes a moratorium on: (a) any suit or proceeding against the corporate debtor; (b) transfer of assets by the corporate debtor; (c) recovering any property by a lessor; (d) any action to foreclose on security; (e) any recovery of any property by any person. Exceptions exist for government dues and actions taken by the RP in the ordinary course of business. The purpose is to preserve the corporate debtor's estate and allow the RP to manage the business as a going concern.
  • Resolution Professional — Role and Powers (Section 15-25): The IRP/RP is responsible for collecting information about the corporate debtor, constituting the CoC, approving applications for relief, publishing an announcement inviting resolution plans, evaluating resolution plans received, and assisting in implementation. The RP can operate bank accounts, engage professionals, and carry on the business of the corporate debtor. The RP owes fiduciary duties to all stakeholders. Replacement of IRP by NCLT is possible if there is cause shown.
  • Committee of Creditors (CoC) — Composition and Decisions (Section 21): Composed of financial creditors who have invoked CIRP or were owed financial debt at the time of commencement. The CoC meets to decide on matters such as extending the CIRP period, appointing/replacing the RP, and approving resolution plans. Decisions are taken by 66% or more of voting share (unless otherwise specified). The CoC may delegate certain functions to the RP. Government financial creditors are included. Secured creditors have a voting right proportional to their financial debt.
  • Resolution Plan — Approval and Implementation (Section 31): A resolution plan must provide for the management of the affairs of the corporate debtor, the mode of implementation, the period over which it is to be implemented, and the distribution of assets to be made to creditors. The CoC must approve the plan by at least 66% of voting share. Upon approval by NCLT, the plan is binding on all stakeholders including the corporate debtor, employees, members, creditors, guarantors, and the RP. Implementation of the approved plan is overseen by the RP. If the plan is not implemented, NCLT may order liquidation.
  • Liquidation Proceedings (Part III, Chapter IV, Sections 32-59, IBC): Liquidation may commence: (a) when a resolution plan is not approved by NCLT; (b) when a resolution plan approved by NCLT is not implemented within the prescribed time; (c) at the request of the CoC (with supermajority). The NCLT orders liquidation, and a liquidator is appointed. The liquidator realizes assets, distributes proceeds in the order of priority under Section 53, and seeks dissolution. The liquidator may disclaim onerous property and repudiate undischarged contracts. Liquidation process follows the waterfall mechanism.
  • Winding Up under Companies Act, 2013 — Modes (Sections 270-322): Voluntary Winding Up (Sections 304-307): Members' Voluntary Winding Up requires a solvency declaration by directors stating the company can pay its debts in full within 3 years. Creditors' Voluntary Winding Up applies when a company cannot pay its debts. Winding Up by Tribunal (Sections 270-284): Grounds include inability to pay debts, just and equitable ground, default in filing statements/accounts, oppression/mismanagement, company acting beyond powers, etc. The NCLT has exclusive jurisdiction. Provisions on preferential payment, avoidable transactions (preferences, undervalue transactions, extortionate credit transactions) under Sections 431-434 mirror IBC principles. Provisions on fraudulent trading (Section 447) also apply.
  • Liquidation — Order of Priority (Section 53, IBC): Upon liquidation, proceeds are distributed in the following order: (1) Secured creditors (realization proceeds) to the extent of security; (2) Workmen's dues and employees' dues (24 months prior to liquidation); (3) Government dues; (4) Unsecured financial creditors; (5) Other creditors; (6) Preferential creditors (to the extent not covered by security); (7) Residue to members/shareholders according to their rights. This is a strictly statutory order. IBC overrides the Companies Act provision where there is conflict.
  • Cross-Border Insolvency (Section 44): The Central Government may notify bilateral agreements or multilateral conventions on cross-border insolvency. The NCLT has adopted principles of model law approach for recognition of foreign proceedings. Cross-border mergers (Sections 234 & 235) provide a framework for Indian companies to merge with foreign companies and vice versa, subject to rules notified by the Central Government. Cross-border insolvency remains evolving with limited judicial guidance in India.
  • Personal Insolvency and Partnership Firm Insolvency: The IBC originally applied only to corporate persons. However, the Government has notified the Individual Insolvency Resolution Process Rules, 2019 and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Amendment) Rules, 2019 extending the framework to individual debtors (natural persons) and partnership firms. For individuals, the DIP (Debt Irrigation Plan) or bankruptcy proceedings apply. For partnership firms, CIRP can be initiated analogous to corporate entities. The distinction between partnership firm insolvency and individual insolvency is important.
  • Key Differences Between CIRP and Winding Up: CIRP aims at revival/rescue of the corporate debtor as a going concern. Winding up leads to dissolution of the entity. CIRP is initiated under IBC with a moratorium; voluntary winding up is under the Companies Act without a moratorium (though creditors may approach tribunal). CIRP involves a CoC-driven process; winding up involves the tribunal supervising. CIRP timelines are strict (180+90 days); winding up has no such fixed timeline but follows procedural requirements. The treatment of secured creditors differs.

Important facts

  • The threshold default for initiating CIRP is ₹1 crore for financial creditors and operational creditors (subject to government notifications amending this figure).
  • CIRP must be completed within 180 days; extension of 90 days is permissible with the consent of financial creditors constituting 66% or more of voting share.
  • A resolution plan requires approval by at least 66% of voting share of financial creditors in the CoC, followed by NCLT sanction.
  • The moratorium under Section 14 of IBC commences from the date of admission of the CIRP application and ends on the date of approval or rejection of the resolution plan, or commencement of liquidation.
  • Under Section 53 of IBC, workmen's dues rank above government dues and unsecured financial creditors in the liquidation waterfall.
  • Avoidable transactions — preference transactions (Section 431 Companies Act), undervalue transactions, and extortionate credit transactions can be challenged in winding up proceedings.
  • Fraudulent trading (Section 447 Companies Act) attracts penalties including imprisonment up to 10 years and/or fine.
  • For Members' Voluntary Winding Up, the directors must make a Declaration of Solvency stating that the company will be able to pay its debts in full within a period not exceeding three years from the commencement of winding up.
  • National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) has exclusive jurisdiction over matters relating to IBC and Companies Act winding-up proceedings at the principal bench and district benches.
  • An appeal from an NCLT order lies to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) within 45 days; further appeal to the Supreme Court on a substantial question of law.
  • Under Section 230 of the Companies Act 2013, the NCLT may order a meeting of creditors or class of creditors or members to be summoned in such manner as the NCLT directs.
  • The Resolution Professional must publish an invitation for resolution plans in the leading English newspaper and on the website of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI).
  • IBBI (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India) is the regulatory authority overseeing the insolvency process, registered intermediaries, and professionals.
  • Operational creditors have the right to challenge the admission of an application for CIRP but cannot participate in the CoC voting on resolution plans.
  • Under Section 232(12) Companies Act 2013, the NCLT may direct that the assets of the transferor company be transferred to the transferee company and the liabilities shall be discharged as provided in the scheme.

Memory aids

  • CIRP Acronym: C.I.R.P. = Commencement → Information Package → Resolution Plan → Plan Approval → Implementation. Remember the flow: you admit first, then collect info, invite plans, evaluate, approve, implement.
  • Waterfall Priority (Section 53): Think of a pyramid — top is Secured Creditors (realization of security), next is Workmen/Dues, then Government Dues, then Unsecured Creditors, then Other Creditors, then Residue to Shareholders. Use the mnemonic: 'S.W.I.G.O.R.' — Secured, Workmen, Government, Indebted/Creditors, Other, Residue.
  • Moratorium Mnemonic: Section 14 = 'STOP' — no suits, no transfers, no recovery, no foreclosure. Memorize the four prohibitions as: Stop Suits, Stop Transfers, Stop Recoveries, Stop Foreclosures.
  • Scheme Sanction Steps: Apply to NCLT → Convene Meeting → Majority (¾) agrees → NCLT Sanctions → File with Registrar → Implementation. Remember the chain: Application → Assembly → Agreement → Approval → Archive.
  • CIRP Timeline: 180 days (standard) + 90 days (extension) = 270 days maximum. Think '180-90 rule'.
  • CoC Thresholds: 66% for most decisions (approval of plan, extension), 51% for some procedural decisions. Remember: 'Supermajority for big decisions, simple majority for routine'.
  • Winding Up vs CIRP Comparison: Create a table mentally. CIRP = Resolution, Revival, Moratorium, CoC. Winding Up = Dissolution, Tribunal, Liquidator, Distribution.

Exam tips

  • For ICSI Professional exam, focus on numerical problems involving calculation of resolution plan distributions under Section 53 waterfall, and identify correct creditor priority in liquidation scenarios.
  • Memorize the exact percentages: ¾ value for scheme sanction meetings under Companies Act; 66% for CoC decisions under IBC; 66% for resolution plan approval; 51% for certain procedural decisions in CoC.
  • Practice writing short notes on 'Advantages of CIRP over traditional winding up' and 'Role of RP in maintaining going concern status'. These are frequently asked descriptive questions.
  • Be prepared to distinguish between a company under CIRP and a company in voluntary winding up — both have different legal consequences for directors, creditors, and employees.
  • Revise landmark NCLT/NCLAT judgments on IBC: In re Essel Vision Productions (on enforcement of ARC rights during CIRP); In re ABIL (on valuation methodology); In re IL&FS (on resolution plan approval). Know the ratio and principle briefly.
  • When answering questions on schemes of arrangement, always mention the dual compliance — with both Companies Act (Section 230-232) and SEBI regulations if listed. Also note tax aspects under Section 47 of IT Act.
  • For liquidation questions, remember the role of the liquidator: realizing assets, investigating affairs, calling meetings of creditors/members, distributing proceeds, and recommending dissolution.
  • Pay attention to recent amendments notified by the government — especially changes to the minimum default amount, co-criteria for resolution plan approval, and any new rules on cross-border insolvency.
  • In MCQs, watch out for options that swap financial and operational creditor rights, or that assign NCLT powers that actually belong to NCLAT or the Supreme Court.
  • Use the IBC's objective — 'to promote available options for maximisation of value of assets of the corporate debtor

Common traps

  • Confusing financial creditors with operational creditors — remember financial creditors are those owed financial debt (loans, bonds, derivatives), while operational creditors are those owed for goods/services (suppliers, employees as operational creditors only for certain claims). Only financial creditors form the CoC and vote on resolution plans.
  • Assuming the moratorium applies to all proceedings — it does not stay proceedings by the ED (Enforcement Directorate) for PMLA offences, nor does it prevent the government from collecting dues in certain circumstances. Also, the RP can continue the business notwithstanding the moratorium.
  • Misremembering the threshold for CIRP initiation — the default amount is ₹1 crore (as per latest notification), not ₹1 lakh. The ₹1 lakh threshold was the original figure and has been amended multiple times.
  • Thinking that a resolution plan can be rejected by NCLT arbitrarily — NCLT can reject only on grounds specified under Section 31(1), namely: (a) plan does not meet the criteria under Section 31(1)(a); (b) plan is not approved by CoC; (c) plan is contrary to law, public policy, or order of NCLT. NCLT cannot substitute its commercial wisdom for that of the CoC.
  • Confusing voluntary winding up with CIRP liquidation — voluntary winding up is under Companies Act, initiated by shareholders via special resolution; CIRP liquidation is under IBC, initiated by creditors. The procedures, timelines, and authorities differ significantly.
  • Assuming individual insolvency is covered under the same IBC provisions as corporate insolvency — while the framework is similar, the rules, processes, and thresholds differ for individual debtors, partnership firms, and corporate persons.
  • Overlooking the requirement for a Declaration of Solvency in members' voluntary winding up — without this declaration, the winding up defaults to creditors' voluntary winding up. The declaration must be made within 5 weeks before passing the special resolution and filed within 1 week after passing.
  • Assuming secured creditors can be sidelined in CIRP — secured financial creditors can vote in the CoC and their claims are to be paid first to the extent of realization from security. However, if they surrender their security to the liquidator, they become unsecured creditors for the balance.
  • Misunderstanding the scope of Section 230 — it applies not only to amalgamations but also to compromises, arrangements, reconstructions, and financial restructuring. The section is wide in ambit.
