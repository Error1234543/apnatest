Quick Revision — Drafting, Pleadings and Appearances (CS Professional, Group 1)

CSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Pleading: Any statement of claim or defence in a suit or proceeding, including the plaint, written statement, and any amendment thereto. Governed primarily by Order VI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, as applicable to tribunal proceedings.
  • Drafting: The skill of reducing into writing any legal document, instrument, application, petition, opinion or appearance that complies with statutory requirements, procedural rules and the standards expected before a judicial/quasi-judicial forum.
  • Petition: A formal written application made to a court or tribunal seeking some form of relief, order or direction. Before NCLT, most matters are instituted by way of petition under the relevant sections of the Companies Act, 2013 and the NCLT Rules, 2016.
  • Application: A request made to the NCLT or other authority during the pendency of a proceeding for some ancillary or interim relief, such as an application for interim order under section 439, adjournment, amendment or compounding.
  • Appearance: The act of a party or its authorised representative ( Advocate/CS/CA) presenting itself before a forum, and includes filing a vakalatnama, appearance memo and making oral submissions.
  • Vakalatnama: A document executed by a client authorising an advocate to appear and act on his behalf before a court/tribunal. It specifies the scope of authority, fees and particulars of the advocate.
  • Opinion: A written legal/advisory note rendered by a Company Secretary or advocate upon a referred question of law or fact, stating the relevant provisions, principles, analysis and conclusions with supporting reasoning.
  • Review: A statutory remedy under which the same court/tribunal re-examines its own decision for apparent error or mistake on the face of the record, or for receipt of new evidence or sufficient reason. Under NCLT Rules, review lies under Rule 43 read with section 246 of the Companies Act, 2013.

Key concepts

  • The rules of pleading govern how a claim or defence must be set out so that the opposite party knows exactly what case it has to meet. The essential principles are: (a) Facts must be pleaded, not evidence — the drafter states material facts but does not rehearse the proof or cite provisions as facts. (b) Material facts must be stated concisely and in a coherent sequence. (c) Each pleading must contain only facts necessary to establish the cause of action or defence; irrelevant or scandalous matter is liable to be struck out. (d) Numbers, dates and sums must be stated accurately where they are material. (e) Where a plea is based on fraud, breach of contract, negligence or misrepresentation, the particulars must be specifically stated — a general averment is insufficient. (f) Every pleading must be verified by endorsement showing which assertions are admitted, which are denied and which are based on information received. (g) The language must be intelligible, simple and free from argumentative or oblique expressions. In the NCLT context, these rules apply through the NCLT Rules and Practice Directions, modified by the procedural nature of the tribunal.
  • A standard NCLT petition generally contains the following blocks, each of which must be drafted with precision: (a) Cause Title — name of the Forum, place, petition number (if already assigned), and full title of the parties. The petitioner is described first; the respondents are described with their capacity (e.g., 'Respondent No. 1 — ABC Ltd., a company incorporated under the Companies Act, 2013, having its registered office at…'). (b) Preliminary/Introductory Paragraphs — citation of the enabling section(s), jurisdictional fact, limitation statement, and a brief synopsis of the case. (c) Facts of the Case — chronological narration in numbered paragraphs, each paragraph confined as far as possible to one distinct fact. (d) Grounds — articulated reasons why the petitioner is entitled to the relief sought; each ground must be linked to a specific factual paragraph. (e) Interim Relief — if an interim order is sought, state the prayer explicitly under section 439 along with the facts necessitating urgency. (f) Verification — signed by the petitioner or authorised person with the usual verification clause. (g) Prayers — numbered prayers clearly stating the specific relief(s) sought; avoid vague or composite prayers. A common error is merging facts and grounds or placing legal provisions inside the facts section — grounds and prayers are separate blocks.
  • Applications before NCLT are numerous and include interim applications, applications for amendment, for substitution of parties, for recall of order, for expedited hearing, and applications for condonation of delay. The essential structural features are: (a) The application must identify the petition/case number and the proceedings in which it arises. (b) Sectional sanction — every application must cite the specific provision authorising the NCLT to pass the sought order (e.g., section 439 for interim order, section 241(3) for amendment in oppression petitions). (c) The 'whereas' or 'furthermore' clauses should logically build the justification in short paras. (d) The prayer must be precise; for interim orders specify the exact restraint or direction sought and the duration. (e) For condonation of delay, the application must separately narrate the timeline of events, show bona fide conduct of the applicant, and demonstrate that no substantial prejudice will flow to the opposite party — mere inadvertence is often held insufficient by tribunals.
  • These are among the most tested drafting areas in CS. An oppression/mismanagement petition must address several threshold requirements beyond the statement of facts: (a) Standing — a minimum of 100 members or members holding not less than 10% of voting power, or any member(s) if the company is a subsidiary, may petition. The petition must set out the petitioner's qualification in terms of shareholding. (b) The conduct complained of must relate to the affairs of the company and must be burdensome, oppressive or unfairly prejudicial. (c) The petition must specifically identify the persons whose conduct is impugned and the nature of their actions — general allegations are vulnerable to dismissal for lack of specificity. (d) The petition should consider whether alternative remedies exist (e.g., statutory demand under section 477 or director removal under section 169), though existence of an alternative remedy is not an absolute bar. (e) The prayer under section 242(2) is wide-ranging — it may provide for regulation of future conduct, purchase of shares, termination of agreements, etc. Drafters must tailor the prayer to the facts rather than copy a generic format.
  • Drafting a scheme petition demands care on three counts: jurisdiction, disclosure and prayers. (a) Jurisdictional facts — the petitioner must establish the company's status, the class of creditors/members, and whether any earlier sanction has been sought. If the scheme involves a cross-border merger, the additional conditions under section 234 must be addressed. (b) Disclosure — the petition must be accompanied by an explanatory statement giving full details of the scheme, financial implications, valuation reports (if any), and impacts on stakeholders. For amalgamations, the income-tax and central-government clearances (where required) must be flagged. (c) The prayer typically seeks: (i) calling of meetings of creditors/members, (ii) directions for convening meetings, (iii) supervision of the meeting process, and (iv) sanction of the scheme on satisfactory reporting. Ensure that the classification of creditors/members into classes is stated and reasoned, because improper class division is a classic ground for objection.
  • An appeal to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal lies against orders of the NCLT under section 424 of the Companies Act, 2013 and must be filed within 60 days from the date of the order or direction appealed against (extendable under section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 on sufficient cause). Essential drafting components include: (a) Title referencing both the NCLT order number and date and the NCLAT appeal number format. (b) Grounds of appeal — each ground must be a separate legal or factual proposition challenging the impugned order; do not merely repeat the facts of the original petition. (c) Specific reference to the paragraphs of the NCLT order that are assailed. (d) Whether a deposit of 50% of the decretal amount (in monetary orders) or a stay pendente lite is sought. (e) Verification and supporting affidavit. A frequent pitfall is including fresh evidence in an appeal — NCLAT ordinarily decides on the record of the NCLT proceedings unless exceptional circumstances justify admission.
  • Legal opinions are regularly examined in the CS Professional draftings paper. A structured opinion should contain: (a) Issue/Questions — precisely frame the questions referred. (b) Facts — a concise statement of the factual matrix on which the opinion is founded, distinguishing established facts from assumptions. (c) Relevant Provisions — list the statutory sections, rules, notifications, judicial precedents and MCA circulars applicable. Do not reproduce entire text — quote the operative part. (d) Discussion/Analysis — apply the law to the facts in a logical sequence; distinguish adverse authorities if any. For each issue, give a rule, apply it to the facts, and conclude. (e) Conclusion — a clear, numbered answer to each question. (f) Caveats — note any dependencies, missing information or limitations of the opinion. Use neutral and measured language — avoid statements that could be read as absolute guarantees.
  • Drafting accuracy on limitation is critical because a limitation issue can defeat an otherwise meritorious petition. Key limits frequently tested: (a) Appeal against NCLT order to NCLAT — 60 days under section 424. (b) Appeal against NCLAT order to Supreme Court — 60 days under section 431. (c) Application for review before NCLT — 30 days from the date of the order/delivery of the judgment. (d) Petition for compounding of offences under section 450 — no strict limitation is prescribed in the Act but is governed by general principles of limitation; NCLT typically applies the residuary period of Limitation Act. (e) Filing of chargesheet/charge sheet under section 212 in examination proceedings — governed by the procedural rules. (f) Objections to reduction of capital under section 66 — 2 months from publication of the register. The drafter must always include a limitation paragraph showing compliance and seek condonation separately if a delay exists.
  • The NCLT and NCLAT have issued Practice Directions that govern formatting and procedural compliance; drafters are expected to know the essentials: (a) Font and spacing — petitions and applications must conform to the font, margin and spacing norms specified by the Central Rules, usually 12 pt font with 1.5 line spacing. (b) Page numbering and paragraph numbering — every page must bear a serial number; every paragraph of the main petition must be consecutively numbered. (c) Index and group-wise arrangement — where a petition relies on multiple documents, an index must be placed at the beginning, and copies of key documents should be grouped and paginated separately. (d) Copy requirements — multiple copies as per the Practice Directions must be filed, including a certified true copy for each respondent. (e) Affidavit — almost every petition or application requires a supporting affidavit; the deponent must have personal knowledge or be authorised based on records. (f) Mentioning/listing — urgent mentioning must follow the procedure laid down in the relevant Practice Direction and cannot be invoked for routine listing.
  • The right to appear and represent before the NCLT/NCLAT is governed by the Act and Rules: (a) A petitioner or respondent may appear in person or be represented by an Advocate enrolled under the Advocates Act, a Company Secretary in practice, a Cost Accountant in practice, a Chartered Accountant in practice, or any employee of the party who is authorized by the board/resolution. (b) A CS in practice may appear on behalf of the company he represents without additional permission, subject to the Bar Council restrictions on advocacy by non-lawyers — the NCLT Rules specifically permit CS practice membership to represent. (c) Vakalatnama must be filed at the time of first appearance and must state the full name, enrollment/practice certificate number, address and email ID of the representative. (d) Appearance memo is required when the representative changes during the pendency of the matter. (e) Power of Attorney — where a party appoints a person under a Power of Attorney, the POA must be produced and the representative must confirm they are acting within its scope. (f) Ex-parte orders — absence of a party does not preclude ex-parte proceedings; however, the absent party may apply to set aside the order under the applicable rule.
  • Recurring mistakes observed in CS drafting examinations and practice include: (a) Including legal provisions inside the facts section — provisions belong in grounds or in a separate 'reliance' section, not in the narrative of facts. (b) Vague prayers — the relief must be specific, enforceable and matched to the facts; a prayer asking for 'any other relief as deemed fit' without supporting specific prayers is deficient. (c) Missing jurisdictional averments — failure to state the company's CIN, registered office address, share capital structure or the section invoking jurisdiction can lead to dismissal. (d) Conflation of limitation with merits — if limitation is contested, raise it in a separate preliminary objection or in the reply, but the petitioner must address limitation affirmatively in the petition itself. (e) Defective verification — a petition not properly verified or verified by a person lacking authority is a fundamental defect. (f) Inadequate particulars in fraud/allegation cases — where fraud is alleged, the draft must provide time, place, person, act and consequence; otherwise the allegation is liable to be struck out. (g) Copy/formatting non-compliance — ignoring Practice Directions on font, pagination, number of copies, and annexure indexing attracts rejection or re-fixing.

Important facts

  • Order VI of CPC lays down the rules of pleading — material facts, not evidence, must be pleaded; fraud, breach, negligence and misrepresentation require specific particulars.
  • Section 241 of the Companies Act, 2013 empowers any member(s) meeting the threshold criteria to apply to NCLT for relief against oppression and mismanagement.
  • Section 242 enumerates the powers of NCLT to make orders on an oppression/mismanagement petition, including regulation of conduct, purchase of shares and termination of agreements.
  • Section 230–232 provide the statutory framework for compromise, arrangement and amalgamation; NCLT sanctions schemes after satisfaction of procedural and substantive conditions.
  • Section 439 vests NCLT with inherent powers to make such orders as it deems just for maintaining fairness and ensuring the ends of justice, including interim orders.
  • Section 424 provides for appeals to NCLAT against any order or direction of NCLT; the limitation period is 60 days.
  • Section 431 provides for appeals to the Supreme Court against NCLAT orders within 60 days.
  • Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 applies to tribunal proceedings and permits extension of limit on showing sufficient cause.
  • Review before NCLT lies under Rule 43 of NCLT Rules, 2016 read with section 246 of the Companies Act, within 30 days from the order.
  • A legal opinion must contain Issue, Facts, Relevant Provisions, Discussion, Conclusion and Caveats in that logical sequence.
  • NCLT Practice Directions mandate font size, spacing, pagination, index, copy requirements and affidavit support for filings.
  • Under the NCLT Rules, a Company Secretary in practice is recognized as an eligible representative who may appear and argue on behalf of a party.
  • In scheme petitions, classification of creditors/members into classes is a jurisdictional requirement; improper class division vitiates the proceeding.
  • An application for condonation of delay must separately address: the period of delay, the cause of delay, the bona fides of the applicant, and absence of prejudice to the opposite party.
  • Vakalatnama must specify the scope of authority, the fees agreed upon and the complete contact details of the appearing representative.
  • Petitions under sections 395–396 (old Act) / section 230–232 (new Act) must be accompanied by the scheme document, explanatory statement and valuation report where applicable.
  • Grounds of appeal must directly assail the impugned order by paragraph; they cannot be used to reopen findings of fact that were available to be challenged earlier.

Memory aids

  • PEEFAC — mnemonic for essential elements of every petition: P = Parties with correct description, E = Enabling section, E = Explanation/Jurisdictional facts, F = Facts in chronology, A = Annexures indexed, C = Confirmation of limitation and verification.
  • GROUD — mnemonic to remember what belongs in the 'Grounds' section: G = Legal basis, R = Reliance on facts, O = Opposed conduct/act, U = Unlawfulness/unfairness asserted, D = Desired direction from the tribunal.
  • OPINION — checklist for drafting a legal opinion: O = Outline the issue, P = Present facts, I = Identify provisions, N = Nuanced discussion applying law to facts, I = Interpret precedents, O = Offer conclusions, N = Note caveats and assumptions.
  • CLASSIFY for scheme petitions: C = Company status and CIN, L = List of classes of members/creditors, A = Ascertain approval thresholds, S = Scheme terms and consideration, I = Inform stakeholders via explanatory statement, F = File valuation/report, Y = Yielding to NCLT discretion.
  • LIMITATION trap — memorise: 60 days to NCLAT from NCLT order; 60 days to SC from NCLAT order; 30 days for review before NCLT; always cross-check with Limitation Act for residuary entries.

Exam tips

  • Always begin by identifying the forum and the section under which the petition/application/appeal is being drafted; this determines the structure and the prayers you can legitimately include.
  • In examination, allocate time proportionally — a 15-mark drafting question deserves roughly twice the time of a 7-mark opinion question; do not rush the concluding prayers.
  • When a question provides a fact pattern, extract and number every material fact before you start drafting; your facts section should mirror the chronological story given.
  • Separately label 'Jurisdiction', 'Facts', 'Grounds', 'Prayers' and 'Verification' with clear headings — examiners mark structure and presentation heavily.
  • For scheme/amalgamation questions, ensure you address class meeting, explanatory statement, valuation and stakeholder impact even if the question does not explicitly ask for them — omission is a common mark-deduction.
  • In appeal drafting, quote the impugned order number, date and paragraph(s) attacked; without these, grounds of appeal are considered vague.
  • For opinions, use a Q-and-A format when multiple issues are raised — it makes evaluation easier and demonstrates structured thinking.
  • Never reproduce entire sections verbatim; quote only the operative portion relevant to the problem and cite the section number correctly.
  • Check whether the question demands a petition, an application or an opinion — confusing these three structures will cost significant marks even if the substantive content is correct.
  • When asked to draft a reply or counter-affidavit, follow the paragraph-wise denial/admission format; address each allegation in the same sequence as the petition and raise preliminary objections before going to the merit.

Common traps

  • Writing evidence instead of facts — citing document names, witness statements or production details inside the body of the petition rather than in a separate list of documents/witnesses.
  • Merging multiple causes of action into one petition without clear separation, leading to confusion and potential rejection for non-compliance with Order II CPC.
  • Praying for vague or omnibus relief without enumerated specific prayers matched to the factual matrix.
  • Omitting a clear limitation statement in the preliminary paragraphs, which invites dismissal on limitation even if the merits are strong.
  • Failing to provide specific particulars when alleging fraud, misrepresentation or misconduct — a general allegation is legally insufficient.
  • Treating an appeal as a fresh rehearing — NCLAT does not ordinarily admit fresh evidence; the appeal must be confined to the ground that the NCLT order is erroneous in law or fact.
  • Using generic format answers without tailoring prayers and grounds to the specific facts given in the examination question — this attracts zero or minimal marks in practical drafting papers.
  • Neglecting the requirement of a supporting affidavit for petitions and applications — a petition filed without an affidavit is procedurally defective.
  • Ignoring the class-divisions requirement in scheme petitions — mixing different classes of stakeholders without justification is a textbook error.
  • Citing repealed provisions instead of the current corresponding sections of the Companies Act, 2013 — a frequent drafting pitfall in CS exams.
