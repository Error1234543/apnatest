GPSC Quick Revision — Panchayati Raj and Local Self-Government

GPSCEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Panchayati Raj: A three-tier system of local self-government at village, intermediate (block/mandal), and district levels, constitutionally mandated under Part IX of the Indian Constitution (Articles 243–243O).
  • Gram Sabha: The assembly of all persons registered as voters in a village panchayat area; it is the foundational body of the Panchayati Raj system and performs advisory, supervisory, and democratic functions.
  • Municipality: An urban local body constituted under Article 243Q for a Nagar Panchayat, Municipal Council, or Municipal Corporation, depending on population and urbanization criteria.
  • 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992: Amended the Constitution to add Part IX (Articles 243 to 243O) and the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects), giving constitutional status to Panchayats; came into force on 24 April 1993.
  • 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992: Added Part IXA (Articles 243P to 243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects), providing constitutional recognition to urban local bodies (municipalities).
  • District Planning Committee (DPC): A body constituted under Article 243ZC to consolidate plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities and prepare a draft development plan for the district as a whole.

Important facts

  • 73rd Amendment Act received Presidential assent on 20 May 1992 and came into force on 24 April 1993.
  • 74th Amendment Act received Presidential assent on 20 May 1992 and came into force on 1 June 1992.
  • The Eleventh Schedule contains 29 subjects for Panchayats; the Twelfth Schedule contains 18 subjects for Municipalities.
  • The first Panchayati Raj institution in India was launched in Rajasthan's Nagaur district on 2 October 1959 by Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Balwantrao Mehta Committee (1957) recommended the three-tier Panchayati Raj system.
  • Ashok Mehta Committee (1977) recommended a two-tier system and called for mandatory taxation power to Panchayats.
  • L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986) first gave the term 'local self-government' a constitutional status and recommended its insertion as a fundamental right.
  • Article 243B(2) permits the Legislature of a State to extend the provisions of Part IX to Scheduled Areas, subject to such exceptions and modifications as the President may specify.
  • Ninth Schedule (States of Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, and Meghalaya) are partially exempted from the 73rd Amendment's application; the Eighth Schedule (Jammu & Kashmir prior to reorganisation) had its own local self-government framework. Post-2019, J&K is covered under the 73rd Amendment via separate legislation.
  • Gujarat has 33 Zila Parishads and approximately 14,300 Gram Panchayats as per the latest reorganization (2024 data).
  • The minimum age for membership of a Panchayat is 21 years (Article 243E(2)).
  • The term of Panchayats is five years from the date of their constituted first meeting (Article 243E(1)).
  • Article 243-K requires the State Election Commission to superintend, direction, and control of the preparation of electoral rolls for Panchayats.
  • The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) audits accounts of Panchayats as per Article 243-K(2) if the State Legislature so provides.
  • Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008, established village courts at the Panchayat level for speedy justice delivery, though implementation has been limited.
  • NITI Aayog's Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) has been used as an accountability mechanism in several states through Panchayats.
  • Gujarat's 'Sugam' e-Panchayat accounting system was launched to bring transparency in panchayat finances.
  • The 15th Finance Commission (2021–2026) recommended ₹3,00,000 crore over five years as vertical devolution to local bodies, with specific allocation for Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • The Ministry of Panchayati Raj releases the 'Status of Panchayati Raj Institutions' report annually, which tracks key indicators across states.

Exam tips

  • For GPSC, expect questions on constitutional articles related to Panchayats — especially Articles 243B through 243O. Memorize key article numbers and their corresponding provisions.
  • GPSC frequently asks about committee recommendations: Balwantrao Mehta (three-tier), Ashok Mehta (two-tier), L.M. Singhvi (local self-government as constitutional right). Know the year, chairman name, and key recommendation for each.
  • Focus on Gujarat-specific implementation: Gujarat Panchayats Act 1993, number of Zila Parishads (33), Gram Panchayat count (~14,300), the Sugam e-governance initiative, and active Gram Sabha social audit practices.
  • Distinguish between Rural (Part IX, 73rd Amendment) and Urban (Part IXA, 74th Amendment) local bodies. Questions often test whether you can identify which part applies to which body.
  • Pay attention to the phrase 'shall be constituted' versus 'may be constituted' in constitutional provisions — this distinction is frequently tested in GPSC's objective papers.
  • Remember that reservation for women is 'not less than one-third' (Article 243D), not exactly one-third. This subtle wording difference is commonly tested.
  • The State Finance Commission's role (Article 243-I) is a high-yield topic — know that it reviews financial position every five years and makes recommendations on tax devolution and grants-in-aid.
  • Be familiar with PESA (1996) — it extends the provisions of Panchayats to Scheduled Areas with modifications, giving Gram Sabhas significant powers over resource management and dispute resolution in tribal areas.
  • Current affairs linkage: Questions may connect Panchayati Raj with recent government initiatives like the Aspirational Blocks Programme, e-Panchayat mission, or the Swachh Bharat Mission-Gramin implementation through local bodies.
  • For statement-based questions (common in GPSC Mains GS paper), practice framing answers that distinguish between constitutional mandate, legislative action, and ground-level implementation — this three-layer approach demonstrates analytical depth expected at the GPSC level.

Common traps

  • Confusing the 73rd Amendment with the 74th Amendment: The 73rd deals with Rural Local Bodies (Panchayats), while the 74th deals with Urban Local Bodies (Municipalities). Both were passed in 1992 but came into force on different dates.
  • Mistaking the 73rd Amendment date: It received Presidential assent on 20 May 1992 and came into force on 24 April 1993 — do not confuse these dates with the 74th Amendment (assent 20 May 1992, effective 1 June 1992).
  • Incorrectly stating that reservation for OBCs in Panchayats is constitutionally mandated: Article 243D only mandates reservation for SCs, STs, and women. Reservation for Other Backward Classes (OBCs) in Panchayats was added later by various state laws and through the 102nd/106th Amendments context — not by the original 73rd Amendment text.
  • Assuming the State Election Commission conducts both Panchayat and Municipal elections: The State Election Commission (Article 243K) conducts Panchayat elections; Municipal elections are conducted by the same SEC but under Article 243ZA — however, the SEC is a single constitutional body for both rural and urban local bodies.
  • Thinking that Gram Sabha is a constitutional body created by the 73rd Amendment: The 73rd Amendment mentions Gram Sabha in Article 243A but does not create it de novo; Gram Sabhas existed before 1992. The Amendment gives them a constitutional recognition but their powers are largely framed by state legislation.
  • Confusing Article 243M exceptions: Article 243M(1) carves out special provisions for Scheduled Areas under the Fifth Schedule and tribal areas of Northeast — states can modify the application of Part IX in these areas. This does NOT mean Panchayats don't exist in these areas; rather, they operate under different legal frameworks (e.g., Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 — PESA).
  • Believing that PESA (1996) amended the 73rd Amendment: PESA is a separate statute passed by Parliament that modifies the application of Part IX in Scheduled Areas. It does not amend the Constitution itself.
  • Assuming that all states have adopted the three-tier system uniformly: Some states have two-tier systems (e.g., Sikkim, Mizoram), and several states have experimented with variations. Gujarat follows the three-tier model, but the constitutional provision (Article 243B) says 'may be established' — it is permissive, not mandatory in all respects.
  • Thinking that the District Planning Committee (DPC) is part of the Panchayati Raj system: The DPC is constituted under Article 243ZC (74th Amendment, Part IXA), not Part IX. It is a separate body that coordinates between Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • Confusing the recommendation of the 14th Finance Commission with the 15th: The 14th Finance Commission (2015–2020) recommended 5% of divisible pool for local bodies (up from 0% earlier). The 15th Finance Commission (2021–2026) maintained this 5% devolution but provided specific guidelines for distribution between Panchayats and Municipalities.
