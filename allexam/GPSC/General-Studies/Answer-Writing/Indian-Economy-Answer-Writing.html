Discuss the major structural reforms undertaken by India since the liberalisation of 1991. How have they shaped India's economic trajectory over the last three decades?

Suggested points

  • Introduction: Context of 1991 balance of payments crisis and the role of Narasimha Rao–Manmohan Singh government in initiating reforms.
  • Licensing abolition: Phase-out of the Licence Raj; removal of industrial licensing for most sectors; simplification of company laws.
  • Trade liberalisation: Reduction in tariff rates, dismantling of quantitative restrictions, establishment of a more open trade regime.
  • Financial sector reforms: Strengthening of banking regulation, capital market development, establishment of SEBI and IRDAI.
  • Privatisation and disinvestment: Strategic sale of PSU stakes, closure of loss-making PSUs, shift from ownership to oversight model.
  • Fiscal reforms: FRBM Act attempts to contain fiscal deficit; GST implementation as a landmark indirect tax reform.
  • Impact on growth: Average GDP growth acceleration post-reforms; rise in foreign exchange reserves; integration with global economy.
  • Critical evaluation: Persistent regional and sectoral disparities; inadequate job creation despite growth; informal sector vulnerabilities.
  • Conclusion: Reforms laid foundation for macro-stability but required inclusive complementary policies for equitable outcomes.

Examine the significance of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) in transforming India's fiscal federalism. What challenges remain in its implementation?

Suggested points

  • Introduction: GST replaced a fragmented indirect tax structure with a unified national market spanning goods and services.
  • Constitutional amendment: 101st Amendment Act, 2016 enabled simultaneous levy by Centre and States; creation of GST Council.
  • Dual GST structure: CGST for central share, SGST/UTGST for state share, IGST for inter-state supply with input tax credit mechanism.
  • Impact on fiscal federalism: Cooperative federalism through GST Council with equal voting weight for Centre and States combined.
  • Compliance simplification: Single tax regime replacing VAT, excise, service tax; reduction in cascading effect through continuous ITC chain.
  • Revenue performance: Consistent GST collections crossing threshold levels; widening of tax base with e-invoicing and e-way bills.
  • Challenges: Multiple rate structures creating classification disputes; compensation dispute between Centre and States on revenue shortfall.
  • Implementation gaps: Compliance burden on MSMEs; delay in refund processing; disputes over place of supply rules.
  • Conclusion: GST represents a historic step towards a common Indian market requiring further rate rationalisation and process simplification.

Critically evaluate the impact of demonetisation of November 2016 on India's economy. Was it an effective anti-black money measure?

Suggested points

  • Introduction: On 8 November 2016, RBI invalidated Rs 500 and Rs 1000 notes amounting to Rs 15.44 lakh crore, approximately 86% of currency in circulation.
  • Stated objectives: Curbing black money, counterfeit currency, promoting digital transactions, and expanding the tax base.
  • Immediate impact: Severe disruption to cash-dependent informal economy; temporary shutdown of markets, construction, and transport sectors.
  • Black money elimination: Only about Rs 3.57 lakh crore returned; evidence suggests limited permanent black money eradication.
  • Digital payment growth: Rapid increase in UPI transactions, card payments, and mobile wallet usage indicating accelerated financial digitisation.
  • Tax base expansion: Significant rise in PAN applications, income tax returns, and direct tax collection in subsequent years.
  • Formalisation push: Encouraged unorganised businesses to enter formal economy and maintain proper accounting records.
  • Agricultural and rural distress: Hardest hit were small farmers, daily wage earners, and NBFCs dependent on cash flows.
  • Long-term institutional changes:推动 toward cashless economy but did not address structural black money stored in real estate or overseas assets.
  • Conclusion: Demonetisation was a short-shock measure with symbolic impact; structural anti-black money measures require stronger institutional mechanisms.

Analyse the role of Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) in India's economy. Discuss the rationale and challenges of disinvestment policy.

Suggested points

  • Introduction: PSUs are enterprises owned wholly or partly by the Central or State governments, playing strategic roles across sectors.
  • Historical contribution: Industrialisation drive under Five-Year Plans; infrastructure creation in steel, energy, and heavy industries.
  • Strategic sectors: Energy (ONGC, IOCL), defence (BEL, HAL), railways, banking, and telecommunications where private participation is limited.
  • Social objectives: Employment generation, regional balancing, provision of essential services at subsidised rates.
  • Performance concerns: Many PSUs became loss-making due to bureaucratic interference, overstaffing, and operational inefficiencies.
  • Disinvestment policy evolution: From minority stake sales (1990s) to strategic disinvestment and maximum external support policy (MES).
  • Recent approaches: IPO route for CPSEs, strategic partnership model, and focus on Navratna/Maharatna status grants.
  • Revenue vs restructuring debate: Disinvestment revenues used for fiscal consolidation but risks undermining public enterprise capacity.
  • Challenges: Labour union resistance, valuation difficulties, strategic sector sensitivity, and political constraints on full privatisation.
  • Conclusion: PSU reforms should focus on operational autonomy, corporate governance, and selective exit rather than blanket disinvestment.

Discuss the challenges facing Indian agriculture and the measures needed to ensure sustainable and inclusive growth in the sector.

Suggested points

  • Introduction: Agriculture employs nearly half of India's workforce but contributes less than 18% to GDP, indicating severe productivity imbalance.
  • Fragmented landholdings: Declining average farm size limits economies of scale and mechanisation potential.
  • Irrigation dependence: Only about 40% of net sown area is irrigated; rain-fed agriculture remains vulnerable to monsoon variability.
  • Input inefficiencies: Overuse of fertilizers degrading soil health; inadequate adoption of precision farming techniques.
  • Market access problems: APMC monopolies, limited direct marketing, post-harvest losses due to weak supply chains.
  • Credit access: Small farmers depend on informal sources at high interest rates despite institutional credit expansion.
  • Climate change impacts: Erratic rainfall, rising temperatures, and increased pest prevalence threatening crop yields.
  • Policy interventions: PM-KISAN income support, AIMS for agriculture marketing, crop insurance schemes (PMFBY), and FPO promotion.
  • Way forward: Diversification into horticulture and dairy; contract farming legal frameworks; agro-processing clusters; technology diffusion.
  • Conclusion: Sustainable agricultural transformation requires shifting from subsidy-centric to investment-centric approach with market linkages.

Examine India's external sector management over the past decade. How did India manage the 2020 pandemic-induced economic shock compared to previous crises?

Suggested points

  • Introduction: India's external sector encompasses trade balance, current account, capital account, and foreign exchange reserve management.
  • Foreign exchange reserves trajectory: Reserves grew from around USD 250 billion in 2010 to over USD 600 billion before COVID-19.
  • Current account dynamics: Services surplus offsetting goods deficit; remittances remaining the largest invisible earnings source.
  • FDI trends: Consistent inflows despite global slowdown; shift from traditional markets to technology and manufacturing sectors.
  • 2020 pandemic shock: Sudden stop in capital flows, sharp decline in remittances and services exports, oil price collapse.
  • Reserve buffer advantage: Adequate FX reserves provided critical cushion absent during 1991 crisis, preventing currency collapse.
  • RBI intervention: Strategic forex swaps, relaxed external commercial borrowing norms, and LRS modifications to stabilise rupee.
  • Remittances resilience: Despite pandemic, India remained top remittance recipient globally due to diaspora support channels.
  • Structural differences from 1991: Diversified export base, robust IT services sector, and deeper domestic financial markets reduced vulnerability.
  • Conclusion: Prudent external sector management and adequate buffers enabled India to weather COVID-19 better than past crises.

Discuss the concept of demographic dividend in the Indian context. What policy measures are required to convert this demographic advantage into economic growth?

Suggested points

  • Introduction: India's working-age population (15-59 years) constitutes about 67% of total population, presenting a unique demographic opportunity.
  • Demographic dividend definition: Economic growth potential arising from a larger working-age cohort relative to dependents.
  • Age structure advantage: India's median age is approximately 28 years versus China's 38 and Japan's 48 years.
  • Savings and investment channel: Larger workforce increases savings rate, boosting capital formation and investment capacity.
  • Labour supply boost: Expanded manpower supports labour-intensive manufacturing and services export competitiveness.
  • Key challenges: Skill mismatch with industry requirements; inadequate education quality reflected in ASER reports.
  • Employment generation gap: Jobless growth pattern where GDP grows without proportionate formal employment creation.
  • Women's workforce participation: Alarmingly low at around 20%, representing massive untapped productive potential.
  • Health and nutrition: Malnutrition indicators and stunting rates affect cognitive development and workforce productivity.
  • Policy measures: NEP 2020 implementation, vocational training expansion through Skill India, Ease of Doing Business for manufacturing, improved healthcare.
  • Conclusion: Demographic dividend is not automatic; without complementary human capital and employment policies, it could become a demographic burden.

Analyse the structural issues in India's banking sector and the reforms undertaken through the bank recapitalisation and insolvency framework.

Suggested points

  • Introduction: India's banking system comprises public sector banks, private banks, foreign banks, and cooperative banks managing savings of over Rs 150 lakh crore.
  • Gross NPA crisis: Gross NPAs rose sharply peaking around 11.2% in FY2019 due to overdue identification and corporate borrowing stress.
  • Root causes: Lax lending standards, political economy pressures, infrastructure project delays, and accounting misclassification before 2015.
  • RBI corrective measures: Enhanced disclosure norms, prompt corrective action framework, and unified moratorium guidance.
  • Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016: Created time-bound resolution mechanism; restored creditor rights and improved recovery environment.
  • Bank recapitalisation: Government announced Rs 2.11 lakh crore two-phase package for PSBs to restore capital adequacy ratios.
  • Consolidation trend: Merger of 10 PSBs into 12 entities reducing number of banks and improving scale efficiency.
  • Emerging challenges: Rising rural NBFC stress post-2019, liquidity constraints during COVID, and continued asset quality monitoring needs.
  • Private bank resilience: Private banks maintained healthier balance sheets due to earlier risk management practices.
  • Conclusion: Banking sector reforms represent significant progress but sustained vigilance on asset quality and governance remains essential.

Evaluate the impact of India's fiscal deficit management on macroeconomic stability. Discuss the effectiveness of FRBM framework in constraining fiscal imbalances.

Suggested points

  • Introduction: Fiscal deficit represents the government's total borrowing requirement and is a key indicator of fiscal health and sustainability.
  • FRBM Act 2003 objectives: Reduce fiscal deficit, consolidate government finances, and promote macroeconomic stability through defined targets.
  • Target trajectory: Original targets of 3% general government fiscal deficit by 2008 were repeatedly missed due to growth slowdowns and revenue shortfall.
  • State-level compliance: Fiscal responsibility laws at state level showed mixed adherence; some states consistently breached their limits.
  • Pandemic fiscal response: Fiscal deficit widened to 9.5% in FY2021 as government prioritised economic stimulus over deficit consolidation.
  • Revenue deficit concerns: Persistent revenue deficits indicate structural dependency on borrowings for current expenditure rather than capital formation.
  • Capital expenditure bias: Recent budgets emphasised capex over subsidies to crowd-in private investment and build productive infrastructure.
  • Criticisms of FRBM: Rigid targets ignored cyclical realities; permitted off-budget borrowing through CPSEs and PPP mode bypassing transparency.
  • Effective fiscal stance: Consideration of interest payments in fiscal deficit measurement would provide more realistic sustainability assessment.
  • Conclusion: FRBM provided useful discipline framework but requires flexibility for crises and comprehensive accounting of all government liabilities.

Discuss India's inclusive growth agenda with reference to poverty alleviation programmes and the multidimensional nature of deprivation.

Suggested points

  • Introduction: Inclusive growth implies equitable distribution of economic gains ensuring all sections participate in and benefit from development.
  • Poverty measurement evolution: Shift from headcount ratio to Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) capturing health, education, and living standards deprivations.
  • MPI estimates: India reduced multidimensional poverty significantly with about 41.5% of population exiting MPI between 2005-06 and 2015-16.
  • MGNREGA: Largest public works programme guaranteeing 100 days employment; acts as social security floor and rural asset creator.
  • National Food Security Act 2013: Legal right to subsidised foodgrains covering two-thirds population through PDS network.
  • Direct Benefit Transfers: DBT revolution redirected subsidy leakages by channeling benefits directly to beneficiaries' bank accounts.
  • Ayushman Bharat: Health insurance coverage for approximately 500 million beneficiaries representing world's largest health scheme.
  • Persistent challenges: Tribal and Dalit communities face disproportionate deprivation; gender disparity in human development indicators remains stark.
  • Urban inclusive gaps: PMAY-U, urban MGNREGA extension, and affordable housing shortage indicate incomplete urban inclusion framework.
  • Conclusion: India has made substantial progress in inclusion but must address quality of public service delivery and deepening of capability deprivation.
