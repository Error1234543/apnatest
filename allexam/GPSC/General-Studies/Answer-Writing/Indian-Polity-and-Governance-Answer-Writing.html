Discuss how the Preamble of the Indian Constitution operates as a mirror of the Constitution’s basic structure and guiding philosophy. In your answer, refer to relevant Supreme Court judgments that have interpreted the Preamble’s operative significance.

Suggested points

  • Preamble as an expression of the Constitution’s basic philosophy and objectives
  • Key terms: Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic, Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity
  • Historical context: Nehru’s Objectives Resolution (1946) as the origin
  • Supreme Court evolution: Berubari Union (1960) held Preamble non-justiciable; Kesavananda Bharati (1973) held it part of the basic structure; LIC of India v. N.U. Pal (1995) reaffirmed basic-structure status; IR Co. v. K.C. Aluka (2007) further strengthened Preamble’s interpretative role
  • Preamble as a tool of constitutional interpretation when provisions are ambiguous
  • Relationship between Preamble and Fundamental Rights/DPSPs
  • Conclusion: Preamble as the ‘key to understanding’ the Constitution, not merely a declaratory statement

Critically examine the scope and limitations of the Right to Privacy as a fundamental right under Article 21, tracing its jurisprudential development from Kharak Singh to the Puttaswamy judgment. How has this judgment influenced subsequent legislative and executive actions in India?

Suggested points

  • Pre-Puttaswamy jurisprudence: Kharak Singh (1962), M.P. Sharma (1954) — no standalone right to privacy
  • Girish Sarna (1967), Peeter Kalburgi (1981) — limited privacy recognition in specific contexts
  • K.S. Puttaswamy v. UOI (2017): nine-judge bench unanimously held privacy is a fundamental right under Articles 14, 17, 19 and 21
  • Test of permissible restriction: legitimacy of state aim, proportionality, procedural propriety
  • Impact on legislation: striking down Section 377 IPC (Navtej Johar, 2018); Aadhaar judgment (2018) — upheld but with safeguards; Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 framed in privacy context
  • Impact on executive: regulation of surveillance powers, criminal procedure amendments, data-protection frameworks
  • Limitations and ongoing debates: national security exceptions, lack of comprehensive data-protection law until 2023, balancing with social rights
  • Conclusion: Puttaswamy transformed Article 21 jurisprudence but its full implementation remains a work in progress

Examine the role and evolving jurisdiction of the Supreme Court of India as the guardian of the Constitution and interpreter of fundamental rights. Discuss how the collegium system reflects judicial independence and analyse the constitutional basis of Judicial Activism in India.

Suggested points

  • Supreme Court as constitutional court: Article 124, original, appellate and advisory jurisdictions
  • Guardian of the Constitution: power of judicial review under Articles 13, 32, 226 (high courts)
  • Public Interest Litigation (PIL) expansion: PIL relaxed locus standi, enabled social justice enforcement
  • Collegium system evolution: First Judges Case (1981), Second Judges Case (1993 — SPSC judgment established collegium), Third Judges Case (1998 — clarified collegium composition), NJAC case (2015) struck down 98th Amendment, reaffirmed collegium
  • Article 124(2) — appointment by President after consultation with judges; Article 217 for HC judges
  • Judicial Activism vs. Judicial Overreach: difference between proactive interpretation and encroachment on executive/legislative domains
  • Landmark activism cases: Vishaka (1997) on sexual harassment, Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984) on bonded labour, Rural Litigation Entitlement Kendra (1985) on environmental protection
  • Criticism: lack of accountability, transparency deficits in collegium, potential for judicial policy-making
  • Conclusion: SC’s activism has been essential for rights enforcement but requires checks to maintain institutional balance

Evaluate the impact of the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts on grassroots democracy and local governance in India. How have these amendments addressed historical exclusions, and what are the major implementation gaps that persist today?

Suggested points

  • Pre-amendment context: local bodies existed but lacked constitutional status and autonomy
  • 73rd Amendment (1992): added Part IX and Schedule XI — Gram Sabhas, Panchayats at three tiers, reservation for SC/ST/Women, five-year tenure, State Election Commission, State Finance Commission
  • 74th Amendment (1992): added Part IXA and Schedule XII — Municipalities (Nagar Panchayat, Nagar Palika Parishad, Nagar Nigam), similar constitutional safeguards
  • 29 subjects in Eleventh Schedule for panchayats; 18 subjects in Twelfth Schedule for municipalities
  • Reservation achievements: over one-third women representation transformed gender dynamics in local governance
  • Implementation gaps: incomplete devolution of functions, funds and functionaries (3Fs); delay in State Finance Commission recommendations; weak Gram Sabha functioning; political interference in elected bodies
  • Special provisions: Scheduled Areas (Part X, 5th Schedule) and Tribal Areas (6th Schedule) exempted or modified
  • Recent developments: e-governance in panchayats, convergence schemes, digital transparency platforms
  • Conclusion: amendments provided robust legal framework but substantive decentralisation remains unrealised

Analyze the relationship between the Governor and the State Council of Ministers under the Indian Constitution. Discuss the controversial uses of gubernatorial discretion and recommend measures to prevent the office from becoming an instrument of political executive dominance.

Suggested points

  • Constitutional framework: Article 153 (Governor), Article 163 (Council of Ministers), Article 164 (ministers hold pleasure of Governor)
  • Governor as constitutional head: Article 163(1) — bound by aid and advice except where Constitution expressly requires discretionary exercise
  • Discretionary powers: Article 163 and 174 — reporting to President under Article 356, assent to bills, reservation of bills, ordinance promulgation
  • B.K. Singhal v. UOI (1973): discretionary powers narrowly construed
  • S.R. Bommai v. UOI (1994): Governor’s report under Article 356 subject to judicial review; must be based on objective material
  • Controversial uses: withholding assent to state bills, recommending President’s Rule without council meeting, indefinite suspension of assembly, biased reporting
  • Nabhabrata Biswas v. UOI (2024) SC judgment: directed timely consideration of bills and transparent processes
  • Recommendations: Sarkaria Commission (1988), Punchhi Commission (2010) — fixed tenure, removed Article 356 abuse, require consultation before appointment, codify discretionary exercise
  • Article 156: Governor holds pleasure of President, tenure insecurity, arbitrary removal
  • Conclusion: Governor’s office needs reform to restore constitutional morality and prevent partisanship

Discuss the doctrinal evolution of the Basic Structure doctrine in Indian constitutional law. How has it served as a check on majoritarian impulses while also raising concerns about unamendable constitution-making?

Suggested points

  • Origin: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) — 13-judge bench, 7:6 majority established basic structure doctrine
  • Core holding: Parliament can amend any provision under Article 368 but cannot alter the basic structure of the Constitution
  • Earlier positions: Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) — amendments are ‘law’ under Article 13; Golaknath (1967) — fundamental rights不可amendable
  • Basic structure elements identified by courts: supremacy of Constitution, republican/democratic form, secular character, separation of powers, federal特征, rule of law, judicial review, dignity of individual, free and fair elections, harmony between FRs and DPSPs
  • Post-Kesavananda development: Minerva Mills (1980) — struck down 42nd Amendment clauses giving unlimited amendment power; Waman Rao (1981); Ilahi炳 v. Union of India (1995); L. Chakamma (2000)
  • Criticism: doctrine is judge-made, lacks textual basis in Constitution, creates unamendable core limiting democratic will, inconsistent application by courts
  • Defense: necessary to protect constitutional identity against majoritarian excess, preserves constitutional democracy
  • Conclusion: basic structure doctrine remains the most significant judicial innovation in Indian constitutionalism, balancing amendment power with constitutional integrity

Explain the constitutional mechanism for addressing financial federalism in India. How do the recommendations of successive Finance Commissions shape Centre-State fiscal relations, and what reforms are needed to address emerging challenges?

Suggested points

  • Constitutional provisions: Articles 268–293 deal with distribution of revenues between Union and States
  • Article 270: taxes shared between Centre and States; Article 272: taxes collected by Centre but belong to States; Article 275: grants-in-aid; Article 280: Finance Commission
  • Finance Commission: Article 280 — recommends tax devolution, grant distribution, principles governing vertical and horizontal imbalances
  • 15th Finance Commission (2015–2020): increased devolution share to 41% (highest yet), introduced new criteria (population 2011, area, income distance, forest cover, demographics)
  • GST Council: Article 279A — co-operative federalism through integrated indirect tax system; 50:50 CGST-SGST split for states
  • Vertical imbalance: Centre collects more taxes, States bear more expenditure responsibilities
  • Horizontal imbalance: large disparities among states in fiscal capacity; equalization principle applied by Finance Commissions
  • Challenges: declining state fiscal space, over-reliance on central transfers, GST compensation dispute, rising state debt, pandemic-induced fiscal stress
  • Reform recommendations: stronger state own-tax effort, transparent conditional grants, review of GST compensation mechanism, institutionalizing fiscal responsibility laws at state level
  • Conclusion: financial federalism requires continuous recalibration to maintain Centre-State equilibrium

Critically examine the role of the Election Commission of India in ensuring free and fair elections. Discuss the constitutional safeguards for its independence and the arguments advanced for and against the proposed Election Commission Act, 2023.

Suggested points

  • ECI’s constitutional status: Article 324 — superintendence, direction and control of elections; independent body with Chief Election Commissioner and ECs
  • Security of tenure: CEC and ECs removable only on grounds and manner similar to Supreme Court judges (Article 324(5))
  • Financial independence: expenses charged on Consolidated Fund of India
  • ECI’s powers: recognition of political parties, symbol allocation, model code of conduct enforcement, election monitoring
  • Landmark contributions: Model Code of Conduct institutionalization, VVPAT introduction, nano-VD ballot paper, transparent election financing regulations
  • Election Commission (Determination of Date of Elections) Act, 2023: allows Central Government to determine election dates during emergency/provisional proclamation
  • Arguments for 2023 Act: government claims it provides clarity during emergencies, prevents ECI delay tactics, aligns with parliamentary sovereignty tradition
  • Arguments against: undermines ECI’s independence, executive gains undue influence over election timing, departs from established constitutional convention of ECI autonomy
  • Supreme Court intervention in 2023: struck down the Act, restored pre-2023 appointment committee (PM, Leader of Opposition, Cabinet Minister) composition
  • Conclusion: institutional independence of ECI is essential for electoral credibility in a democracy

Analyse the concept of‘concurrent list’in Indian federalism. How does the concurrent list reconcile unitary and federal characteristics of the Indian Constitution, and what disputes arise from its operation in practice?

Suggested points

  • Three-list federal structure: Union List (98 subjects), State List (59 subjects), Concurrent List (52 subjects) — Seventh Schedule
  • Concurrent list enables both Centre and States to legislate on same subjects, reflecting ‘quasi-federal’ character
  • Article 254: Centre’s law prevails in case of repugnancy; State law with Presidential assent can prevail in that State; subsequent Centre law overrides State law even with Presidential assent
  • Subjects of concurrent importance: education, forests, marriage and divorce, adoption and succession, trade unions, economic and social planning, weights and measures
  • Unitary bias: Article 356 (President’s Rule), single constitution, all-India services, emergency provisions, Reserve Bank control — concurrent list adds to this tendency
  • Federal flexibility: States can experiment with legislation tailored to local needs within concurrent subjects
  • Disputes: frequent Centre-State立法冲突 over interpretation, overlapping regulatory regimes (e.g., education, environment), center’s dominant position in conflict resolution
  • Judicial role: Supreme Court resolves repugnancy disputes using harmonious construction principle
  • Conclusion: concurrent list is a pragmatic compromise but tends to tilt towards Centre due to override mechanism

Evaluate the effectiveness of anti-defection law in preserving political stability versus undermining democratic accountability. Discuss the constitutional amendments, key judicial interpretations, and ongoing demands for reform.

Suggested points

  • Anti-defection law: 52nd Amendment Act, 1985 — added Tenth Schedule to Constitution
  • Objective: curb political instability caused by frequent defections post-Emergency
  • Grounds for disqualification: voluntary giving up membership of party, voting/abstaining contrary to party whip (with exception for merger of one-third group)
  • Exceptions: pre-poll mergers, Whip issued before vote but member votes contrary — no disqualification if party condones within 30 days
  • Decision authority: Speaker/Chairman of respective houses — now subject to judicial review per Kihoto Hollohan (1992)
  • Supreme Court in S. R. Bose v. Speaker, Lok Sabha (2023): disqualified MP voted ‘for’ despite whipping ‘against’; reinforced strict interpretation
  • Criticism: suppresses legitimate dissent, turns MPs into rubber stamps, Speaker’s partisan role in deciding cases, no time limit for Speaker’s decision
  • Demand for reform: time-bound disposal of defection cases, removal of Speaker’s sole discretion, permitting conscience-based voting on crucial issues
  • Effectiveness: reduced frequency of floor-testing but also suppressed legitimate opposition within parties
  • Conclusion: law achieved stability at cost of intra-party democracy; needs balance between accountability and defection prevention
