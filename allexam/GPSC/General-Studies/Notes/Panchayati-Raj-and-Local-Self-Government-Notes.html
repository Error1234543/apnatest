Panchayati Raj and Local Self-Government — Complete Notes for GPSC

GPSCEnglishNotes

Introduction and Historical Background

Local self-government in India has ancient roots, dating back to the Vedic period when village panchayats functioned as autonomous units of governance. The modern Panchayati Raj system, however, emerged from the colonial critique and post-independence nation-building efforts. The Committee on National Development (1948) first discussed decentralization, but the landmark moment came with the Balwant Mehta Committee (1957), which recommended a three-tier Panchayti Raj structure: Gram Panchayat at the village level, Panchayat Samiti at the block level, and Zila Parishad at the district level. Jawaharlal Nehru inaugurated the Panchayati Raj on October 2, 1959, in Nagaur, Rajasthan. Despite early enthusiasm, the system stagnated in most states by the 1970s due to political apathy, inadequate funding, and bureaucratic dominance. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act of 1992 gave constitutional status to Panchayats, transforming them from advisory bodies into institutions of self-government. For Gujarat specifically, the Gujarat Panchayat Act, 1961 was amended several times before the constitutional mandate reshaped the entire framework.

Constitutional Framework — 73rd and 74th Amendment Acts

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, effective from April 24, 1993, added Part IX (Articles 243 to 243O) and the Eleventh Schedule (29 functional items) to the Constitution. It covers rural local self-government (Panchayats). The 74th Amendment Act, 1993, added Part IXA (Articles 243P to 243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule (18 functional items) for urban local bodies (Municipalities). Key structural provisions under the 73rd Amendment include: a mandatory three-tier system (Article 243B); Gram Sabha as the foundation (Article 243A); direct elections to all panchayat levels (Article 243D); reservation of seats for SCs/STs in proportion to their population and at least one-third for women (Article 243D); an State Election Commission to conduct panchayat elections (Article 243K); a State Finance Commission every five years to recommend fiscal devolution (Article 243T); a five-year tenure with no dissolution without fresh elections (Article 243E); and a composite list of 29 functions (Eleventh Schedule) including agriculture, minor irrigation, rural housing, drinking water, literacy, and poverty alleviation programs. The 74th Amendment similarly mandates urban local bodies (Nagar Palikas, Nagar Panchayats, and Maha Nagar Palikas) with 18 specified functions in the Twelfth Schedule covering economic/social development, civic amenities, and infrastructure.

Structure of Panchayati Raj in India

The three-tier structure mandated under Article 243B operates as follows:

• Gram Panchayat (Village Level): The basic unit comprising a PanchayatSamiti area. It consists of elected ward members (panchas), each representing a territorial constituency within the village. The head is the Pradhan (Sarpanch), directly elected by villagers. There may also be nominated members from Anglo-Indian community if representation warrants.

• Panchayat Samiti (Block Level): Also called Block Panchayat, it covers an entire block. Members include elected panchas from Gram Panchayats within the block, MPs and MLAs from the area, and other nominated/ ex-officio members. The head is the Prasanth or Chairman.

• Zila Parishad (District Level): The apex body at the district level, comprising Chairmen of all Panchayat Samitis in the district, MPs, MLAs, and other nominated members. The District Collector typically serves as the executive head. The head is the Zila Parishad Chairperson.

Additionally, Article 243C provides for a Chairperson of the Gram Panchayat to preside over meetings of the constituent Panchas. Article 243P defines Gram Sabha as an assembly of persons registered in the electoral roll of a village, serving as the deliberative and oversight body.

Gujarat follows this three-tier model through its 18 districts, 224 blocks, and thousands of Gram Panchayats, with approximately 77% of the rural population covered under this system.

Gram Sabha — Powers, Functions, and Significance

The Gram Sabha is the foundational democratic institution under Article 243A. It is constituted by enrolling all voters in a village or across several villages. Its significance cannot be overstated: it is the primary platform for grassroots democracy where citizens directly participate in decision-making.

Key functions and powers include:
• Reviewing the annual accounts of the Gram Panchayat and approving expenditure
• Recommending beneficiaries for various government schemes and welfare programmes
• Discussing the annual budget and plan of works of the Gram Panchayat
• Ensuring accountability and transparency in local governance
• Identifying beneficiaries for ration cards, pensions, and other social security measures
• Monitoring the implementation of government programmes at the village level
• Preventing corruption and misappropriation of funds

In Gujarat, Gram Sabhas are increasingly being used for social auditing under the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA). The Right to Information Act further strengthens Gram Sabha oversight. However, practical challenges remain: low attendance, dominance of local elites, and limited awareness among women and marginalized communities hinder effective functioning.

Elections, Reservation, and Representation

Under Article 243D, elections to all panchayats are conducted by the State Election Commission (Article 243K), an independent constitutional authority headed by a State Election Commissioner appointed by the Governor. Key electoral provisions:

• Direct elections: All panchas at every tier are directly elected by the people.
• Reservation of seats: Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs) in proportion to their population. Not less than one-third of total seats (including those reserved for SCs/STs) shall be reserved for women.
• Reservation of office of Chairpersons: At least one-third of the total positions of Chairpersons (including SC/ST) shall be reserved for women, following the pattern of seat reservations.
• Term: Five years from the date of election. No panchayat can be dissolved before the expiry of its term unless dissolved by a legislative law on grounds of dissolution.
• Disqualifications: Governed by state law; persons convicted of certain offenses, holding office of profit, or declared unsound mind may be disqualified.

In Gujarat, the reservation policy has been particularly progressive. As of recent cycles, approximately 50% of Sarpanch positions are held by women — exceeding the constitutional minimum of one-third. This has significantly altered the gender dynamics of rural politics. The reservation pattern rotates after each delimitation exercise, and seats are reserved for SC/ST categories based on their population concentration in specific areas.

Powers, Functions, and Responsibilities of Panchayats

The Eleventh Schedule enumerates 29 functional items that panchayats may be endowed with by state legislation. In Gujarat, the Gujarat Panchayat Act, 1961 (as amended) vests powers across these domains:

Core functions include:
• Economic development and social justice: Implementation of poverty alleviation schemes, preparation of plans for economic development and social justice
• Agriculture and rural infrastructure: Promotion of cottage industries, minor irrigation, water management, agricultural extension
• Social services: Education (especially primary education), adult literacy, vocational training, healthcare and sanitation, maternal and child health, family welfare
• Public utilities: Drinking water, fire services, cemeteries and crematoriums, rural housing, public distribution systems
• Welfare of vulnerable groups: Welfare of women and children, welfare of weaker sections including SCs/STs and persons with disabilities
• Cultural and environmental: Libraries, cultural centers, preservation of local arts, prevention of cruelty to animals, forest management (in some states)

Governance mechanisms include:
• Gram Panchayat: Manages village-level development, maintains public property, collects local taxes, implements central and state schemes
• Panchayat Samiti: Coordinates between Gram Panchayats and Zila Parishad, oversees block-level planning, implements regional schemes
• Zila Parishad: District-level planning and coordination, approves block plans, allocates resources, monitors overall district development

Financial powers include levying and collecting taxes on properties, lands, wells, and professions; receiving grants-in-aid from the state and central governments; and managing funds allocated under various schemes.

Finance and Fiscal Devolution

Article 243T mandates the constitution of a State Finance Commission (SFC) every five years to review the financial position of panchayats and recommend:
• The principles governing the distribution between the state and panchayats of net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls, and fees assignable to panchayats
• The formation of taxes, duties, tolls, and fees that may be assigned to panchayats
• Grants-in-aid from the state consolidated fund to panchayats
• Measures to improve panchayat finances through rationalization

Revenue sources for panchayats include:
• Own revenues: Property tax, water tax, profession tax, market fees, registration fees, rent from panchayat properties
• State share: Share in state taxes and duties as recommended by SFC
• Central and state grants: Funds under Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) and State Sponsored Schemes (SSS)
• Specific scheme allocations: MGNREGA, Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana — Gramin (PMAY-G), Swachh Bharat Mission — Gramin (SBM-G), etc.

The Fourteenth Finance Commission (2015–2020) recommended 40% of GST divisible pool go to local bodies (shared between urban and rural), significantly enhancing fiscal federalism. Gujarat's State Finance Commission recommendations have progressively increased devolution, though implementation varies across districts. Recent reports indicate that panchayat own-source revenue remains low, making them heavily dependent on transfers.

Urban Local Bodies — Constitutional Provisions

Urban local self-government is governed by the 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1993, under Part IXA (Articles 243P to 243ZG). Three types of municipal bodies exist:

• Nagar Nigam (Municipal Corporation): For large urban areas with population generally above one million. Has a mayor, commissioner, and municipal council.
• Nagar Palika (Municipal Council): For smaller urban areas with populations between 50,000 and one million.
• Nagar Panchayat (Nasha Premises Committee): For transitional areas transitioning from rural to urban.

Twelfth Schedule lists 18 functions: urban planning, water supply, fire services, urban forestry, burial grounds, cattle pounds, waste management, roads and bridges, public health, sanitation, slum improvement, urban poverty alleviation, protective works, cultural and educational matters.

Gujarat has several municipal corporations including Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation (AMC), Surat Municipal Corporation (SMC), Vadodara Municipal Corporation (VMC), and Rajkot Municipal Corporation. These are among the largest and most resource-rich ULBs in the country. AMC, established in 1950, administers one of India's largest municipal areas and has significant autonomy in taxation and planning.

Gujarat-Specific Panchayati Raj Structure and Developments

Gujarat's Panchayati Raj system is administered under the Gujarat Panchayat Act, 1961, which has undergone multiple amendments to align with the 73rd Amendment. Key features:

• Administrative structure: 18 districts → 224 panchayat blocks → approximately 13,400 Gram Panchayats covering about 77% of the state's population.
• Total panchayat seats: Over 80,000 elected representatives including ward members, chairpersons, and sarpanchs.
• Women's representation: Gujarat has been a national leader in women's political participation at the grassroots. Approximately 50% of pradhan positions are held by women, far exceeding the constitutional one-third requirement.
• Digital governance: Gujarat has implemented e-governance initiatives in panchayats, including online grievance redressal, digital accounting systems, and online scheme beneficiary databases.
• Social audit: Regular social audits under MGNREGA have strengthened Gram Sabha accountability.
• District Planning Committees (DPCs): Constituted under Article 243ZA to consolidate plans prepared by panchayats and municipalities for sustainable local development.

Notable developments include the Gujarat Gramonnati Nidhi (GGN) for fund transfer to Gram Panchayats, the installation of solar-powered streetlights in thousands of villages under PMAY-G, and the Jal Jeevan Mission aiming to provide tap water connections to every rural household.

Challenges and Reform Directions

Despite constitutional recognition, panchayats face persistent challenges:

• Functional overload without adequate power transfer: Many departments continue to bypass panchayats, retaining control over key functions.
• Financial dependency: Own revenue collection remains minimal; heavy reliance on conditional and earmarked grants limits discretionary spending.
• Capacity deficits: Elected representatives, especially women and those from marginalized backgrounds, often lack training in governance, accounting, and planning.
• Bureaucratic dominance: The role of the District Collector and block-level officers sometimes undermines elected leadership.
• Inadequate representation: Despite reservations, actual decision-making power of reserved-category chairpersons can be constrained by informal power structures.
• Political interference: State governments occasionally delay elections, imposeadministrators, or dissolve panchayats on flimsy grounds.

Reform directions recommended by various committees and observers include:
• Full functional devolution as per the 11th Schedule with clear division of responsibilities
• Timely release of finance commission recommendations and grants
• Mandatory capacity-building programs for elected representatives
• Strengthening Gram Sabhas through regular meetings and social audits
• Greater use of technology for transparent governance and service delivery
• Empowering panchayats to levy and collect their own taxes more effectively
• Ensuring timely elections and preventing arbitrary dissolution

Important Schemes and Programs Linked to Panchayats

Several flagship schemes are implemented through the panchayati raj system:

• Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), 2005: Guarantees 100 days of wage employment annually to rural households. Gram Sabhas identify beneficiaries and work sites; panchayats oversee implementation. Social audits are conducted at the gram panchayat level.
• Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana — Gramin (PMAY-G): Provides financial assistance for constructing pucca houses to homeless families in rural areas. Beneficiary selection is done by Gram Sabhas.
• Swachh Bharat Mission — Gramin (SBM-G): Focuses on eliminating open defecation and solid waste management in rural areas. Gram Panchayats are the implementing agencies.
• Jal Jeevan Mission: Aims to provide functional tap water connections to every rural household by 2024. Gram Panchayats play a key role in identification and maintenance.
• National Health Mission (NHM): Panchayats coordinate with ANMs and ASHA workers for health outreach.
• Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS): Anganwadi centers operate at the gram panchayat level.
• Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana — National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM): Supports Self Help Groups (SHGs) at the panchayat level for livelihood generation.

These schemes demonstrate the critical linkage between central/state policy and grassroots implementation through panchayats.

  • Part IX of the Constitution (Articles 243–243O) covers Panchayats; Part IXA (Articles 243P–243ZG) covers Municipalities.
  • Eleventh Schedule contains 29 functional items for panchayats; Twelfth Schedule has 18 items for urban local bodies.
  • At least one-third of panchayat seats and chairperson positions are reserved for women.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the 73rd Amendment (rural panchayats) with the 74th Amendment (urban municipalities).
  • Forgetting that reservation for women applies not only to seats but also to chairperson positions (one-third of both).
  • Assuming panchayats have independent taxing power — they can only levy taxes as permitted by state legislation.
  • Overlooking that State Election Commissioner, not the Election Commission of India, conducts panchayat elections.
  • Mistaking the role of the District Collector, who is an ex-officio member of the Zila Parishad but does not preside over it.
  • Assuming Gram Sabha is the same as Gram Panchayat — Gram Sabha is the entire electorate body; Gram Panchayat is the elected executive committee.
  • Ignoring that finance commissions recommend but do not mandate fiscal devolution — implementation depends on state legislation.
  • Believing panchayats have exclusive jurisdiction over all 29 Eleventh Schedule functions — actually, states decide which functions to devolve.
  • Forgetting that the five-year term provision allows for early dissolution under certain conditions prescribed by state law.
  • Not knowing that Gujarat's women's reservation in panchayats (~50%) exceeds the constitutional floor of one-third.
