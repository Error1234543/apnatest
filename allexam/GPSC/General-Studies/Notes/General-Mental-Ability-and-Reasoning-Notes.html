General Mental Ability and Reasoning — GPSC General Studies

1. Verbal Reasoning — Analogies, Classification and Word Formation

Analogies test your ability to identify the underlying relationship between a given pair of words or concepts and replicate that same relationship in another pair. Common relationship types in GPSC reasoning include: (a) Part to Whole — e.g., Page : Book :: Chapter : Novel; (b) Worker to Tool — e.g., Dentist : Scalpel :: Tailor : Needle; (c) Cause and Effect — e.g., Disease : Germs :: Fire : Heat; (d) Degree/Intensity — e.g., Chill : Freeze :: Boil : Simmer; (e) Place to Thing — e.g., Hospital : Doctor :: Court : Judge; (f) Object to Purpose — e.g., Pen : Write :: Knife : Cut; (g) Synonym/Antonym pairs; and (h) Animal to Young/Den — e.g., Lion : Cub :: Ant : Hill.

For classification questions, you must identify the odd one out from a given set. The key strategy is to look for a single distinguishing property shared by three options but absent in the fourth. GPSC commonly uses: (a) Category classification — e.g., which word does not belong: Mango, Apple, Carrot, Guava (Carrot is a vegetable, others are fruits); (b) Letter-pattern classification — groups sharing a vowel/consonant arrangement or alphabetical positioning; (c) Number-classification — grouping by even/odd, prime/composite, perfect square/cube, digit-sum patterns; (d) Word-meaning classification — one word has a meaning unrelated to the others despite superficial similarity.

2. Number and Alphabet Series

A number series presents a sequence where one or more terms are missing or incorrectly placed. You must detect the governing pattern and supply the correct answer.

Common patterns for GPSC include:

(a) Difference pattern: The difference between consecutive terms follows a sequence. Example: 2, 5, 10, 17, 26, ? → differences are +3, +5, +7, +9, so next is +11 giving 37.

(b) Multiplication/division with addition/subtraction: Each term is obtained by multiplying or dividing by a number and then adding or subtracting another number. Example: 3, 7, 15, 31, ? → pattern is ×2+1, ×2+1, ×2+1, so next is 63.

(c) Square/cube pattern: Terms relate to consecutive squares or cubes. Example: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ? → n², next is 36. Or example: 8, 27, 64, 125, ? → n³, next is 216.

(d) Two-tier series: Two alternate subsequences interleave. Example: 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, ? → first subsequence (+3): 2, 5, 8... second subsequence (+3): 3, 6, 9... next is 11.

(e) Mixed pattern involving primes, factorials, or digit operations. Example: 1, 1, 2, 6, 24, ? → n! (factorial), next is 120.

(f) Fraction/decimal series: Patterns involving recurring decimals or fractions derived from division.

For alphabet series, similarly look for positional shifts (A=1, B=2, ... Z=26), skipping patterns, reverse order, or alternating forward/backward sequences. Memorise forward and backward positions of key alphabets (especially EJOTY for quick reference: E=5, J=10, O=15, T=20, Y=25).

3. Coding and Decoding

Coding-decoding tests your ability to deduce a rule that transforms letters/words into a code and apply it to new items.

(a) Letter-shifting coding: Each letter is shifted by a fixed number of places in the alphabet. Example: if TRAIN is coded as WUDQK, each letter is moved +3 forward. Apply same rule to decode or encode other words.

(b) Pair-reversal coding: Letters within the word are rearranged in pairs or the entire word is reversed. Example: COMPUTER coded as ROMPUTEC (reverse). This is common in GPSC.

(c) Substitution coding: Each letter is replaced by another specific letter consistently throughout. Example: if CAT = XZG, every occurrence of C is X, A is Z, T is G.

(d) Number-letter coding: Letters are converted to numbers using A=1...Z=26 or reverse mapping. Sometimes only the sum of positions is asked.

(e) Word substitution coding: A word is redefined — e.g., 'apple' is called 'mango', 'mango' is called 'banana'. Then the question asks what something actually means under the new naming convention. For instance: 'Dog means Cat, Cat means Mouse, Mouse means Elephant. What does 'Elephant' mean?' Answer: Dog.

(f) Matrix-based coding: Letters are represented by two-digit codes using a matrix grid. Approach by listing possible codes and matching with given options.

Tips: Always write down the forward and sometimes reverse position values quickly. Look for patterns like adjacent letter pairs, mirror-image pairs (A↔Z, B↔Y), or complementary pairs (sum to 27).

4. Blood Relations

Blood relation questions require you to trace family relationships from given information. There are two main formats:

(a) Point-to-point type: A statement describes one person in relation to another, and you must identify the relationship. Example: 'Pointing to a photograph, a man said, "I have no brother or sister, but that man's father is my father's son."' Solve by drawing a small family tree step by step.

(b) Puzzle type: Multiple persons and relationships are described in a paragraph, and you need to find a specific relationship among them.

Key symbols and notation to remember:
- + = Male, - = Female
- × = Sibling relation
- ≠ = Not sibling
- Parents: Father (+), Mother (-)
- Children: Son (+), Daughter (-)
- Spouse: Husband/Wife links the couple
- Generation gap: Each generation is one step apart

Common relationships tested:
- Paternal uncle/aunt vs Maternal uncle/aunt (different sides)
- Brother-in-law/Sister-in-law (spouse's sibling or sibling's spouse)
- Daughter-in-law/Son-in-law
- Nephew/Niece (sibling's child)
- Grandfather/Grandmother (parent's parent)
- Cousin (father's/brother's/mother's/sister's child)
- In-law relationships (through marriage, not blood)

Always draw quick diagrams. Mark gender carefully — it is often the deciding factor between two similar options.

5. Direction Sense and Distance

Direction sense questions involve tracking movement through cardinal directions (North, South, East, West and their intermediates).

Core principle: When moving, always mark the starting point and track each turn/change of direction systematically.

Key directional rules:
- Facing North: Left turn → West, Right turn → East
- Facing South: Left turn → East, Right turn → West (flipped)
- Facing East: Left turn → North, Right turn → South
- Facing West: Left turn → South, Right turn → North

Standard approach:
1. Draw a rough cross indicating N, S, E, W
2. Mark the starting point as 'O'
3. Follow each movement segment on paper
4. After completing all movements, determine the final direction facing and displacement from start

For distance calculation, use Pythagoras theorem when the path forms a right triangle: Distance from origin = √(horizontal² + vertical²).

Example: A person walks 5 km East, turns right (South) and walks 12 km. Distance from start = √(5²+12²) = √(25+144) = √169 = 13 km. Direction from start = South-East.

Shadow problems: In morning (sun in East), shadows fall to the West. In evening (sun in West), shadows fall to the East. If someone faces North in morning, shadow falls left; if faces South, shadow falls right. These questions often combine direction with timing.

6. Seating Arrangement and Puzzle Types

Seating arrangement questions place people in circular, linear, or rectangular arrangements with given constraints.

(a) Linear arrangement: People sit in a row facing North or South. Key rule — if facing South, left/right is reversed from the reader's perspective. Always note which direction each row faces.

(b) Circular arrangement: People sit around a circle. Some face center, some face outward. If mixed, carefully track each person's individual orientation.

(c) Double row arrangement: Two rows of equal/unequal number of people facing each other.

Problem-solving strategy:
1. Read all conditions before drawing anything
2. Draw the basic framework (line, circle, rectangle)
3. Fill in definite positions first (those with no alternatives)
4. Use conditional statements after definite ones are placed
5. Keep an elimination list for ambiguous cases
6. Check each condition against your final arrangement

Common constraint types:
- Immediate neighbour: sits directly beside
- Second to the left/right: one person between
- Facing opposite: sits across in double-row
- Between: with at least one on either side
- Not adjacent: cannot sit together
- Similar cases: two people of same attribute sit together

For puzzle types beyond seating, the same logical elimination method applies — map attributes (name, colour, profession, city) to persons using a table/grid.

7. Syllogism

Syllogism tests logical deduction from given statements. You must determine which conclusions logically follow, ignoring real-world knowledge.

Venn diagram method (most reliable for GPSC):
Draw circles representing sets mentioned in the statements and shade/mark regions to show inclusion/exclusion relationships.

Standard statement types:
- 'All A are B' → A circle completely inside B circle
- 'No A is B' → Two separate non-overlapping circles
- 'Some A are B' → Partial overlap between A and B circles
- 'Some A are not B' → Part of A lies outside B (this also implies 'Some A are B' may or may not hold)

Four conclusion patterns to memorise:

Complementary pairs (Either-Or):
1. Some A are B + No A is B
2. All A are B + Some A are not B
3. All A are C + Some A are not C

Definite conclusions (must follow):
- From 'All A are B' and 'All B are C' → 'All A are C'
- From 'All A are B' and 'No B is C' → 'No A is C'
- From 'Some A are B' and 'All B are C' → 'Some A are C'

Never-follow conclusions:
- If a conclusion requires a case that is NOT covered by any valid Venn diagram, it does not follow.

Quick elimination tips: If 'All A are B' is true, then 'Some B are A' definitely follows. 'Some A are not B' is the complement of 'All A are B'. Be careful with 'Only a few' type statements.

8. Statement, Conclusion and Assumption

These three question types test your ability to evaluate reasoning quality.

Statement + Conclusion:
- The conclusion must be a logical consequence of the statement alone
- Ignore external general knowledge
- Absolute words like 'always', 'never', 'all', 'none' in conclusions are risky unless the statement justifies them
- Words like 'some', 'may', 'possible' are safer and more likely correct
- A conclusion that reverses cause-effect or goes beyond the scope of the statement is invalid

Statement + Assumption:
- An assumption is an unstated premise that MUST be true for the statement to hold
- Test each option: negate it. If negating the option makes the statement meaningless or false, then it was a necessary assumption
- Self-evident facts or universally accepted truths are usually NOT assumptions (they don't need to be assumed)
- Examples of assumptions: policy statements, advice, warnings, proposals — each assumes certain things are desirable or factual

Important distinction:
- A conclusion is something you can derive FROM the statement
- An assumption is something you must take FOR granted TO make the statement valid

Common traps:
- Confusing an assumption with a restatement of the statement
- Treating a likely outcome as an assumption
- Selecting overly broad assumptions that go beyond necessity

9. Calendar and Clock

Calendar questions ask about the day of the week for a given date or the number of odd days between dates.

Key formulas and facts:
- Ordinary year = 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 odd day
- Leap year = 366 days = 52 weeks + 2 odd days
- A leap year occurs if the year is divisible by 4 (except century years which must be divisible by 400)
- 100 years = 76 ordinary + 24 leap = 100×1 + 24×1 extra... Actually: 100 years contain 24 leap years + 76 ordinary = 76+48 = 124 odd days = 17 weeks + 5 odd days → 5 odd days (Friday)
- 200 years = 10 odd days = 3 odd days (Wednesday)
- 300 years = 15 odd days = 1 odd day (Monday)
- 400 years = 0 odd days (Sunday)
- Same cycle repeats every 400 years

Odd-day calculation method: Count total days between two dates, divide by 7, remainder = odd days. Add to known reference day.

Clock angle formula:
- Angle between hour hand and minute hand = |30H − (11/2)M| degrees, where H = hour, M = minute
- In 12 hours, the hands coincide 11 times (not 12 — they coincide at approximately 1:05, 2:10, etc.)
- The hands are at right angles 22 times in 12 hours
- The hands are in a straight line (opposite direction) 22 times in 12 hours
- The hands are in a straight line (same direction) 11 times in 12 hours
- Relative speed of minute hand = 5.5 degrees per minute faster than hour hand
- The hands overlap exactly every 65+5/11 minutes, not every 60 minutes

10. Number, Ranking and Time-Sequence Tests

(a) Ranking and Position: When A is 'p-th from the top and q-th from the bottom in a row, Total persons = p + q − 1. If you need to find how many are between two persons A and B: if total = N, A's position from one end = a, B's position from the same end = b, then persons between = N − a − b (when A and B are counted from opposite ends, the subtraction changes accordingly). For overlapping ranges, careful sign consideration is needed.

(b) Interchanging positions: If A is at position p from left and B at position q from left, and after interchange A moves to B's original position, then Total = p + q − 1. New position of B from other end can be found from this total.

(c) Time-sequence problems: Determine which event occurs earliest/latest among a set of chronological events. Often presented as historical facts, events in a passage, or sequences. Build a timeline from the given data.

(d) Input-output/step-pattern reasoning: Words or numbers are rearranged following a fixed rule across successive steps. Track one element at a time to understand the rule — it could be alphabetical order, ascending/descending numeric order, or alternating front-and-back placement. Identify the step at which arrangement is complete (no further change occurs).

11. Venn Diagram and Set Logic

Venn diagram questions represent relationships between different sets using overlapping circles. For GPSC, focus on identifying which diagram correctly represents the relationship among three given terms.

Common set relationships:
- Completely separate: e.g., Lions, Tigers, Cows (no overlap)
- One inside another: e.g., Mammals ⊃ Dogs ⊃ Labrador (subset hierarchy)
- Partial overlap: e.g., Doctors ∩ Teachers = some who are both doctors and teachers
- Circle inside but touching boundary: representing possible but not universal inclusion
- All overlapping: e.g., Men, Fathers, Doctors (each pair partially overlaps)
- Concentric: Total set contains subset which contains sub-subset

To solve:
1. Read the three terms carefully
2. Determine pairwise relationships
3. Eliminate diagrams showing impossible overlaps
4. Check if all three pairwise relationships are satisfied simultaneously

For set notation questions (less common but appear):
- Union (A ∪ B) = elements in A or B or both
- Intersection (A ∩ B) = elements in both A and B
- Complement (A') = elements not in A
- Difference (A − B) = elements in A but not in B

12. Non-Verbal Reasoning — Mirror Images, Water Images, Paper Folding and Cut Diagrams

(a) Mirror Image: When a figure is placed before a mirror, the reflected image appears laterally inverted (left becomes right, right becomes left). The mirror line's position determines the axis of inversion:
- Mirror at bottom → figure flips vertically (top becomes bottom)
- Mirror at right → figure flips horizontally (left becomes right)
- Mirror at left → same horizontal flip as right mirror
- Mirror above → vertical flip

Key tip: Hold the answer sheet up to the light to check mirror images yourself during the exam.

(b) Water Image: Reflection in water — the image is upside down (vertical inversion only). Top of figure becomes bottom and vice versa. Lateral positions remain unchanged (left stays left).

(c) Paper Folding and Cutting: When a folded paper is cut, the cuts reflect symmetrically upon unfolding. Count the number of layers created by folds:
- Single fold → 2 layers (cut appears twice, mirrored)
- Two perpendicular folds → 4 layers
- Two parallel folds → 3 visible layers on one half
Unfolding produces symmetrical patterns. Practice recognising whether the symmetry is horizontal, vertical, or both.

(d) Embedded Figure: Find the given small figure hidden within a larger complex figure. Scan systematically — do not rush. The embedded figure may be rotated or flipped.

13. Pattern Completion and Figure Classification (Non-Verbal Series)

(a) Pattern completion: A sequence of figures follows a logical transformation rule. Identify the rule by comparing consecutive frames. Common transformations include:
- Rotation: clockwise or anticlockwise by 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°
- Reflection: flipping across an axis
- Addition/Subtraction: a new element appears or disappears in each step
- Movement: elements shift position in a cycle (e.g., positions 1→2→3→1)
- Changing attributes: shape, shading, size, or orientation changes systematically
- Combination/Splitting: two elements merge or one splits into two
- Diagonal/traverse movement: element moves along a path through positions

Strategy: Check each element independently. In complex figures, track each component (dot, line, symbol) separately rather than treating the figure as a whole.

(b) Figure classification: Four or five figures are shown; one does not belong to the group. Find the common property shared by four figures — it could be number of sides, type of angle, symmetry, shading pattern, enclosed regions, rotational property, or intersection count.

(c) Odd-figure-out: Similar to classification but framed differently — identify the single figure that breaks the pattern established by others.

14. Logical Puzzles — Seating, Arrangement with Multiple Parameters

Advanced puzzle questions in GPSC combine several constraints and may involve multiple parameters such as name, colour, profession, and city simultaneously.

Approach for multi-parameter puzzles:
1. Create a table with columns for each parameter and rows for each person
2. Fill in definite information first
3. Use conditional clues to eliminate possibilities
4. Cross-reference: once a person's name is fixed, apply that person's attributes to other clues
5. Work backwards from negative clues ('A is not X') to eliminate rows
6. Look for the most constrained entity — the one with the maximum number of clues mentioning it

Types of multi-parameter puzzles:
- Floor-based puzzles: 7–10 floors, each person lives on a different floor with additional attributes
- Box-stacking puzzles: boxes stacked vertically with weight/colour/content constraints
- Scheduling puzzles: events scheduled across days/hours with conditions
- Group/direction puzzles: people in rows with attributes and direction facing

Tip: Underline every clue as you read. Revisit clues that seemed simple initially — they often become critical once partial deductions are made. Never assume information not explicitly stated.

15. Exam Strategy and Common Question Patterns for GPSC Reasoning

GPSC General Mental Ability and Reasoning in the Prelims test focuses on speed and accuracy. Typical question counts range from 15 to 25 reasoning questions per paper depending on the notification year.

High-yield topics to prioritise based on repeated appearance:
- Blood Relations (usually 2–3 questions)
- Coding-Decoding (2–3 questions)
- Series completion (number/alphabet) (2–3 questions)
- Direction and Distance (1–2 questions)
- Syllogism (1–2 questions)
- Mirror/Water images (1–2 questions)
- Ranking/Position (1–2 questions)
- Calendar/Clock (occasional)
- Statement-Conclusion/Assumption (1–2 questions)
- Seating Arrangement (1 complex set, 3–4 questions)

Time management: Reasoning questions should take 30–60 seconds each. If stuck, mark for review and move on. Do not spend more than 2 minutes on any single question.

Common mistake areas:
- Confusing 'sister-in-law' with 'brother-in-law' — always track gender
- Mixing up 'from left' and 'from right' in ranking problems
- Forgetting the ±1 adjustment in total counting (between vs inclusive)
- Lateral inversion errors in mirror-image questions
- Applying real-world knowledge instead of strict logic in syllogism
- Shadow-direction confusion between morning and evening scenarios

Important facts

  • In a row, if A is p-th from top and q-th from bottom, Total = p + q − 1
  • 1 ordinary year = 1 odd day; 1 leap year = 2 odd days
  • 400-year calendar cycle repeats exactly (0 odd days)
  • Hands of a clock coincide 11 times in 12 hours, not 12
  • Angle between hands formula: |30H − (11/2)M| degrees
  • Mirror image causes lateral inversion; water image causes vertical inversion
  • Paper folded once gives 2 symmetric halves upon unfolding
  • Leap year rule: divisible by 4, except century years divisible by 400
  • Syllogism: 'All A are B' implies 'Some B are A' (conversion)
  • Assumption must be NECESSARILY true — negate test determines this
  • In circular arrangement, clockwise means rightward if facing centre
  • 100 years = 5 odd days; 200 years = 3 odd days; 300 years = 1 odd day

Formulas and relationships

Total persons in row = position from left + position from right − 1
Persons between A and B (non-overlapping) = Total − A_pos − B_pos
Angle between clock hands = |30H − (11/2)M| degrees
Ongoing overlap interval = 65 + 5/11 minutes (not 60 minutes)
Odd days in N years = N + number of leap years within N, mod 7
Mirror reflection: left-right swap; Water reflection: top-bottom swap
Layers from folding: single fold = 2 layers, two perpendicular folds = 4 layers
Circular arrangement clockwise: if facing centre, clockwise is to the right of each person

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to subtract 1 when calculating total from left and right positions — answering N+1 instead of N-1
  • Mixing up mirror image (lateral flip) with water image (vertical flip) — these are fundamentally different transformations
  • In syllogism, accepting conclusions based on real-world knowledge rather than strictly from the given statements
  • In blood relations, confusing in-law relationships with blood relationships — spouse's sibling is not a blood relative
  • In direction sense, failing to account for the person's current facing direction before applying left/right turns — directions reverse when facing South
  • In clock problems, assuming the hands overlap every 60 minutes — they actually overlap every 65 + 5/11 minutes
  • In calendar problems, forgetting the special leap year rule for century years (must be divisible by 400, not just 4)
  • In paper folding-cutting, miscounting the number of layers — each additional fold doubles or adds layers depending on fold type
  • In ranking, treating 'between' inclusively when exclusive is required, or vice versa
  • In statement-assumption, selecting obviously true universal statements as assumptions — assumptions must be specific unstated premises necessary for the statement to hold
