Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization

The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC), also called Harappan Civilization, flourished circa 2600–1900 BCE across present-day India, Pakistan and parts of Afghanistan. It represents one of the three earliest urban civilizations of the world (along with Mesopotamia and Egypt).

• Major sites in India: Harappa (Punjab), Mohenjo-daro (Sindh), Lothal (Gujarat — famous for the ancient dockyard and beads factory), Dholavira (Kutch, Gujarat — known for its water management system and signboard), Kalibangan (Rajasthan), Rakhigarhi (Haryana), Banawali (Haryana), Alamgirpur (UP), Sutkagendor (Balochistan).
• Urban planning: grid-pattern streets, baked-brick construction (ratio 4:2:1), advanced drainage and sanitation systems, citadel and lower town division.
• Economy: agriculture (wheat, barley, peas, sesame), trade with Mesopotamia, craft specialization (bead-making, shell-working, metallurgy — copper, bronze, iron occasionally), domesticated animals (bull, elephant, camel).
• Symbols and script: undeciphered script on seals; most seals are square with animal motifs (unicorn bull, elephant); the 'dancing girl' bronze statue from Mohenjo-daro and the 'Priest-King' bust are iconic finds.
• Decline (c. 1900 BCE): likely multi-causal — climate change, drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra river, tectonic shifts, possible Aryan migration — no single cause is universally accepted.

GPSC relevance: Gujarat hosts two of the most important IVC sites (Lothal and Dholavira, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites since 2021/2022). Expect questions on their unique features.

Vedic Period — Early Vedic and Later Vedic Age

The Vedic period (c. 1500–500 BCE) is divided into the Early Vedic Period (c. 1500–1000 BCE) and the Later Vedic Period (c. 1000–500 BCE).

• Early Vedic Period:
- Geographical focus: Sapta Sindhu region (land of seven rivers — Punjab and Haryana);
- Rigveda (oldest Veda, 1028 hymns) composed during this phase;
- Polity: tribal monarchies; king (Raja) was elected and not absolutely powerful; sabha and samiti were important assemblies;
- Society: relatively flexible varna system based on occupation; women had respectable status, could participate in religious discourses;
- Economy: pastoral (cow as primary wealth — 'gorsha'), limited agriculture;
- Religion: nature worship — Indras (rain/war), Agnis (fire), Varunas (cosmic order), Somas;

• Later Vedic Period:
- Expansion eastward into the Gangetic plain;
- Three Vedas composed: Samaveda (chants), Yajurveda (sacrificial rituals), Atharvaveda (spells and charms);
- Iron discovered and used (Krushna Ayas), enabling forest clearance;
- Varna system became rigid; four-fold varna firmly established;
- Rise of Kshatriya dominance; great epics (Mahabharata, Ramayana) took shape;
- Religious shift: ritualism increased; Yajnas like Ashvamedha, Rajasuya, Vajapeya became prominent;
- Women's status declined; exclusion from Vedic learning became more pronounced;
- Political organization evolved into larger kingdoms (Janapadas). Notable later Vedic kingdoms: Kuru-Panchala, Kosala, Videha, Matsya, Assika-Pakika.

GPSC relevance: Match the Veda with its subject matter; know the geographical shift from Punjab to Gangetic plain between the two phases.

Mahajanapadas, Buddhism, Jainism and Mauryan Empire

• Mahajanapadas (6th century BCE):
- Sixteen Mahajanapadas emerged; capitals included Rajagriha (Magadha), Shravasti (Kosala), Kaushambi (Vatsa), Taxila (Gandhara), Pataliputra (Magadha), Vaishali (Licchavi — a republic/gana-sangha), Champa (Magadha), Ujjain (Avanti).
- Magadha eventually rose to supremacy under the Haryanka, Shishunaga and Nanda dynasties before the Mauryas.

• Origin of Buddhism and Jainism:
- Both arose in the 6th century BCE as Shramana movements reacting against Vedic ritualism and caste rigidity;
- Buddhism: founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha). First Sermon at Sarnath (Dhammachakkappavattana). Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path are core teachings. Buddha died at Kushinagar (c. 483 BCE). Buddhist councils: First at Rajagriha (Ajatashatru patronage), Second at Vaishali, Third at Pataliputra (Ashoka), Fourth at Kashmir (Kanishka);
- Jainism: 24th Tirthankara Lord Mahavira (contemporary of Buddha). Core tenets: triple doctrine (right faith, right knowledge, right conduct), ahimsa, anekantavada. Mahavira died at Pavapuri. Split into Digambara and Svetambara;

• Mauryan Empire (322–185 BCE):
- Chandragupta Maurya (founded empire with Chanakya/Kautilya's help; defeated Seleucus Nicator, acquired Kabul-Afghanistan territories);
- Bindusara: 'Amitraghata'; extended south;
- Ashoka (268–232 BCE): fought Kalinga War (261 BCE) — turned to non-violence after it. Edicts inscribed in Brahmi, Kharosthi, Greek and Aramaic scripts. Dharma policy: welfare state, animal protection, moral codes. Missionary activity spread Buddhism;
- Administration: centralized with detailed provisions in Arthashastra (Kautilya) and Dharmashastra traditions;
- Decline: weak successors, Brahminical reaction, assassination of last Maurya king by Pushyamitra Shunga in 185 BCE.

GPSC relevance: Ashoka's edicts are a major theme. Know locations of major edict sites (Rock Edicts at Dwarka/Girnar in Gujarat, Shahbazgarhi in NW, Maner near Patna).

Post-Mauryan Period — Gupta Empire

• Post-Mauryan developments (200 BCE–300 CE):
- Shakas (Western Kshatrapas): ruled parts of Gujarat and western India; Rudradaman I inscribed the Junagadh rock inscription (Sanskrit, no Brahmi) celebrating repair of Sudarshana lake;
- Kanishka (Kushana): spread Mahayana Buddhism; Fourth Buddhist Council; Gandhara and Mathura schools of art flourished;
- Satavahanas (Andhra): ruled Deccan; Gautamiputra Satakarni was prominent; Nagarjuna (Buddhist philosopher) associated with them;
- Sangams (Tamil): three Tamil Sangams documented literary culture in South India;

• Gupta Empire (c. 320–550 CE) — the 'Golden Age':
- Chandragupta I: began era, alliance with Licchavis;
- Samudragupta: 'Napoleon of India' (Prayag Prashasti/Allahabad Pillar inscription by Harishena); extensive military campaigns;
- Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya): defeated Sakas, captured Ujjain; court had Nine Gems (Navaratna) including Kalidasa;
- Kumaragupta and Skandagupta later faced Hun invasions;
- Contributions: Aryabhata (zero, heliocentric ideas), Varahamihira, Vasubandu, Pushpadanta; Ajanta and Ellora caves painting and sculpture; Nalanda University established;
- Administration: decentralized; lands granted to Brahmins and officials;
- Decline: Huna invasions under Mihirakula; internal fractures; invasion by Toramana and Mihirakula in 6th century weakened the empire.

Medieval India — Delhi Sultanate

The Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) consisted of five dynasties:

• Slave/Dynasty (1206–1290): Qut ud din Aibak (built Quwwat-ul-Islam, started Qutub Minar), Iltutmish (real organizer, introduced Iqta system, silver Tanka and copper Paisa), Balban (theory of blood/zabti and nail Theory/zanjir strengthened kingship, crushed rebellion of Forty/Takini);
• Khilji Dynasty (1290–1320): Jalal ud din Khilji founded; Alauddin Khilji was the great conqueror — market reforms (fixed prices, regulated trade), standing army, land revenue reforms (measurement-based tax, jaziya on non-Muslims), defeated Mongols and expanded south to Devagiri, Malwa, Kampil, Madurai;
• Tughlaq Dynasty (1320–1414): Muhammad bin Tughlaq — transferred capital to Daulatabad, introduced token currency (brass replaced copper), Jaziya on Brahmins — experimental but failed; Firuz Shah Tughlaq — built canals, hospitals (Dar ul-Shifa), Jagiris, abolished jaziya on Brahmans;
• Sayyid Dynasty (1414–1451): weakest; Khalji revivalist tendencies;
• Lodi Dynasty (1451–1526): Bahlul Lodi founded; Sikandar Lodi shifted capital to Agra (1526); Ibrahim Lodi defeated by Babur at Panipat (1526).

• Administrative terms: Iqta (land grant for revenue collection and military service), Wazir (finance minister), Mir Bakshi (military/despatch), Kazi (judiciary), Kotwal (city magistrate).

GPSC relevance: Alauddin's market reforms are frequently asked. The Junagadh inscription of Rudradaman connects to Gujarat history.

Mughal Empire and Bhakti-Sufi Movements

• Mughal Empire (1526–1857):
- Babur: founded Mughal dynasty after First Battle of Panipat (1526, defeated Ibrahim Lodi); used gunpowder and tulugma tactics; wrote Baburnama (autobiography in Turkic);
- Humayun: lost empire to Sher Shah Suri, recovered later; Diwan-i-Arz established;
- Akbar (1556–1605): Second Battle of Panipat (1556, Hemu defeated by Bairam Khan); abolished jaziya; Din-i-Ilahi; Sulh-i-kul (universal peace);mansabdari system; matrimonial alliances; Navratnas (Birbal, Tansen, etc.); constructed Fatehpur Sikri;
- Jahangir: chain of justice; Nur Jahan influential; Europeans (British, Dutch, Portuguese) established trading centres;
- Shah Jahan: Peak of Mughal architecture — Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Jama Masjid, Golden Mosque; spent heavily;
- Aurangzeb: re-imposed jaziya; expanded empire to Deccan; conflicted with Marathas, Sikhs, Rajputs; died 1707 — begins decline;
- Later Mughals: weak rulers after 1707; Persian invader Nadir Shah looted Delhi (1739, took Peacock Throne); Ahmad Shah Abdali defeated Marathas at Third Battle of Panipat (1761);

• Bhakti and Sufi Movements:
- Bhakti: Ramanuja, Kabir (Dhamin poetry, Din-i-Ilahi influence), Nanak (Sikhism founder), Mirabai, Tulsidas, Surdas, Chaitanya, Tukaram, Eknath (Gujarat — played vital role in Gujarat's cultural heritage);
- Sufi orders: Chishti (Moinuddin Chishti — Ajmer, Nizamuddin Auliya), Suhrawardi, Qadiri, Naqshbandi. Sufi-Bhakti interaction promoted communal harmony.

GPSC relevance: Gujarat has strong connections — Eknath, regional Sufi saints like Pir Sadardin, and the Junagadh connection to Islamic inscriptions. Akbar's policies and Aurangzeb's religious policies are commonly contrasted.

Society, Culture and Architecture

• Art and Architecture:
- Temple architecture: Nagara (North — Shikhar style, e.g., Khajuraho, Konark), Dravida (South — Gopuram, e.g., Brihadeeswara), Vesara (hybrid in Deccan); Gujarat has excellent examples at Modhera Sun Temple, Dilwara Jain temples (Mount Abu), Somnath;
- Mughal architecture: blend of Persian, Islamic and Indian styles — use of red sandstone and white marble, domes, arches, minarets, charbagh gardens;
- Paintings: Miniature painting — Mughal school (Babur, Akbar, Jahangir periods), Rajput schools (Mewar, Bundi, Kishangarh, etc.), Pahari schools;

• Literature:
- Classical Sanskrit: Kalidasa (Shakuntala, Meghaduta), Banabhatta (Harshacharita), Vishakhadatta (Mudrarakshasa), Sudraka (Mrichchhakatika);
- Regional: Bhakti literature in Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu;
- Persian: Ain-i-Akbari (Abul Fazl), Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi (Ziauddin Barani), Tarikh-i-Akbari (Faizi);

• Social conditions:
- Caste system rigidified over time;
- Status of women deteriorated — sati, child marriage, purdah became more prevalent (especially among upper classes);
- Guilds (shrenis) were important economic units;
- Trade: silk route, maritime trade with Rome, Southeast Asia, Arabia; Pepper, cotton, indigo were major exports.

Arrival of Europeans and Rise of Indian Resistances (17th–18th Century)

• European arrival:
- Portuguese: Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498; established factories at Goa, Diu, Daman (Portuguese strongholds in Gujarat/West Coast);
- Dutch: arrived early 17th century; focused on Indonesia;
- British East India Company: founded 1600; factory at Surat (1613, granted by Jahangir via Captain Hawkins); Bombay granted to England as part of Catherine of Braganza's dowry (1661);
- French: Compagnie des Indes Orientales (1664); Pondicherry established;

• Key battles:
- Battle of Plassey (1757): Robert Clive defeated Siraj ud Daulah (with Mir Jafar's treachery) — beginning of British political rule;
- Battle of Buxar (1764): East India Company defeated combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula and Shah Alam II; obtained Diwani rights of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa (Treaty of Allahabad 1765);

• Resistance movements:
- Sikh: Guru Gobind Singh, later Misls resisted Mughal and Afghan domination;
- Maratha: Shivaji Maharaj (1630–1680) founded Maratha power; guerrilla warfare (ganimi kava); crowned at Raigad (1674); signed treaty with Aurangzeb (Treaty of Purandar);
- Peasant and tribal uprisings: frequent against Mughal officials;
- In Gujarat: Rajputs of Idar and other states maintained autonomy; Portuguese influence persisted at Diu and Daman.

Indian Freedom Struggle — Phases and Movements

• Moderate Phase (1885–1905):
- Indian National Congress founded in 1885 by A.O. Hume;
- Early presidents: W.C. Bonnerjee, Dadabhai Naoroji (Naoroji's 'Drain of Wealth' theory), Surat session president, G.K. Gokhale;
- Methods: petitions, prayers, protests ('3 Ps');
- Achievements: Indian Councils Act 1892, Indian National Congress sessions gained importance;

• Extremist Phase (1905–1919):
- Partition of Bengal (1905, Lord Curzon) sparked Swadeshi Movement;
- Leaders: Bal Gangadhar Tilak ('Swaraj is my birthright'), Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal (Lal-Bal-Pal); Aurobindo Ghosh;
- Revolutionary activities: Anushilan Samiti, Yugantar, Khudai Khidmatgar (Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan), Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad, Sukhdev, Rajguru, Bhagat Singh execution (1931);
- Home Rule Leagues: Tilak and Annie Besant (1916);

• Gandhian Phase (1919–1947):
- Rowlatt Act (1919) → Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (Baisakhi 1919, General Dyer); Jallianwala Bagh is in Amritsar, Punjab;
- Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22): Chauri-Chaura incident (1922) led to withdrawal;
- Swaraj Party: C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru;
- Simon Commission (1927): 'Simon Go Back';
- Nehru Report (1928):
- Poona Pact (1932): Gandhi and Ambedkar agreement on separate electorates;
- Civil Disobedience / Salt March (1930): Dandi March 12 March–6 April 1930 (240 miles), broke salt law;
- Round Table Conferences (1930–32):
- Government of India Act 1935: federal structure, provincial autonomy;
- Provincial elections 1937: Congress formed governments in several provinces;
- August Offer (1940), Cripps Mission (1942), Quit India Movement (1942): 'Do or Die';
- INA: Subhash Chandra Bose, Indian National Army, Azad Hind Fauj, Slogan 'Chalo Delhi', 'Jai Hind';
- Cabinet Mission (1946), Mountbatten Plan (June 3, 1947), Indian Independence Act 1947.

GPSC relevance: Gujarat contributions include Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (leader of Bardoli Satyagraha 1928 — 'Sardar'), Mahatma Gandhi's early work (Champaran, Kheda), and the role of Gujarat in the freedom movement.

Social Reform Movements and Regional Context

• National social reformers:
- Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833): Brahmo Samaj (1828), fought sati, widow remarriage, education;
- Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar: Widow Remarriage Act 1856;
- Jyotiba Phule (1827–1890): Satyashodhak Samaj, worked for lower castes and women's education;
- Savitribai Phule: first female teacher in India;
- Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902): Ramakrishna Mission, represented at Parliament of Religions (Chicago 1893);
- Dayanand Saraswati: Arya Samaj (1875), Shuddhi movement, Back to the Vedas;
- Sri Aurobindo: integral yoga, revolutionary thinker;
- Mahatma Gandhi: Harijan movement, abolition of untouchability, promotion of khadi and village industries;

• Gujarat-specific context:
- Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel: born in Nadiad (1875); Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) earned him title 'Sardar'; led integration of princely states post-independence;
- Mahatma Gandhi: studied in Rajkot, trained in law in London; returned to India and began activism in South Africa (1893); established Sabarmati Ashram (1917); key role in Champaran (1917), Kheda (1918), Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918);
- Narayan Mehta: poet and social reformer in Gujarat;
- Modern Gujarat saw participation in each major movement: Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience, Quit India;
- The role of Muslim leaders in Gujarat: Abdulla Haji Baghdchi, Muhammad Husain Beegum, and others participated actively.

GPSC relevance: Questions may connect national events with Gujarat's specific contribution. Remember: Bardoli is called 'Satyagraha of the Rich' or 'Victory Satyagraha'. The Kheda Satyagraha involved peasant protest against tax collection during famine.

Important facts

  • Indus Valley Civilization: c. 2600–1900 BCE; major Gujarat sites: Lothal (dockyard), Dholavira (water management, signboard); both UNESCO sites.
  • Rigveda: oldest Veda, composed during Early Vedic Period; four Vedas — Rig, Sama, Yajur, Atharva.
  • Sixteen Mahajanapadas: Magadha, Kosala, Vajji (republic), Avanti, Gandhara, Kuru, Panchala, Matsya, etc.
  • Mahavira: 24th Tirthankara of Jainism; Buddha: founder of Buddhism; both 6th century BCE contemporaries.
  • Ashoka's Kalinga War: 261 BCE; turning point — embraced non-violence after bloodshed.
  • Junagadh rock inscription: by Rudradaman I (Saka ruler), in Sanskrit, records repair of Sudarshana lake.
  • Delhi Sultanate: five dynasties — Slave, Khilji, Tughlaq, Sayyid, Lodi (1206–1526).
  • Akbar: Sulh-i-kul policy; mansabdari system; Din-i-Ilahi; married Hindu princesses; abolised jaziya.
  • Aurangzeb: re-imposed jaziya; conflicts with Sikhs, Marathas, Rajputs; death 1707 marks beginning of decline.
  • Battle of Plassey: 1757 (Robert Clive vs Siraj ud Daulah); Battle of Buxar: 1764.
  • Treaty of Allahabad (1765): East India Company got Diwani rights of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa.
  • GNCC founded: 1885 by A.O. Hume; Dadabhai Naoroji — 'Grand Old Man of India'; first Asian president of INC was Surendranath Banerjea (Calcutta 1887).
  • Swadeshi Movement: sparked by Partition of Bengal (1905, Lord Curzon).
  • Rowlatt Act: 1919; Jallianwala Bagh Massacre: 13 April 1919 (Baisakhi day).
  • Non-Cooperation Movement: 1920–22; withdrawn after Chauri-Chaura (1922).
  • Dandi March: 12 March–6 April 1930; 240 miles; broke salt law.
  • Poona Pact: 1932 between Gandhi and Ambedkar on reserved seats for Depressed Classes.
  • Government of India Act 1935: provincial autonomy; federal scheme.
  • Quit India Movement: 8 August 1942; slogan 'Do or Die'.
  • Bose: INA, 'Jai Hind', 'Give me blood and I will give you freedom'.
  • Cabinet Mission: 1946; proposed grouping scheme; Mountbatten Plan announced 3 June 1947.
  • Indian Independence Act: passed in July 1947; came into force 15 August 1947.
  • Sardar Patel: Bardoli Satyagraha 1928; integration of 562 princely states post-independence.
  • Gandhi's first satyagraha in India: Champaran (1917, Bihar, indigo peasants).
