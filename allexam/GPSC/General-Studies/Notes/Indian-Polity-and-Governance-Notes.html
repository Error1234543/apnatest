1. Historical Background of the Indian Constitution

['The Constitution of India was adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 26 November 1949 and came into effect on 26 January 1950, making India a sovereign democratic republic. The Constituent Assembly was formed in 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan, with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar serving as Chairman of the Drafting Committee and Dr. Rajendra Prasad as President of the Assembly.', "Several important acts shaped the constitutional evolution before 1950: the Government of India Act 1935 provided the structural framework (federal scheme, provincial autonomy, Governor's powers); the Indian Independence Act 1947 ended British rule; the Objectives Resolution moved by Nehru in December 1946 became the philosophical foundation; and the Constitution drew borrowings from multiple sources — Britain (parliamentary system, rule of law, speech immunity), USA (Fundamental Rights, judicial review, impeachment), Ireland (DPSP nominations), Canada (federation with strong centre), Germany/Weimar (suspension of Fundamental Rights during emergency), Australia (concurrent list), South Africa (amendment procedure), and Japan (fundamental duties added later).", 'Key dates: drafting began December 1946, first meeting of Constituent Assembly December 1946, drafting committee formed August 1947, draft published February 1948, final adoption 26 November 1949, enforcement 26 January 1950. The original Constitution had 395 articles arranged in 22 parts and 8 schedules, containing about 145,000 words — it remains the longest written national constitution.']

2. salient Features of the Indian Constitution

["India's Constitution has several distinctive features that make it both rigid and flexible. It is a comprehensive document blending elements of rigidity and flexibility — some provisions require a simple majority while others demand special majorities or ratification by states. This dual character earned K.C. Pant the description of 'partly rigid and partly flexible.'", "Federal system with unitary bias: India is described as a 'Union of States' (Article 1) with a federal structure dividing powers between Centre and States through three lists (Union, State, Concurrent), yet during emergencies the Constitution assumes a unitary character. The Centre holds overriding powers through appointments, financial control, all-India services, and the power to reorganize states.", 'Parliamentary form of government: both at Centre and states, the executive is responsible to the legislature. The President/Governor is a nominal head while real power lies with the PM/CM and Council of Ministers. This model ensures accountability and prevents dictatorship.', 'Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35) and Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A, Article 51A): justiciable rights guarantee equality, freedom, against exploitation, freedom of religion, cultural and educational rights, and constitutional remedies. Duties were added by the 42nd Amendment Act 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee.', 'Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36–51): non-justicible guidelines for the State to establish social and economic democracy. They must be balanced against Fundamental Rights as held in Minerva Mills case.', 'Single citizenship: unlike the USA which offers dual citizenship, India provides only Indian citizenship irrespective of the state of residence.', 'Independent judiciary: a unified judicial system with the Supreme Court at apex, possessing power of judicial review and original, appellate, and advisory jurisdictions.', 'Integrated election machinery: the Election Commission conducts free and fair elections for Parliament, State Legislatures, President, and Vice-President through a single authoritative body.', "Secular state: the Constitution originally did not explicitly mention 'secularism' but it became part of the basic structure via the 42nd Amendment 1976. The State maintains equal respect for all religions without establishing any official religion.", 'Universal adult franchise: every citizen above 18 years has the right to vote regardless of caste, religion, gender, education, or economic status — lowered from 21 by the 61st Amendment Act 1988.']

3. Union and Its Territory / Citizenship

["Article 1 declares India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States. The term 'Union' deliberately avoids 'Federation' to signify that India is not the result of an agreement among states and that states have no right to secede. Article 2 empowers Parliament to admit or establish new states; Article 3 allows formation of new states by separation of territory from existing states, elevation of territories to statehood, or alteration of area/boundaries/name of any state.", "The First Schedule lists all states and union territories. As of recent reorganization, there are 28 states and 8 UTs. Notable changes include the creation of Telangana (2014, 111th Amendment), Jammu & Kashmir reorganization into two UTs (2019, 101st Amendment of relevant provisions), and Delhi's special status debates.", 'Citizenship is governed by Articles 5–11 under Part II. At commencement, citizenship was granted by domicile (Article 5). The Citizenship Act 1955, amended several times (most significantly in 2003, 2005, and 2019), governs acquisition and determination. Five modes: by birth, descent, registration, naturalization, and incorporation of territory. The 2019 amendment introduced provisions for registered citizens from Pakistan, Afghanistan, and Bangladesh belonging to six specified communities.', 'Important: There is only one citizenship — Indian citizenship — not dual citizenship. Overseas Citizens of India (OCI) provide loyalty-based deference but not political rights.']

4. Fundamental Rights (Articles 12–35)

["The Sixth Schedule (Part III) contains six categories of Fundamental Rights, collectively termed 'Magna Carta of India.' They are justiciable — enforceable by courts under Article 32.", 'Right to Equality (Articles 14–18): Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws. Article 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Article 16 ensures equality of opportunity in public employment. Article 17 abolishes untouchability. Article 18 abolishes titles except military and academic distinctions. Reasonable classification is permitted under Article 14 but must satisfy two tests: intelligible differentia and rational nexus to the object sought.', 'Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22): Article 19 protects six freedoms — speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession. These are subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2)–(6) on grounds like sovereignty, public order, morality, etc. Articles 20–22 deal with protection against conviction, protection of life and personal liberty (expanded interpretation in Maneka Gandhi case), and protection against arrest and detention.', "Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24): prohibits traffic in human beings, forced labour, and child labour in hazardous industries (below 14 years). The term 'exploitative' is broad — covers forced labour of any kind and child employment in dangerous conditions.", 'Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28): guarantees freedom of conscience, practice, propagation, and management of religious affairs. Subject to public order, morality, health, and other Fundamental Rights. Articles 27–28 deal with freedom from taxation for religious promotion and religious instruction in educational institutions.', 'Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29–30): protect interests of minorities — any section with distinct language/script/culture has right to conserve it. Minorities have right to establish and administer educational institutions.', "Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32): Dr. Ambedkar called it the 'heart and soul' of the Constitution. The Supreme Court can issue writs — Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto. Article 226 grants similar power to High Courts with wider territorial jurisdiction and scope."]

5. Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) — Articles 36–51

['DPSPs are guidelines to the State for establishing social and economic democracy. They are non-justiciable (Article 37) but fundamental in governance. The State shall apply them in making laws. They occupy Part IV and draw inspiration from the Irish Constitution.', 'Socialistic principles (Articles 38–39A): State shall secure social, economic, and political justice; equal justice; free legal aid to poor; living wage; healthy development of children; prevention of concentration of wealth; uniform civil code consideration.', 'Gandhian principles (Articles 40–48): organize village panchayats, promote cooperatives, prevent cattle slaughter, protect forests and wildlife, provide early childhood care, promote Hindi, protect monuments, prohibit consumption of intoxicating drinks.', 'Liberal-intellectual principles (Articles 44–49, 51): uniform civil code (Article 44), separation of judiciary from executive (Article 50), protection of environment and wildlife (Article 48A), promotion of international peace (Article 51).', 'Conflict between FR and DPSP: the Supreme Court in Champakam Dorairajan (1951) held FRs prevail over DPSPs. This led to the 1st Amendment. In Golaknath (1967), the Court held Fundamental Rights are transcendental and unamendable. The 24th Amendment reversed this partially. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Basic Structure doctrine was established — DPSPs and FRs are complementary, and neither can destroy the basic structure. In Minerva Mills (1980), the Court struck down clauses of the 42nd Amendment that gave DPSPs supremacy over FRs, reaffirming the harmony between Parts III and IV.']

6. Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

['Fundamental Duties were added by the 42nd Amendment Act 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Originally there were 10 duties; the 86th Amendment Act 2002 added one more (Clause (k) regarding parent/guardian providing educational opportunities to child aged 6–14 years). They are located in Part IV-A, Article 51A.', 'The duties encompass: compliance with the Constitution and respect for the Flag and Anthem; cherishing noble ideals of freedom struggle; protecting sovereignty, integrity, and defence of India; rendering national service when called; preserving composite cultural heritage; protecting environment and woodland/wildlife; developing scientific temper, humanism, and inquiry spirit; safeguarding public property and abjuring violence; striving for excellence in all fields; and parental duty toward education of children.', 'Importantly, Fundamental Duties are non-justiciable — they cannot be enforced through courts directly. However, they serve as a moral compass and can be used by courts while interpreting ambiguities in legislation. The Supreme Court has held in various cases that duties and rights are correlative — one cannot enjoy rights without performing duties. The National Memorial Trust and various commissions have promoted duty awareness through education.']

7. The President of India — Articles 52–78

['The President is the Head of State, Supreme Commander of Armed Forces, and the first citizen of India. Elected indirectly by an Electoral College consisting of elected members of both Houses of Parliament and elected members of Legislative Assemblies of States (including NCT Delhi and Puducherry). The method is proportional representation by single transferable vote. The value of vote of an MLA is calculated differently from an MP to maintain federal balance.', 'Qualifications: citizen of India, age 35+, qualified to be a member of Lok Sabha, holds no office of profit. Term is 5 years. Impeachment requires a special majority in either House with prior notice of 14 days. Grounds: violation of Constitution.', 'Powers: Executive — appoints PM, governors, judges, AG, CAG, election commissioners, etc.; Legislative — summons/prorogues Parliament, nominates 12 members to Rajya Sabha, can ordinance promulgate when Parliament not in session, gives assent to bills; Financial — money bills introduced only on his recommendation, annual budget presented, contingency fund under his control; Judicial — appoints SC and HC judges, grants pardon/suspension/commutation; Emergency — can proclaim three types of emergencies; Diplomatic — represents India internationally, appoints ambassadors.', 'Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when both Houses of Parliament are not in session — these have same force as Acts but must be approved within 6 weeks of reassembly. The Supreme Court in DC Wadhwa case held that continued re-promulgation without legislative scrutiny is unconstitutional.']

8. Prime Minister and Council of Ministers — Articles 74–75

['The President appoints the Prime Minister (Article 74(1)) who is the head of government and real executive authority. The PM advises the President on appointment of ministers, allocates portfolios, and coordinates cabinet work. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Article 75(3)).', 'Ministers must be members of Parliament or get elected within 6 months. The number of ministers including PM cannot exceed 15% of total Lok Sabha members (91st Amendment Act 2003). Cabinet ministers, ministers of state, and deputy ministers form the hierarchy.', 'The oath of office is administered by the President. A minister who is not a member of either House for six consecutive months ceases to be a minister. The real power lies with the Cabinet — the inner circle of ministers that makes key policy decisions. The PM chairs Cabinet meetings and is the chief architect of foreign policy.', 'The 9th Schedule was created to protect certain land reform laws from judicial review but was partially struck down in I.R. Coelho case (2007) where the Supreme Court held that laws placed in the 9th Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to judicial review if they violate basic structure.']

9. Parliament of India — Articles 79–122

['Parliament consists of the President, Rajya Sabha (Council of States), and Lok Sabha (House of People). It is a bicameral legislature. The Rajya Sabha has a maximum strength of 250 members — 238 elected by state/UT assemblies by proportional representation and 12 nominated by the President from fields of literature, science, art, and social service. The Lok Sabha has a maximum of 552 members — 530 from states, 20 from UTs, and 2 nominated from Anglo-Indian community (though the 104th Amendment 2019 discontinued the Anglo-Indian nomination after 2020).', 'Rajya Sabha is a permanent body — one-third members retire every two years, each serving a six-year term. It cannot be dissolved. Lok Sabha has a normal term of five years but can be extended during emergency by one year at a time (maximum six months beyond emergency end).', 'Legislative powers: Parliament can make laws on Union List (97 subjects), Concurrent List (47 subjects), and residuary matters go to Parliament (Article 248). In case of conflict between Union and State laws on concurrent subjects, Union law prevails (Article 254). Ordinary bills require simple majority; money bills require only Lok Sabha approval (Article 110); constitutional amendment bills need special majority.', "Financial business: budget presentation, appropriation bills, and annual financial statement (Article 112) are central. Money bills can only be introduced in Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha has only 14 days to suggest changes. The concept of 'Vote on Account' and 'Interim Budget' operates under specific rules. No tax can be levied without parliamentary authorization (Article 265)."]

10. State Government — Governor, Chief Minister, State Legislature

["Each state has a Governor appointed by the President for a term of five years (Article 155–156). The Governor is the constitutional head similar to the President at the Centre but with more discretionary powers in certain situations, particularly under Article 200 (assent to bills) and Article 217 (reservation of bills). The Governor can reserve certain bills for the President's consideration.", "The Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister aids and advises the Governor (Article 163). The CM is appointed by the Governor; in case of clear majority, the leader of the largest party is appointed. If no clear majority, the Governor may use discretion to appoint the most likely candidate or recommend President's Rule.", 'State Legislatures vary: most states have bicameral legislatures (Legislative Assembly + Legislative Council), but Parliament can abolish or create Councils under Article 169. The Maharashtra and Karnataka legislatures have been subjects of debate. The Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) is directly elected; the Council (Vidhan Parishad) is partly elected, partly nominated, and partly graduated electorate.', "State legislative power extends to State List (66 subjects) and Concurrent List. residuary powers with the Centre. The Governor's role during President's Rule (Article 356) and President's satisfaction for imposition is critical. The SR Bommai judgment (1994) laid down crucial safeguards against arbitrary imposition."]

11. Judiciary — Supreme Court, High Courts, Subordinate Courts

['The Supreme Court is the apex judicial body comprising the Chief Justice and up to 34 other judges (number fixed by Parliament). Judges are appointed by the President after consultation with the collegium system (established through three Judges Cases: 1981, 1993, 1998). The collegium comprises the CJI and four senior-most judges of the SC. Retired judges can be called upon to sit and act as ad-hoc judges.', 'Jurisdiction: original (disputes between Centre and States or between States — Article 131), appellate (in civil, criminal, and constitutional matters under Articles 132–136), and advisory (Article 143). The Court has inherent power under Article 142 to pass orders necessary for complete justice. It also exercises writ jurisdiction under Article 32.', "High Courts exist in each state or group of states. There are currently 25 High Courts. The Chief Justice and other judges are appointed by the President. High Courts have supervisory jurisdiction over subordinate courts, writ jurisdiction under Article 226 (wider than SC's Article 32), and appellate jurisdiction. The 42nd Amendment attempted to create inter-State High Courts but was not fully implemented.", "Subordinate judiciary includes District Judges, Sessions Courts, Magistrates, and other lower courts under the supervision of the respective High Court (Article 233–237). The National Judicial Academy and state judicial academies handle training. The concept of 'independence of judiciary' was established in the First Judges Case and reinforced in subsequent cases. The NJAC Act (99th Amendment) was struck down in 2015 by the Supreme Court in the Fourth Judges Case, restoring the collegium system."]

12. Constitutional Bodies and Independent Institutions

['The Constitution establishes several independent constitutional bodies essential for democratic functioning: Election Commission of India (Articles 324–329) — superintendence, direction, and control of elections; holds office during pleasure of President but can only be removed like a Supreme Court judge. The Chief Election Commissioner enjoys security of tenure since the 2023 Constitutional Amendment Act which also provided for a selection committee for appointments.', 'Finance Commission (Articles 280–290) — constituted every five years to recommend distribution of tax proceeds between Centre and States, principles governing grants-in-aid, and augmentation of Consolidated Funds of States. The 15th Finance Commission (2021–2026) recommended using 1971 population as weightage alongside modern parameters.', 'Public Service Commissions (Articles 315–323) — Union Public Service Commission and State PSCs conduct recruitment for civil services. Members hold office until age 65 (UPSC) or 62 (SPSC). The 2023 PSC Amendment Act introduced a search cum-empanelment committee for appointment of Chairpersons and Members.', 'Comptroller and Auditor General (Articles 148–151) — audits all receipts and expenditures of the Government of India and States. The 44th Amendment removed the condition that CAG reports on accountancy. Removed from Schedule VII of the Constitution but retained as constitutional authority.', 'Other bodies: National Commission for Scheduled Castes (Article 338), National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (Article 338A), National Commission for Women (statutory, not constitutional), National Human Rights Commission, Central Information Commission, and various tribunals under Articles 323A and 323B.']

13. Emergency Provisions (Articles 352–360)

["Three types of emergencies are provided: National Emergency (Article 352), State Emergency/President's Rule (Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360). These provisions were based on the Government of India Act 1935 and are considered the most controversial part of the Constitution.", "National Emergency (Article 352): can be proclaimed on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion (the word 'internal disturbance' was omitted by the 44th Amendment 1978). Must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month (originally two months) by special majority. Can be extended indefinitely with yearly parliamentary approval. During emergency, Fundamental Rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended; other Fundamental Rights (except Articles 20 and 21) can be suspended by the President under Article 359.", "President's Rule (Article 356): imposed when the Governor reports that the state government cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution. The President can assume all functions of the State Government, dissolve the Legislative Assembly, and promulgate ordinances. The Supreme Court in Bommai case (1994) laid down that the Governor's report must be based on objective material, not subjective satisfaction. The validity of proclamation can be judicially reviewed. The 44th Amendment made it difficult to impose President's Rule — it requires either Presidential satisfaction or approval by Parliament within two months.", 'Financial Emergency (Article 360): has never been proclaimed. The President can direct reduction of salaries of all government servants including judges. States must comply with the directions. Current economic challenges have not led to invocation.']

14. Amendment of the Constitution (Article 368)

['The Indian Constitution is neither strictly rigid nor strictly flexible. Article 368 provides the amendment procedure. Three methods exist: simple majority (for matters not covered by Article 368, like admission of new states, citizenship, etc.), special majority of Parliament (majority of total membership + 2/3 of members present and voting), and special majority plus ratification by half of state legislatures (for federal provisions affecting Supreme Court, Election Commission, distributive powers, etc.).', "Key landmark amendments: 1st (1951) — added Ninth Schedule, reasonable restrictions on FRs; 7th (1956) — reorganized states on linguistic basis; 24th (1971) — confirmed Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights; 42nd (1974) — 'Mini Constitution' added Fundamental Duties, DPSP supremacy clause, reduced judicial review; 44th (1978) — rolled back many 42nd Amendment provisions; 52nd (1985) — anti-defection law added as 10th Schedule; 61st (1989) — lowered voting age to 18; 73rd and 74th (1992) — Panchayati Raj and Municipalities; 76th (1994) — reservation in education; 86th (2002) — made education a Fundamental Right for children 6–14; 101st (2016) — GST introduction; 104th (2019) — extended SC/ST reservation and Anglo-Indian nomination; 105th (2021) — restored state power to identify SEBCs; 106th (2023) — women's reservation in legislatures.", "The basic structure doctrine (Kesavananda Bharati 1973) limits Parliament's amending power — Parliament cannot alter the basic structure of the Constitution. This includes secularism, federalism, separation of powers, judicial review, free and fair elections, and the rule of law."]

15. Panchayati Raj and Local Self-Government

['The Panchayati Raj system in India has ancient roots but was formally institutionalized through the 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act 1992, which came into force on 24 April 1993. It added Part IX (Articles 243–243O) and the 11th Schedule (29 subjects). The Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) first recommended a three-tier system, followed by the Ashok Mehta Committee (1977) and the G.V.K. Rao Committee (1985).', 'Three-tier structure: Gram Panchayat at village level, Panchayat Samiti at block/intermediate level, and Zila Parishad at district level. Every gram panchayat consists of directly elected members. The Sarpanch is elected directly. Vacancies are filled by by-elections. Reservation of seats for SC/ST (in proportion to their population) and women (not less than one-third) is mandated.', 'State Election Commission conducts panchayat elections. State Finance Commission recommends financial powers distribution. The 11th Schedule lists 29 functional items including agriculture, minor irrigation, rural housing, drinking water, literacy, health, sanitation, fodder, power, rural roads, and poverty alleviation programs.', 'Important: the 73rd Amendment did not apply to Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram, and tribal areas of Assam, where autonomous councils operate under the Sixth Schedule. Similarly, the 74th Amendment (1992) deals with urban local bodies (Part IXA, Articles 243P–243ZT, 12th Schedule with 18 subjects).']

16. Important Articles and Schedules — Quick Reference for GPSC

Understanding the Gujarat Panchayat Act alongside the 73rd Amendment is important for state-level questions.']

Important facts

  • Constitution adopted on 26 November 1949 and enforced on 26 January 1950. Total original articles: 395; parts: 22; schedules: 8.
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar is the Father of the Indian Constitution. Dr. Rajendra Prasad was the President of the Constituent Assembly.
  • The Constitution is the longest written constitution of any sovereign country. Original word count: approximately 145,000 words.
  • Fundamental Rights are in Part III (Articles 12–35). Directive Principles are in Part IV (Articles 36–51). Fundamental Duties are in Part IV-A (Article 51A), added by the 42nd Amendment 1976.
  • The 44th Amendment Act 1978 removed 'internal disturbance' as a ground for Emergency, replacing it with 'armed rebellion'.
  • The Basic Structure Doctrine was propounded in Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) — Parliament cannot amend the basic structure.
  • Article 21 has been interpreted expansively to include: right to livelihood, right to privacy (Puttaswamy judgment, 2017), right to speedy trial, right to clean environment, right to health, and right to dignity.
  • The 73rd Amendment (1992) gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj with 29 subjects in the 11th Schedule. The 74th Amendment (1992) dealt with Urban Local Bodies with 18 subjects in the 12th Schedule.
  • There are currently 22 languages in the 8th Schedule (Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, and Santhali were added by the 92nd, 95th, 100th, and 104th Amendments respectively).
  • The Election Commission of India was established on 25 March 1950. The 2023 Constitutional Amendment changed the appointment process to involve a search cum-empanelment committee.
  • The 101st Amendment Act 2016 introduced Goods and Services Tax (GST) with Article 246A giving concurrent power to Centre and States.
  • The 106th Amendment Act 2023 provides for 33% reservation for women in Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.
  • GPSC exam tip: Focus on Articles related to President, Parliament, Fundamental Rights, Emergency, and Constitutional Bodies as these have high weightage in the General Studies paper.
