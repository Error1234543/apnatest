1. Parts of Speech: Nouns

A noun names a person, place, thing, idea, or quality. Nouns are classified as:

• Common nouns: General names (city, man, book). Capitalized only at the beginning of a sentence.
• Proper nouns: Specific names (Ahmedabad, Mahatma Gandhi, Arabian Sea). Always capitalized; take no article when referring to a single unique entity, but take 'the' with plural proper nouns (the Ali Bans, the Waghais).
• Collective nouns: Groups treated as one unit (team, committee, jury, parliament). With collective nouns, the verb is singular when the group acts as one, plural when members act individually (e.g., 'The jury has delivered its verdict' vs 'The jury were arguing among themselves').
• Material nouns: Substances (gold, water, timber, sand).
• Abstract nouns: Ideas, qualities, states (honesty, freedom, happiness, courage). Generally uncountable; do not take 'a/an'.
• Countable vs Uncountable: Countable nouns have plural forms and take numbers (one book, two books). Uncountable nouns do not take a plural and cannot be directly counted (information, advice, luggage, furniture, poetry). Use quantifiers like 'some, much, a little, a piece of' instead of 'many/much' directly.

Plural formation rules: Add -s (cats); add -es after s, x, z, ch, sh (boxes, buses); change -y to -ies after consonant (cities, but boys because y follows vowel); irregular plurals (man/men, foot/feet, child/children, mouse/mice, person/people, ox/oxen); same form in singular and plural (sheep, deer, fish, series, species); plurals only (scissors, tongs, spectacles, trousers, jeans).

2. Parts of Speech: Pronouns

A pronoun replaces a noun (the antecedent). Key types and rules:

• Personal pronouns: I, you, he, she, it, we, they (subject); me, you, him, her, it, us, them (object). Use subjective case after 'than' in formal comparison (She is taller than I), objective case after prepositions (between you and me).
• Relative pronouns: who (people, subject), whom (people, object), which (things/animals), that (people/things in restrictive clauses), whose (possession). 'That' is preferred over 'which' in restrictive/defining relative clauses; use comma + 'which' for non-restrictive clauses.
• Demonstrative pronouns: this, that, these, those. 'This/these' for nearby; 'that/those' for distant.
• Indefinite pronouns: all, some, any, none, each, every, either, neither, one, ones, somebody, anyone, everything, nothing. 'Each/every' take singular verbs. 'None' takes singular or plural depending on context (none of the water is / none of the students are).
• Reflexive pronouns: myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves. Used when subject and object are the same, or for emphasis. Not used as substitutes for personal pronouns after prepositions (say 'between you and me', not 'between you and myself').
• Reciprocal pronouns: each other (two), one another (more than two).

Common error: Using 'whoever' as an object when it is the subject of the subordinate clause (Give it to whoever wants it — 'whoever' is subject of 'wants').

3. Parts of Speech: Adjectives and Adverbs

Adjectives modify nouns/pronouns. Adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs.

• Adjective order (OSASCOMP): Opinion → Size → Age → Shape → Colour → Origin → Material → Purpose → Noun. Example: 'a beautiful small old rectangular Indian cotton sleeping bag'. Memorize this sequence for error correction questions.
• Comparative and Superlative degrees: Regular — add -er/-est (tall, taller, tallest); double consonant after CVC (big, bigger, biggest); change -y to -i (happy, happier, happiest). Irregular — good/better/best, bad/worse/worst, little/less/least, much/many/more/most, far/farther(future distance)/further(abstract degree), old/older(elapse time)/elder(rank), sober/soberer/soberest, wild/wilder/wildest. Some adjectives don't form comparatives (unique, perfect, dead, round, hollow) but may appear in relaxed usage.
• Double comparatives/superlatives are wrong: never say 'more better' or 'most best'. Never use both forms together: 'more superior' is incorrect; superior already means 'higher'.
• Adverbs of frequency (always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, seldom, never) go before the main verb but after 'be' (She always arrives late / She is always late).
• Adverbs of manner usually follow the verb or object (He runs quickly / He quickly finished his work).
• Flat adverbs have same form as adjective (fast, hard, late, early, daily): 'work hard', 'drive fast', not 'work hardly' (hardly means 'barely').

4. Tenses: Formation and Usage

English has three basic tenses — past, present, future — each with four aspects, giving twelve tense forms:

• Simple Present: habit, general truth, scheduled future. Markers: always, usually, every day. Form: base verb (+s/-es for third person singular). 'The sun rises in the east.'
• Present Continuous: action happening now, temporary situation, definite future arrangement. Form: am/is/are + V-ing. Stative verbs are generally NOT used in continuous form (know, believe, belong, seem, understand, want, need, prefer, like, love, hate, remember, forget).
• Present Perfect: completed action with present relevance, experience, action starting in past continuing to now. Form: has/have + V3. Markers: already, yet, just, ever, never, since, for. 'I have lived here since 2010.'
• Present Perfect Continuous: ongoing action emphasizing duration. Form: has/have been + V-ing. 'She has been studying for three hours.'
• Simple Past: completed action at specific past time. Form: V2. Markers: yesterday, last week, in 1990, ago. 'He went there yesterday.'
• Past Continuous: ongoing past action, interrupted by simple past. Form: was/were + V-ing. 'I was reading when he called.'
• Past Perfect: action completed before another past action. Form: had + V3. 'She had left before I arrived.'
• Past Perfect Continuous: duration up to a point in past. Form: had been + V-ing.
• Simple Future: will/shall + base verb. 'I will help you.'
• Future Continuous: will be + V-ing.
• Future Perfect: will have + V3.
• Future Perfect Continuous: will have been + V-ing.

Sequence of tenses: In reported speech, present → past, past → past perfect, future → conditional. However, universal truths remain in present tense even in reported speech ('He said the Earth revolves around the Sun').

5. Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb must agree with the subject in number and person.

• Singular subjects take singular verbs; plural subjects take plural verbs.
• Compound subjects joined by 'and' usually take plural verbs (Rama and Sita are coming). Exception: when 'and' joins two nouns referring to the same person/thing/concept (bread and butter is my breakfast; thief and robber is caught). 'Every/each/either/neither' before compound subjects takes singular verb (Every boy and every girl has won a prize).
• Indefinite pronouns (everyone, somebody, anybody, nobody, each, either, neither) take singular verbs.
• 'One of the + plural noun + relative clause': verb agrees with 'one' (singular) — 'She is one of the students who studies hard.' (relative pronoun refers to 'students' → plural; but 'She is the only one of the students who studies hard' → singular, because 'the only one' is the antecedent).
• Collectives (committee, government, team, family): singular when acting as unit, plural when members act individually.
• 'Neither...nor / Either...or / Not only...but also': verb agrees with the nearest subject (proximity rule). 'Neither the teacher nor the students were present.'
• Subjects connected by 'with, along with, together with, as well as, accompanied by, including' do NOT change the number of the subject — the verb agrees with the main subject alone. 'The principal, along with the teachers, is going.'
• 'There is/are' and 'Here is/are': verb agrees with the following noun (There is a book / There are many books).
• Words like 'news, physics, mathematics, economics, mechanics, civil rights' are plural in meaning but singular in form? No — these end in -s but take plural verbs (Physics are interesting is wrong; Physics is interesting). Actually: subjects ending in -ics denoting a branch of study are singular (Physics is, Economics is, Mathematics is, Politics is, Acrobatics is). But 'Means' is singular or plural depending on sense.
• Fractions and percentages: take singular or plural based on the noun they refer to (Half of the water is / Half of the students are).
• 'The number of' is singular; 'A number of' is plural (The number of students is increasing / A number of students are absent).
• 'More than one + singular noun' takes singular verb: 'More than one student has failed.' But 'More than one and a half' takes plural.

6. Articles

Three articles in English: a, an, the.

• 'A/An' = indefinite article, used with singular countable nouns on first mention. 'A' before consonant sounds (a university, a one-time event, a European country); 'an' before vowel sounds (an hour, an MBA, an honest man). Note: it's about SOUND, not spelling.
• 'The' = definite article, used when both speaker and listener know the specific referent. Rules for 'the': before superlatives (the best, the highest); before ordinal numbers (the first, the second); before unique things (the sun, the moon, the world, the Internet); before rivers, seas, oceans, mountain ranges, deserts, plural country names (the Ganga, the Pacific, the Alps, the Sahara, the United States); before names of newspapers, hotels, restaurants (the Times, the Taj Mahal); before adjectives representing a class (the poor, the blind, the elderly).
• No article (zero article) is used: before abstract nouns in general sense (Honesty is the best policy); before plural/uncountable nouns in general sense (Lions are dangerous; Water is essential); before meals (have breakfast); before languages, subjects, sports (study English, play cricket); before days/months/seasons (in January, on Monday) unless specified; before titles with names (Doctor Ram, President Modi);
• Common errors to avoid: 'in the front' (inside part) vs 'in front of' (outside ahead); 'at night' (no article) but 'in the night'; 'go to school' (purpose) vs 'go to the school' (visit); 'go to hospital' (India: treat) vs 'go to the hospital' (visit). GPSC frequently tests these nuances.

7. Prepositions

Prepositions show relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word. GPSC focuses on fixed collocations and commonly confused pairs.

• Time prepositions: 'at' for precise times (at 5 o'clock, at noon, at night); 'on' for days/dates (on Monday, on 15th August); 'in' for months/years/seasons/long periods (in June, in 2024, in summer, in the morning/afternoon/evening).
• Place prepositions: 'at' for point (at the door); 'on' for surface (on the table); 'in' for enclosed space (in the room). 'In the river' (swimming inside) vs 'on the river' (floating on surface).
• Fixed prepositional phrases (frequently tested):
– Acquit someone OF a crime; Agree with a person / to a proposal / about a topic; Angry with a person / at a situation; Afraid of; Beside oneself with emotion; Different from/than (both acceptable, 'from' preferred in formal writing); Expectation of; Good at / Bad at; Interested in; Proud of; Responsible for; Succeed in; Similar to; Superior/Inferior to (NOT 'of'); Familiar with (person knows thing) / Familiar to (thing known by person); Marry someone / Married to someone; Listen to; Look at / Look for (search) / Look after (take care) / Look into (investigate).
• Commonly confused pairs: 'below' (figuratively lower status) vs 'under' (physical position); 'beside' (next to) vs 'besides' (in addition to); 'except' (exclusion from same category) vs 'except for' (exception to general statement); 'since' (point in time) vs 'for' (duration); 'lie' (recline, intransitive — lay/lain/lain) vs 'lay' (put down, transitive — laid/laid/laid) vs 'lie' (tell untruth, regular — lied/lied/lied).
• Prepositions that commonly get omitted wrongly: depend ON, insist ON, approve OF, apologize FOR, accuse OF, consist OF, succeed IN, rely ON.

8. Conjunctions

Conjunctions join words, phrases, or clauses.

• Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS): for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. Join equal grammatical units. Comma before coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses.
• Correlative conjunctions: either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also, both...and, whether...or. When joining subjects, verb agrees with the nearer subject (proximity rule — see Subject-Verb Agreement).
• Subordinating conjunctions: introduce dependent clauses. Causal — because, since, as, owing to the fact that; Temporal — when, while, before, after, until, till, as soon as, by the time; Conditional — if, unless, provided that, assuming that; Purpose — so that, in order that; Concession — although, though, even though, while (whereas); Contrast — whereas, while; Reason — since, as.
• 'Although/Though' vs 'But': Don't use both in the same sentence. 'Although he is poor, he is happy' (correct). 'Although he is poor, but he is happy' (incorrect). In Hindi-influenced English, 'although...but' is a common error.
• 'Because' vs 'Since/As': 'Because' gives direct reason (strongest); 'since/as' imply known or less direct cause.
• 'Unless' = 'if...not'. 'Unless you work hard, you will fail' = 'If you do not work hard...' Do NOT say 'unless if'.
• 'So...that' vs 'such...that': 'So' modifies adjectives/adverbs (so brave that); 'such' modifies nouns (such bravery that / such a brave man that). Watch the article placement: 'such a brave man' not 'such brave man'.

9. Active and Passive Voice

In active voice, the subject performs the action. In passive voice, the subject receives the action.

• Formation: Object of active → Subject of passive + be (in appropriate tense) + V3 (past participle) + by + Agent (optional).
• Tense-wise passive forms:
– Simple Present: writes → is written
– Present Continuous: is writing → is being written
– Present Perfect: has written → has been written
– Simple Past: wrote → was written
– Past Continuous: was writing → was being written
– Past Perfect: had written → had been written
– Simple Future: will write → will be written
– Future Perfect: will have written → will have been written
– Modal: can write → can be written
• When the active object is a gerund/participle, passive uses infinitive: 'They saw him crossing the street' → 'He was seen crossing the street' (not 'to cross' — except with certain verbs of perception where the infinitive without 'to' is used: 'He was seen to cross the street' — both accepted, GPSC prefers gerund retained).
• Verbs that do NOT take passive: intransitive verbs (arrive, sleep, die, happen, occur, rise, fall, escape, exist). Exception: causative have/get (have something done).
• Impersonal passive with reporting verbs: 'It is said that...' / 'He is said to...' (say, think, believe, expect, consider, report, know). 'People say he is rich' → 'He is said to be rich' (NOT 'It is said him to be rich').
• Double-object verbs: Two possible passives. 'He gave me a pen' → 'I was given a pen' OR 'A pen was given to me'. Prefer the human object as subject.
• Questions in passive: 'Did they complete the work?' → 'Was the work completed by them?'
• 'Be' vs 'Get' passive: 'Get' is informal (He got injured).

10. Direct and Indirect (Reported) Speech

Direct speech quotes exact words. Indirect speech reports the meaning.

• Removal of quotation marks. Use 'that' as connector for statements (optional in informal).
• Pronoun changes: Speaker's 'I/me/my' → third person per context; listener's 'you' → first person per context.
• Tense backshift (when reporting verb is in past):
– Present Simple → Past Simple
– Present Continuous → Past Continuous
– Present Perfect → Past Perfect
– Past Simple → Past Perfect
– Past Continuous → Past Perfect Continuous
– Will → Would; Can → Could; May → Might; Shall → Should; Must → Had to / Must (obligation rule stays)
• Exceptions to backshift: universal truths, habitual actions still true, reporting verb in present/future, subjunctive mood, past perfect already in direct speech.
• Questions:
– Yes/No questions: use 'if' or 'whether' + subject + verb (He asked if I was coming.)
– WH- questions: keep WH-word + subject + verb (She asked where he had gone.)
– Do/Does/Did helpers are removed; verb carries tense.
• Commands/Requests: use 'to + infinitive' or 'not to + infinitive'. Imperative → to-infinitive. Strong commands: 'ordered/told/forbade'. 'He told me to go.' Negative command: 'He warned me not to touch.'
• Exclamations: convert to statement form. 'What a beautiful flower!' → He exclaimed that it was a very beautiful flower.
• Important: 'Had better' → 'had better' (no change); 'Would rather' → 'would rather'; 'Should' → 'should'; 'Ought to' → 'ought to'. GPSC often tests these invariant modals in reported speech.

11. Modals and Semi-Modals

Modals are auxiliary verbs expressing modality: necessity, possibility, permission, ability, obligation, advice.

• Core modals: can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must. Characteristics: no -s in third person; followed by base verb (without 'to'); form questions by inversion; negative by adding 'not'.
• Can/Could: ability (I can swim), permission (informal — Can I go?), possibility (It can get cold here). 'Could' is past of can, also softer/more polite.
• May/Might: permission (formal), possibility (may = more likely, might = less likely). 'May I come in?' (permission). 'It may rain.' (possibility).
• Will/Would: 'Will' for future, willingness, requests. 'Would' for conditional, polite request, past habit.
• Shall: formal future with I/we; suggestion/offer with you? (Shall we go?)
• Should: advice, duty, expectation. 'You should study harder.' Not obligation (must is stronger).
• Must: strong obligation, logical deduction (must be true). Negative 'must not' = prohibition. Past obligation: had to.
• Ought to: moral obligation (similar to should). Followed by 'to'.
• Semi-modals: need, dare, used to, have to, be to, be going to.
– 'Need' as modal: rare, mainly negative/interrogative (Need I say more?). As main verb: need(s) to.
– 'Dare' as modal: rarely used positively (How dare you!). As main verb: dares to/dared to.
– 'Used to' + base verb = past habit/state no longer true. 'I used to play' (now don't). 'Be used to' + V-ing/noun = accustomed to. 'I am used to waking up early.' 'Get used to' = process of becoming accustomed.
– 'Have to' = external obligation (similar to must but less strong). Past: had to. Negative: don't have to = lack of obligation (different from must not = prohibition).
• Modals of deduction: must (near certainty positive), can't/couldn't (near certainty negative), may/might/could (possibility).

12. Conditionals and Subjunctive Mood

Conditionals express results contingent on conditions.

• Zero conditional: general truth. If + present, present. 'If you heat water, it boils.'
• First conditional: real/possible future. If + present, will + base verb. 'If it rains, I will stay home.' Also may, can, must, imperative in result clause.
• Second conditional: unreal/imaginary present. If + past simple, would + base verb. 'If I were rich, I would travel.' (Note: 'were' used for all persons in formal English — If I were, If he were.)
• Third conditional: unreal past. If + past perfect, would have + V3. 'If I had studied, I would have passed.' Regret about past.
• Mixed conditionals: past condition → present result (If I had studied then, I would be in a good job now); present condition → past result (If I were smarter, I wouldn't have made that mistake).
• Inversion in conditionals (formal/literary): omit 'if' and invert subject-auxiliary. 'Were I rich...' (= If I were rich); 'Had I known...' (= If I had known); 'Should you need help...' (= If you should need). GPSC may test these structures in error detection.
• Subjunctive mood: used for wishes, demands, suggestions, hypothetical situations.
– After verbs like suggest, recommend, demand, insist, propose: base form of verb (present subjunctive). 'The doctor suggested that he rest.' (NOT 'rests'). 'I demand that she be present.'
– Wish + past simple (present regret): 'I wish I knew the answer.'
– Wish + past perfect (past regret): 'I wish I had studied harder.'
– If only + past simple/past perfect: similar to wish.
– It's time + past simple: 'It's time we went home.'
– As if/as though + past (unreal): 'He talks as if he knew everything.'
– Lest + should: 'He ran lest he should be seen.' (rare but tested).

13. Non-Finite Verbs: Gerunds, Infinitives, Participles

Non-finite verbs do not show tense, person, or number. They function as nouns, adjectives, or adverbs.

• Gerund (V-ing used as noun): Subject (Swimming is fun), Object (I enjoy swimming), After preposition (good at drawing), After certain verbs (avoid, admit, appreciate, consider, delay, deny, enjoy, finish, imagine, involve, keep, mind, miss, practice, risk, suggest, can't help, feel like). Formula: verb + gerund.
• Infinitive (to + base verb): Subject (To err is human), Object (I want to go), After adjectives (easy to understand), Purpose (I came to see you), After certain verbs (agree, aim, appear, arrange, ask, attempt, decide, expect, hope, learn, manage, need, offer, plan, pretend, promise, refuse, seem, tend, want, will). Structure: verb + infinitive.
• Verb patterns (highly tested in GPSC):
– Verb + object + to-infinitive: advise, ask, allow, encourage, expect, force, hint, invite, intend, invite, remind, teach, tell, warn.
– Verb + gerund (NOT infinitive): admit, avoid, consider, discuss, enjoy, finish, imagine, keep, miss, practice, quit, suggest, risk.
– Verb + object + gerund: advise someone doing, stop someone doing, prevent someone from doing.
– Verbs changing meaning: stop to do (pause in order to) vs stop doing (cease the action); remember to do (future task) vs remember doing (past memory); try to do (make effort) vs try doing (experiment); need to do (require) vs need doing (requires being done = passive meaning); have to do (obligation) vs had better do (strong advice).
• Participle:
– Present participle (V-ing): active meaning, ongoing action. 'The crying baby needs milk.' (The baby is crying.)
– Past participle (V3): passive/completed meaning. 'The broken vase lies on the floor.' (The vase was broken.)
– Perfect participle (Having + V3): action completed before another. 'Having finished the work, he went home.'
– Participle phrases reduce relative clauses: 'The man who is standing there' → 'The man standing there'.
– Dangling participles are errors: 'Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful.' (Trees can't walk.) Correct: 'Walking down the street, I thought the trees looked beautiful.'

14. Clauses: Noun, Adjective (Relative), and Adverb

A clause contains a subject and a verb. Independent clauses stand alone; dependent (subordinate) clauses cannot.

• Noun clauses: Function as noun — subject, object, complement, apposition. Introduced by: that, whether, if, wh- words (what, who, whom, which, when, where, why, how). Examples: 'That he passed is surprising.' (subject) 'I know what he wants.' (object) 'The question is whether we can afford it.' (complement) 'His belief that honesty is best remains unchanged.' (apposition). 'That' in noun clause is not omitted in subject position but may be omitted in object position in informal English. 'What' = the thing that; 'Whoever' = any person who; 'However' = no matter how. Common error: using 'that' where 'what' is needed ('I know that he did' is fine as noun clause, but 'I know what he did' is different meaning).
• Adjective/Relative clauses: Modify nouns. Introduced by relative pronouns (who, whom, which, that, whose) or relative adverbs (when, where, why).
– Defining (restrictive): essential to identify the noun; no commas; 'that' preferred. 'The book that I bought is interesting.'
– Non-defining (non-restrictive): extra information; commas required; 'that' cannot be used; relative pronoun cannot be omitted. 'My brother, who lives in Delhi, is a doctor.' (NOT 'my brother that lives in Delhi' in non-defining).
– Relative adverbs: 'where' (place), 'when' (time), 'why' (reason). 'The house where I was born' / 'The day when we met' / 'The reason why he left'. Note: 'The reason is because' is colloquially common but considered redundant; prefer 'The reason is that'.
– Preposition + relative pronoun: 'the house in which I live' (formal) vs 'the house which I live in' (informal). Cannot use 'that' after preposition.
• Adverb clauses: Function as adverbs — modify verb/adjective/whole clause. Types: time (when, while, before, after, until, since, as soon as), place (where, wherever), reason (because, since, as, now that), purpose (so that, in order that), condition (if, unless, provided that), concession (although, though, even though, while), manner (as, as if), comparison (than, as...as). Note: adverb clauses are NOT set off by commas when they follow the main clause (except for contrast/concession for clarity).

15. Sentence Types and Structures

Sentences classified by purpose and structure.

• By purpose: Declarative (statement), Interrogative (question), Imperative (command/request), Exclamatory (strong emotion).
• By structure: Simple (one independent clause), Compound (two+ independent clauses joined by FANBOYS or semicolon), Complex (one independent + one+ dependent clause), Compound-Complex (two+ independent + one+ dependent).
• Parallelism: Items in a series or paired structures must share the same grammatical form. 'She likes reading, writing, and to paint.' → 'She likes reading, writing, and painting.' Or 'She likes to read, to write, and to paint.' Mix is error.
• Commas: (a) Between items in a list (Oxford comma optional but recommended in formal writing); (b) Before coordinating conjunction joining independent clauses; (c) After introductory elements (Although he was tired, he continued.); (d) Around non-defining relative clauses and parenthetical expressions; (e) Between coordinate adjectives (a long, narrow table).
• Semicolon: Joins closely related independent clauses without conjunction; separates complex list items containing commas. 'I have two dogs; they are very friendly.' NOT used to join dependent and independent clause.
• Colon: Introduces explanation, list, or quote after an independent clause. 'She had one goal: to win.' Do NOT use colon after preposition or verb.
• Apostrophe: Possession (Ravi's book — singular; Ravis' books — plural ending in s); contraction (don't, it's = it is, NOT its = possessive).
• Common punctuation errors tested: comma splice (joining two independent clauses with only a comma — use semicolon or conjunction); run-on sentences; missing comma after introductory phrase; misplaced apostrophe (its vs it's).

16. Transformation of Sentures: Common Patterns

GPSC frequently tests sentence transformation. Master these patterns:

• Affirmative ↔ Negative (keeping same meaning): 'Nobody can do this' ↔ 'Everybody cannot do this'; 'I have no choice' ↔ 'I have no alternative'; 'The patient is too weak to walk' ↔ 'The patient is so weak that he cannot walk'; 'It is rare to find a honest man' ↔ 'Hardly any honest man is found'.
• Positive ↔ Positive (synonym substitution): 'He is addicted to drinking' ↔ 'He has a drinking habit'; 'He is absent-minded' ↔ 'He does not pay attention'; 'He is bent on killing' ↔ 'He is determined to kill'.
• Single word ↔ Phrase: 'He is well-off' ↔ 'He is wealthy'; 'He has a good command over' ↔ 'He is proficient in'; 'To cut a long story short' ↔ 'In brief'.
• Degree transformations: 'Too + adj + to' ↔ 'So + adj + that + cannot'. 'The box is too heavy to lift' ↔ 'The box is so heavy that it cannot be lifted.' 'Enough' placement: adj + enough (good enough) vs enough + noun (enough time).
• Comparative ↔ Superlative: 'No other city is as populous as Mumbai' ↔ 'Mumbai is the most populous city.' 'Ram is taller than any other boy' ↔ 'Ram is the tallest boy.' 'Nothing is more precious than life' ↔ 'Life is the most precious thing.'
• Assertive ↔ Interrogative: 'Who does not know Ram?' ↔ 'Everyone knows Ram.' 'Who but a fool would do this?' ↔ 'Only a fool would do this.'
• Assertive ↔ Exclamatory: 'It is a wonderful sight' ↔ 'What a wonderful sight it is!' 'How unfortunate!' ↔ 'It is very unfortunate.'
• Simple ↔ Compound/Complex: 'Being ill, he did not come' (Simple) → 'As he was ill, he did not come' (Complex). 'He died after drinking poison' → 'He died by drinking poison' (Complex: 'who drank poison, died').
• Note: In transformation, NEVER change the core meaning. The answer choices in MCQ format test precise equivalence.

17. Common Error Detection: High-Yield Rules

Error detection questions test habitual mistakes. Focus on these high-frequency error categories:

• Subject-Verb Agreement: (a) 'Each of the boys have...' → 'has'; (b) 'The number of students are...' → 'is'; (c) 'One of the students who is...' → wrong; should be 'are' (who refers to students) OR correct if 'the only one of the students who is'; (d) 'Bread and butter are...' → 'is' (same thing); (e) 'News are...' → 'is'; (f) 'Physician and poet is...' → 'is' (same person).
• Tense consistency: Mixing tenses without justification. 'He said he goes to Delhi yesterday.' → 'went'.
• Wrong preposition: 'He is good in math' → 'good at math'; 'He is afraid from snakes' → 'afraid of snakes'; 'She married to a doctor' → 'married a doctor'.
• Wrong article: 'He is an M.A.' → 'an MA' (pronounced em-ay); 'He is the honest man' (general) → 'an honest man'; 'Go to the bed' (sleep) vs 'Go to bed'.
• Wrong word choice (homophones/near-homophones): their/there/they're; your/you're; its/it's; affect/effect; influence/influence; principal/principle; complement/compliment; lie/lay; raise/reign/raze; accept/except; advice/advise; practice/practise (noun/verb in British English); loose/lose; affect/effect; sure/surely; silent/silent.
• Double negative: 'I don't need no help' → 'I don't need any help'; 'Nobody hasn't done it' → 'Everybody has done it'.
• Wrong modifier: 'Very much pleased' (correct) vs 'Much pleasing'; dangling modifier; misplaced modifier.
• Wrong comparative: 'More better' → 'better'; 'Most best' → 'best'; 'Far more better' → 'far better'. Comparative with 'than' not 'then'.
• Wrong verb form after preposition: 'He is interested in to learn' → 'in learning'; 'She avoids to go out' → 'avoid going'; 'He admitted to having stolen' (after prep 'to', gerund).
• Redundancy: 'The reasons are because...' → 'The reason is that...' or 'It is because...'; 'The end result' (end = result); 'Free gift' (gift = free); 'Round in shape' (round = shape).

18. Determiners and Quantifiers

Determiners specify reference of nouns. They include articles, demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers, numbers, and distributives.

• 'Some' vs 'Any': 'Some' in affirmative sentences, offers, requests (Would you like some tea?); 'Any' in negatives and questions. 'Do you have any money?' 'I don't have any money.' 'Some' can appear in questions when offering/asking (Would you like some water?).
• 'Much' vs 'Many': Much with uncountable (much water, much time); Many with countable plural (many books, many people). 'Too much/too many'; 'A lot of' works with both (informal).
• 'Little' vs 'A little': Little = barely enough (negative connotation); A little = some (positive). 'He has little money' (almost none); 'He has a little money' (some).
• 'Few' vs 'A few': Few = almost none (negative); A few = some (positive). 'Few students passed' (disappointment); 'A few students passed' (modest success).
• 'All' vs 'Whole': All + the/a/my + singular/uncountable/plural (all the time, all my friends); Whole + a/the + singular (the whole day, a whole week). 'All India' not 'Whole India'.
• 'Each' vs 'Every': Each = individual members (two or more, emphasis on separateness); Every = group viewed as whole (three or more). 'Each student has his own book' (individual); 'Every student passed' (group).
• 'Either' vs 'Neither': Either = one or the other (of two); Neither = not one nor the other. 'Either of the two' takes singular verb. 'Neither of them is...' (formal) / 'are...' (informal acceptance).
• 'Fewer' vs 'Less': Fewer with countable plural (fewer people, fewer options); Less with uncountable (less water, less time). Exception: 'less than 10 years' (period as unit).
• 'Fewer' vs 'Less' is frequently tested. Also: 'Number of' (specific count, takes plural) vs 'Amount of' (uncountable quantity, takes singular).

19. Sentence Correction and One-Word Substitution

Sentence correction requires identifying the underlined/error segment and choosing the correct alternative. Strategy: read the whole sentence, locate the error type, eliminate wrong options systematically.

• Common correction patterns:
– Confused words: 'Their going there' → 'They're going there'; 'Its a beautiful day' → 'It's a beautiful day'; 'He said me that' → 'He told me that' (tell + indirect object + that-clause; say + that-clause + to indirect object).
– Wrong preposition after verb/adjective: correct per fixed usage lists above.
– Wrong tense: ensure consistency and logical sequence.
– Wrong article: check countability, specificity, uniqueness.
– Wrong modifier placement: ensure modifier is near the word it modifies.

20. Exam Strategy and High-Yield Revision Checklist

GPSC English Language grammar questions are predominantly objective. Focus your preparation on these high-yield areas:

• Highest frequency topics: Subject-Verb Agreement (each, none, number of, collective nouns); Tense consistency; Active-Passive transformation; Direct-Indirect transformation; Preposition collocations; Article usage; Error detection (confused words, wrong prepositions, double negatives); Sentence transformation (too/so, such/a, one-word substitutions); Non-finite verb patterns (gerund vs infinitive after specific verbs).
• Quick revision checklist:
– Every/each + singular verb. One of the + plural noun + plural verb in relative clause; but the only one of the + singular verb.
– The number is; A number are.
– Neither/nor, either/or: verb agrees with nearest subject.
– With/along with/as well as do NOT change subject number.
– Too + adj + to inf = so + adj + that clause.
– Enough comes AFTER adjective, BEFORE noun: good enough; enough time.
– Gerunds after prepositions ALWAYS: interested in learning, good at swimming, accused of stealing.
– Remember to do (future task) vs remember doing (past memory).
– Stop to do (pause for purpose) vs stop doing (cease activity).
– Say + that-clause; Tell + person + that-clause; Ask + person + to-infinitive.
– Its (possessive) vs It's (it is); Their (possessive) vs They're (they are) vs There (place).
– Fewer (countable) vs Less (uncountable).
– Principal (head/main) vs Principle (rule); Complement (complete/praise) vs Compliment (praise/greeting).
– Less = uncountable, Fewer = countable. 'Less than 5 kg' (weight as measure).
– Although/Though never paired with 'but' in same sentence.
– Unless = If...not. Don't use 'if' after 'unless'.
– In spite of / Despite + noun/gerund (NO 'of' after despite).
– Due to + noun (attributable to); Because of + clause/noun (result of).
– Since (time/reason) vs For (duration).
• Practice recommendation: Solve previous years' papers and practice sets focusing on error detection and transformation. Learn the top 100 one-word substitutions listed above by heart. Drill subject-verb agreement and reported speech rules until automatic.

Important facts

  • GPSC English Language grammar carries significant weight in the preliminary objective paper. Questions are mostly MCQ-based error detection, sentence improvement, transformation, and one-word substitution.
  • Subject-Verb Agreement is the single most frequently tested grammar concept. Master each/every, none, the number vs a number, collective nouns, and proximity rule.
  • Active-Passive and Direct-Indirect Speech transformations require mechanical accuracy. Practice tense backshifting rules and exception handling.
  • Gerund vs Infinitive after specific verbs is a high-yield area. Make a personal list of verbs followed by gerund vs infinitive.
  • Preposition collocations are tested as fixed phrases. Learn verb+preposition, adjective+preposition, and noun+preposition combinations.
  • One-word substitutions (OWS) form a dedicated question type. Focus on personality traits, beliefs, literary terms, and Greek/Latin-derived vocabulary.
  • Transformation of sentences tests equivalence: too/so, such/a, degree, assertive/exclamatory/interrogative. Preserve original meaning exactly.
  • Punctuation rules (comma, semicolon, colon, apostrophe) appear occasionally but are easy marks if memorized.
  • Confused words (homophones/near-homophones) are a persistent error source. Learn distinctions: their/there/they're, its/it's, affect/effect, lie/lay, rise/raise.
  • Sentence transformation preserves meaning. Never change the core proposition when converting between forms.
  • Subjunctive mood after suggest/demand/insist uses base verb form, not third-person -s. This is a classic GPSC trap.
  • Relative clause comprehension: defining vs non-defining, use of 'that', omission rules, and preposition placement are all tested separately.

Formulas and relationships

Active → Passive: Object + be(V in tense) + V3 + by + Subject. Tense mapping: does → is done; is doing → is being done; has done → has been done; did → was done; was doing → was being done; had done → had been done; will do → will be done; can do → can be done.
Direct → Indirect (Statement): that + subject pronoun change + tense backshift + time expression change. Reporting verb in past → present becomes past; past becomes past perfect; will becomes would; can becomes could.
Direct → Indirect (Question): WH-word/if + subject + verb (no do/does/did helper). Yes/No: if/whether. Imperative: to-infinitive.
Too + Adj + to Infinitive = So + Adj + that Clause + cannot. 'The box is too heavy to lift' = 'The box is so heavy that it cannot be lifted.'
Such + a/an + Adj + Singular Noun = So + Adj + a/an + Singular Noun. 'Such a brave man' = 'So brave a man.'
Each/Every/One of the + Singular Noun → Singular Verb. 'Each of the boys has...' NOT 'have'.
The number of + Plural Noun → Singular Verb. A number of + Plural Noun → Plural Verb. 'The number of applicants is large.' 'A number of applicants are waiting.'
Collective Noun → Singular Verb (acting as unit) / Plural Verb (members acting individually). 'The committee has decided.' 'The committee were divided.'
Gerund after Preposition: Preposition + V-ing ALWAYS. 'Good at swimming', 'Accused of stealing', 'Interested in learning'.
Neither/Nor, Either/Or, Not only/But also → Verb agrees with NEAREST subject (Proximity Rule). 'Neither the teacher nor the students were present.'
With/Along with/As well as + Noun → Does NOT change subject number. Verb agrees with MAIN subject only. 'The captain, along with the players, is coming.'
One of the + Plural Noun + Relative Clause → Plural Verb (relative refers to plural noun). 'The only one of the + Plural Noun + Relative Clause → Singular Verb (relative refers to 'one').
Used to + Base Verb (past habit). Be used to + V-ing/Noun (accustomed). Get used to + V-ing (process of adaptation).
Wish + Past Simple (present regret). Wish + Past Perfect (past regret). If only + Past Simple/Past Perfect (same as wish).
Subjunctive after suggest/demand/insist/propose: that + subject + BASE VERB (no -s). 'The judge recommended that he be released.'
Fewer + Countable Plural. Less + Uncountable. Exception: time/money/distance as unit takes 'less'. 'Less than 10 km away.'
In spite of / Despite + Noun/Gerund (despite has NO 'of'). 'Despite the rain, we went out.' NOT 'despite of the rain.'
Say + that-clause. Tell + Person + that-clause. Ask + Person + to-infinitive. 'He said that he was tired.' 'He told me that he was tired.' 'He asked me to go.'

Common mistakes

  • Using 'more better' or 'most best' — double comparatives/superlatives are always wrong. Better and best are already comparative and superlative forms.
  • Writing 'despite of the rain' — 'despite' never takes 'of'. Use 'in spite of' or 'despite' (alone).
  • Using 'Although...but' in the same sentence — 'although' and 'but' cannot coexist. Choose one: 'Although he is poor, he is happy' OR 'He is poor but he is happy.'
  • Confusing 'its' (possessive) with 'it's' (it is). 'Its tail' vs 'It's raining.'
  • Confusing 'their/there/they're': 'Their house' (possessive), 'There is' (place/existence), 'They're happy' (they are).
  • Using 'fewer' with uncountable nouns: 'fewer water' is wrong → 'less water'. Using 'less' with countable nouns: 'less books' is wrong → 'fewer books'.
  • Wrong gerund/infinitive after specific verbs: 'I avoid to go out' → 'I avoid going out'. 'I want to going' → 'I want to go'. Memorize verb patterns.
  • Subject-verb disagreement with 'each of the boys have' → should be 'each of the boys has'. 'Each' is always singular.
  • Incorrect passive of intransitive verbs: 'He was died' is wrong — 'die' is intransitive, no passive exists. 'He died.'
  • Confusing 'lie' (recline) with 'lay' (put down): lie-lied-lied-lied (untruth); lie-lay-lain-lying (recline); lay-laid-laid-laying (put down). GPSC loves this distinction.
  • Using 'who' after preposition in formal English — use 'whom': 'To whom it concerns' not 'To who'. Though 'who' is increasingly accepted informally.
  • Adding '-to' after modals: 'I can to swim' is wrong — modals take bare infinitive. 'I can swim.'
  • Dangling modifier: 'Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful.' — trees cannot walk. Correct: 'Walking down the street, I thought the trees looked beautiful.'
  • Confused 'affect' (verb: to influence) with 'effect' (noun: result). 'The effect of the policy was positive.' 'The policy affected everyone.'
  • Confused 'principal' (head/main) with 'principle' (rule). 'The school principal' vs 'Moral principle.'
  • Wrong article before subjects ending in -ics: 'Physics are interesting' is wrong — branch of study is singular: 'Physics is interesting.'
  • Omitting comma in non-defining relative clause: 'My brother, who lives in Delhi, is a doctor.' — commas required because it's non-defining.
  • Using 'that' in non-defining relative clause: 'My car, that I bought yesterday...' is wrong — use 'which' in non-defining clauses.
  • Wrong preposition after adjective: 'angry at person' (wrong) → 'angry with person'; 'angry at situation' (correct). 'Afraid of snakes' not 'afraid from snakes.'
  • Redundancy: 'The reason is because...' → 'The reason is that...' or 'It is because...' Both 'reason' and 'because' indicate cause — using both is redundant.
