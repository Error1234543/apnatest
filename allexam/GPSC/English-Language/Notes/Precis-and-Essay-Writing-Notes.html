Precis and Essay Writing — GPSC English Language

GPSCEnglishNotes

1. What Is a Précis?

A précis is a concise, coherent summary that captures the central idea and essential supporting points of an original passage in the writer's own words, at roughly one-third of the original length. It is not a mere collection of excerpts stitched together, nor is it an opinion piece. Think of it as a faithful distillation: the skeleton remains intact but all non-essential flesh is removed. In the GPSC English Language paper, précis writing tests your ability to read carefully, distinguish signal from noise, and reproduce meaning accurately and economically. The passage chosen may be expository, argumentative, narrative, or descriptive, but the core skill demanded is the same — compressing without distorting.

2. Key Principles and Rules of Précis Writing

Follow these rules strictly; they are the single most tested aspect of this topic in GPSC:

• ONE IDEA RULE: Identify the single controlling idea (theme) of the original passage. Every sentence in your précis should serve that central point.
• OWN WORDS: Do not copy sentences verbatim from the passage. Paraphrase everything. The examiner checks for plagiarism of phrasing, not just meaning.
• ONE-THIRD LENGTH: Aim for approximately one-third of the original word count. If the passage is 300 words, your précis should be around 90–100 words. GPSC typically specifies the required reduction.
• NO EXAMPLES OR ILLUSTRATIONS: Remove all specific examples, anecdotes, quotations, numerical data used for illustration, and rhetorical flourishes. Retain only the logical argument or factual claim they support.
• COHERENCE OVER BULLET POINTS: Write the précis as a flowing paragraph, not as a list of points. Use appropriate conjunctions so the summary reads smoothly.
• THIRD-PERSON NEUTRAL VOICE: Maintain an objective tone. Avoid 'I think', 'In my opinion', or any first-person intervention.
• PRESERVE THE ORIGINAL MEANING: Never add information that was absent from the passage, and never omit a point that changes the author's argument.
• SUITABLE TITLE: Provide a short, descriptive heading that captures the theme.

3. Step-by-Step Method for Writing a Précis

Follow this sequence during the exam to maximise accuracy and save time:

Step 1 — Read the passage through completely once, without underlining, to grasp the overall sense and tone.
Step 2 — Read again slowly. Underline or mentally mark the topic sentence of each paragraph or logical unit. These are your anchor points.
Step 3 — Identify and eliminate: Cross out every example, illustration, repetition, figurative language, secondary detail, and rhetorical device. What remains should be only claims, reasons, and conclusions.
Step 4 — Draft the skeleton: Write down, in your own words, the central idea plus each essential supporting point in logical order. Keep each point to one line maximum.
Step 5 — Compose the draft: Convert your skeleton into a continuous paragraph. Use linking words (however, therefore, consequently, although) to preserve the logical flow.
Step 6 — Count and trim: Tally words. If you are over one-third, remove secondary qualifiers and combine sentences. If you are far under, check whether an essential point was missed.
Step 7 — Proofread: Check for distortions, copied phrases, grammatical errors, and whether the title matches the content. A correct title is usually awarded marks even if the précis body needs minor work.

4. Types of Titles for a Précis

A good précis title is short (usually 3–8 words), neutral in tone, and reflects the theme rather than repeating the passage's first sentence verbatim. Examples of effective titles include:

• The Need for Environmental Sustainability
• Challenges of Urbanisation in India
• The Importance of Moral Education
• Digital Literacy and Its Implications

Avoid titles that are too vague (e.g., 'Environment' or 'Education') or too sensational (e.g., 'Catastrophe Ahead!'). In GPSC, a well-chosen title can differentiate an average précis from a good one.

5. What Is an Essay? Structure and Forms

An essay is a composed piece of writing, usually in prose, presenting the author's perspective on a particular topic. In the GPSC exam, essays tend to be expository or argumentative in nature — you may be asked to explain a concept, analyse a problem, or defend a position.

Standard three-part structure:

• INTRODUCTION (approx. 10–15% of total length): Begin with a hook or contextual statement, define key terms if necessary, and present your thesis — the central argument or focus of the essay.
• BODY PARAGRAPHS (approx. 70–80%): Develop your argument in logically ordered paragraphs. Each paragraph should have a clear topic sentence, supporting evidence or reasoning, and a concluding or transitional sentence. Use 3–5 body paragraphs depending on word requirement.
• CONCLUSION (approx. 10–15%): Restate the thesis in different words, summarise key arguments briefly, and end with a forward-looking statement, recommendation, or broader implication. Avoid introducing entirely new ideas in the conclusion.

Common essay types in GPSC:
- Problem-solution essay
- Cause-effect essay
- Comparative essay
- Opinion/argumentative essay
- Descriptive-explanatory essay

6. Essay Planning and Outlining Technique

Before writing, spend 5–7 minutes planning. This prevents structural failure mid-way and saves time.

Outlining steps:
1. Keyword extraction: Write the given topic and brainstorm associated keywords in a quick mind map.
2. Group related ideas: Cluster your keywords into 3–4 thematic groups — these become your body paragraphs.
3. Decide the angle: Choose whether the essay will argue a position, describe phenomena, or analyse causes and effects.
4. Draft a micro-outline: Jot one line per paragraph — introduction thesis, body topic sentences, and conclusion direction.
5. Select opening and closing: Prepare a strong opening line (quote, statistic, definition, or striking observation) and a tentative closing thought.

Example — Topic: 'Importance of Women Empowerment':
- Intro: Define empowerment + state thesis (empowerment is essential for balanced development)
- Body 1: Economic empowerment and its impact
- Body 2: Social and political dimensions
- Body 3: Challenges and government initiatives
- Conclusion: Call for sustained multi-dimensional effort

7. Essay Writing: Language, Style, and Presentation

GPSC values clarity and structure over literary flourish. Follow these style guidelines:

• Formal register: Avoid slang, colloquial expressions, contractions (don't, can't), and conversational fillers (well, you know).
• Variety in sentence structure: Mix simple, compound, and complex sentences to maintain reader engagement.
• Paragraph unity: One idea per paragraph. Do not mix economic arguments with social ones in the same paragraph.
• Transitional devices: Use words like furthermore, on the other hand, consequently, in contrast, similarly, nevertheless to guide the reader.
• Factual support: Where relevant, cite well-known facts, constitutional provisions, committee names, or government schemes to strengthen arguments — but do not fabricate data.
• Balanced tone: Acknowledge counter-arguments where appropriate; this demonstrates critical thinking.
• Spelling and grammar: Even one repeated error can lower impressions. Write legibly if handwritten; keep within word limits strictly.
• Length discipline: If the question asks for 300 words, write 280–320. Going significantly over or under attracts penalties in marking.

8. Common Mistakes in Précis and Essay Writing (GPSC Focus)

Précis — frequent errors:
• Copying sentences directly instead of paraphrasing.
• Including examples, quotations, or statistics from the passage.
• Adding personal opinions or external information.
• Producing a bullet-point summary instead of a flowing paragraph.
• Writing a title that repeats the passage title verbatim.
• Ignoring word limit — both over-length and excessively short précis lose marks.

Essay — frequent errors:
• Starting without a plan and going off-topic.
• Writing an extremely long introduction at the expense of body paragraphs.
• Using informal or emotive language ('I strongly believe this is absolutely terrible').
• Repetition of the same point in different words across paragraphs.
• Presenting only one-sided arguments without acknowledging complexity.
• Poor paragraphing — writing one massive block or too many tiny paragraphs.
• Neglecting the conclusion or making it identical to the introduction.
• Spelling mistakes in key terms and improper punctuation throughout.

9. GPSC Exam Weightage and Expected Question Pattern

In the GPSC English Language paper, précis and essay writing typically carry significant marks, often appearing as a long-answer descriptive question worth 15–25 marks combined or separately. A common pattern includes:

• One précis-writing question: A passage of 250–350 words is provided, and you must reduce it to approximately one-third with a suitable title.
• One essay question: A topic of contemporary, social, cultural, or administrative relevance is given, with a word requirement (usually 300–500 words).

Occasionally, the paper may offer a choice between two topics for the essay. Preparation should therefore cover both skills equally. Since GPSC uses a descriptive-mains style for this section, practice under timed conditions — aim for 25–30 minutes per task during preparation.

10. Worked Example — Précis Writing

Original passage (illustrative, ~270 words):
'The rapid pace of urbanisation in India has created enormous pressure on infrastructure and public services. Cities are expanding faster than roads, water supply systems, sewage networks, and housing can keep up with. Millions of rural migrants flock to metropolitan areas seeking employment, but they often end up in overcrowded slums with inadequate sanitation and limited access to healthcare. Traffic congestion has become a daily nightmare, polluting the air and wasting productive hours. Local municipal bodies, burdened by corruption and bureaucratic delays, struggle to deliver even basic amenities. Yet urbanisation also brings economic opportunities, cultural exchange, and innovation. If managed through smart city initiatives, decentralised governance, and sustainable planning, Indian cities could become engines of inclusive growth rather than symbols of chaos. The key lies in proactive policy-making that prioritises people over concrete.'

Analysis: Central theme = urbanisation challenges and the need for planned management. Eliminate: specific details about slums, traffic, corruption (examples). Retain: expansion pressure on infrastructure, rural-to-urban migration consequences, economic benefits, need for smart/sustainable planning.

Précis (~90 words):
Title: Managing Urbanisation in India
India's rapid urbanisation strains infrastructure, water, housing, and transport systems beyond their capacity. Rural-to-urban migration worsens overcrowding and inadequate civic amenities, while traffic congestion and pollution degrade quality of life. Municipal bodies face governance and corruption challenges in delivering services. However, urbanisation also generates economic opportunity and cultural dynamism. With smart-city planning, decentralised administration, and sustainable policies, Indian cities can transform from crisis-ridden centres into drivers of equitable growth. Proactive, people-oriented urban policy is therefore essential to harness urbanisation's potential while mitigating its hazards.

11. Worked Example — Essay Outline (Topic: Digital India)

Topic: 'Digital India: Promise and Challenges'

Micro-outline:
- Introduction: Define Digital India as a flagship programme launched in 2015 to ensure government services are available electronically and to improve digital literacy.
- Body 1 — Achievements: Universal internet access expansion, Aadhaar-linked service delivery, UPI revolution in payments, e-Governance platforms (DigiLocker, CoWin), increased financial inclusion through Jan Dhan-Aadhaar-Mobile triad.
- Body 2 — Challenges: Digital divide between urban and rural areas, cybersecurity threats, low digital literacy among elderly and marginalised groups, language barriers, infrastructural gaps in remote regions.
- Body 3 — Way Forward: Strengthening rural broadband, multilingual digital interfaces, cyber-security frameworks, community digital training, public-private partnerships for last-mile connectivity.
- Conclusion: Digital India represents a transformative vision; its success depends on inclusive implementation that leaves no citizen behind.

This outline ensures balanced coverage, logical flow, and sufficient depth for a 350–400 word essay suitable for GPSC.

Important facts

  • A précis is a concise summary in the writer's own words, ideally one-third the length of the original passage.
  • Never copy sentences directly from the passage; paraphrasing is mandatory for full marks.
  • A précis must be written as a single flowing paragraph, never as bullet points or a list.
  • All examples, illustrations, repetitions, and rhetorical devices must be eliminated from a précis.
  • Every précis requires a short, theme-based title of 3–8 words.
  • An essay follows a three-part structure: Introduction (thesis), Body (developed paragraphs), Conclusion (restatement + forward look).
  • Essay planning and outlining (5–7 minutes) is essential to prevent structural failure during writing.
  • Maintain formal register throughout both précis and essay; avoid contractions, slang, and first-person language.
  • In GPSC, précis and essay questions typically carry 15–25 marks each and require timed practice of 25–30 minutes per task.
  • Over-length or under-length submissions attract marking penalties in GPSC descriptive papers.

Formulas and relationships

Précis Length ≈ Original Word Count ÷ 3 (aim for ±5 words tolerance)
Essay Structure Ratio ≈ Introduction : Body : Conclusion = 10–15% : 70–80% : 10–15%
Title Length Guideline ≈ 3 to 8 words capturing the central theme

Common mistakes

  • Copying original sentences verbatim into the précis instead of fully paraphrasing — this is the single most common error and attracts heavy penalty.
  • Including examples, data, anecdotes, or illustrations that were merely supporting details in the original passage.
  • Writing the précis as a list of bullet points rather than a coherent paragraph with logical connectors.
  • Adding personal opinions, external facts, or conclusions not present in the original passage.
  • Producing a précis that is either too long (exceeding one-third) or so condensed that key arguments are lost.
  • Forgetting to provide a title, or writing a title that simply repeats the passage heading.
  • In essays, starting to write without a plan, leading to off-topic digressions or unbalanced structure.
  • Using informal language, contractions, or emotive expressions that downgrade the formal tone expected in GPSC.
  • Writing an overly long introduction and rushing the body or skipping the conclusion entirely.
  • Repeating the same argument across multiple paragraphs using only different wording instead of developing distinct points.
