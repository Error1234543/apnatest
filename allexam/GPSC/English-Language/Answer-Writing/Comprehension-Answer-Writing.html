Read the passage below and answer the questions that follow. “Education in India has undergone a profound transformation over the past three decades. Once confined to urban centres and accessible only to elites, schooling has gradually reached rural heartlands through mid-day meal schemes, scholarship programmes, and the Right to Education Act. Yet, despite these gains, learning outcomes remain a matter of serious concern. Annual surveys by organisations such as ASER continue to reveal that a significant proportion of children in upper-primary classes struggle with basic arithmetic and reading at a lower-primary level. The gap between enrollment and actual competence suggests that mere access to schools does not guarantee quality learning. Teacher absenteeism, overcrowded classrooms, rote-based pedagogy, and inadequate infrastructure remain stubborn obstacles. Reforming education demands more than legislative guarantees; it requires systemic rethinking of curriculum design, teacher training, community participation, and accountability mechanisms.” Answer each question in 2–3 sentences: a) What are the key achievements in Indian education mentioned in the passage? b) Why do learning outcomes remain poor despite increased access to schooling? c) What systemic reforms does the author propose?

Suggested points

  • a) The passage highlights three key achievements: (i) expansion of schooling beyond urban centres into rural areas, (ii) introduction of supportive measures such as mid-day meal schemes and scholarship programmes, and (iii) the enactment of the Right to Education Act, which provided a legal guarantee of access to schooling.
  • b) Poor learning outcomes persist because access alone does not ensure quality education. The passage identifies multiple factors: teacher absenteeism, overcrowded classrooms, reliance on rote-based teaching methods, and inadequate school infrastructure. These systemic weaknesses mean that while children are enrolled, they are not necessarily learning foundational skills.
  • c) The author argues that reform must go beyond legislation and address four areas: redesigning the curriculum to be more meaningful, strengthening teacher training, involving communities in the educational process, and establishing clear accountability mechanisms to ensure that schools function effectively.

Read the passage and answer the questions in 2–4 sentences each: “The Indian Constitution is often described as both flexible and rigid. Its amendment procedure under Article 368 varies depending on the nature of the change proposed. Some provisions can be amended by a simple majority in Parliament, others by a special majority, and still others require ratification by at least half of the state legislatures in addition to a special parliamentary majority. This graduated approach was deliberately designed by the framers to protect the basic structure of the Constitution while allowing necessary adaptations to changing social and political realities. Over the decades, the judiciary has played a crucial role in interpreting these boundaries. The landmark Kesavananda Bharati case established that Parliament cannot amend the ‘basic structure’ of the Constitution, thereby placing an implicit limit on amendment power. Critics argue that judicial review slows down constitutional reform, while supporters maintain that it prevents majoritarian encroachment on democratic foundations.” a) Explain the phrase ‘flexible yet rigid’ as applied to the Indian Constitution. b) What is the significance of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment? c) Summarise the debate between critics and supporters regarding judicial review of constitutional amendments.

Suggested points

  • a) The Constitution is called flexible because it can be amended through varied procedures suited to different types of changes—simple majority, special majority, or with state ratification. It is called rigid because certain core features cannot be altered, and the amendment process itself involves multiple checks that prevent hasty or arbitrary changes to the foundational document.
  • b) The Kesavananda Bharati judgment is significant because it introduced the ‘basic structure’ doctrine, ruling that Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 does not extend to destroying or altering its basic structure. This created an implicit constitutional limit on amending power and positioned the judiciary as the guardian of the Constitution’s core principles.
  • c) Critics believe judicial review delays necessary constitutional reforms by allowing courts to block parliamentary amendments, potentially hindering progressive change. Supporters counter that judicial oversight is essential to prevent the majority from undermining democratic foundations, minority rights, and other structural safeguards embedded in the Constitution.
