Definitions

  • Grammar: The systematic study of the rules governing the structure, usage, and composition of a language, including syntax, morphology, tense, voice, and punctuation.
  • Tense: A grammatical category that expresses the time of an action or state (past, present, future) along with aspect (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous).
  • Voice: The relationship between the subject and the verb; Active voice has the subject performing the action, while Passive voice has the subject receiving the action.
  • Reported/Indirect Speech: Reporting what someone said without quoting their exact words, requiring changes in pronouns, tenses, time expressions, and order.
  • Subject–Verb Agreement: The rule that a singular subject takes a singular verb form and a plural subject takes a plural verb form, regardless of intervening phrases.
  • Preposition: A word placed before a noun or pronoun to show its relation to another word, indicating direction, location, time, or method.
  • Conjunction: A word that joins clauses, sentences, or words together, including coordinating, subordinating, and correlative types.
  • Article: A determiner used before a noun; 'a/an' is indefinite, 'the' is definite, and zero article occurs in fixed expressions, generalisations, or proper nouns.
  • Modal Verb: A helping verb expressing ability, permission, obligation, possibility, or request (can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, ought to).

Key concepts

  • TENSES: The fourteen English tenses are organised by time (past/present/future) and aspect (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect continuous). Simple tenses express habitual or complete actions. Continuous tenses emphasise ongoing duration. Perfect tenses relate one time to another and signal completion before a reference point. Perfect continuous tenses stress duration up to a point. Common signals: always/usually/simple past = did; now/a moment ago/present continuous = is doing; already/yet/just/perfect = has done; since/for/over a period = has been doing. Note the difference between past simple (finished action with a specific time) and present perfect (result or relevance in the present, no specific time).
  • VOICE (Active / Passive): In active voice, the subject performs the action; in passive voice, the subject receives it. Conversion rule: object of active becomes subject of passive; main verb becomes past participle with the appropriate form of 'be'; agent appears after 'by' (optional when agent is unknown or unimportant). Tense mapping for passive: present simple → am/is/are + V3; past simple → was/were + V3; present continuous → is being + V3; past continuous → was being + V3; present perfect → has been + V3; past perfect → had been + V3; future → will be + V3; modals → modal + be + V3. Imperatives and infinitives become passive with the base form (e.g., 'Let the work be done'). Verbs with two objects allow two passive forms, e.g., Active: 'She gave him a pen' → Passive A: 'He was given a pen by her' / Passive B: 'A pen was given to him by her.' Not all verbs have a passive form (intransitive verbs such as arrive, die, happen, sleep, fall cannot be rendered passive).
  • REPORTED SPEECH: When changing direct speech to indirect speech, shift pronouns according to perspective, move tense one step back (present → past, past → past perfect, will → would), adjust time/place markers (now → then, today → that day, yesterday → the day before, here → there), and change question order from interrogative to statement form. Commands and requests use 'to + infinitive' after tell/order/ask. Questions beginning with a question word keep that word; yes/no questions use 'if' or 'whether'. Exceptions to backshift: universal truths, habitual facts, historical events, and situations still true at the time of reporting may retain the original tense. Modals behave differently: can → could, will → would, may → might, must → had to (for obligation); must stays 'must' for strong necessity/prohibition in formal contexts; should, could, might, ought to usually do not change.
  • SUBJECT–VERB AGREEMENT: Match verb number with the true subject, ignoring intervening prepositional phrases, appositives, or relative clauses. Key rules: (1) Each/every/somebody/nobody + singular verb. (2) 'Either…or', 'neither…nor', 'not only…but also' agree with the nearer subject. (3) Collectives (committee, team, government) take singular when acting as one unit, plural when members act individually. (4) Nouns appearing plural but denoting a single entity (mathematics, economics, news, measles) take singular verbs. (5) 'Each of', 'one of' + plural noun takes singular verb. (6) Amounts, distances, periods, sums of money expressed as a unit take singular verb. (7) Two subjects joined by 'and' generally take plural verb, except when they name a single idea (bread and butter is…; law and order is…).
  • PREPOSITIONS: Memorised collocations link certain adjectives, nouns, and verbs to specific prepositions (afraid of, interested in, depend on, consist of, accuse of, succeed in, belong to, complain about). Time usage: 'at' for clock time and festivals (at 5 p.m., at Diwali); 'on' for days/dates (on Monday, on 15 August); 'in' for months/years/seasons/long periods (in June, in 1947, in winter). Space usage: 'at' for specific points, 'on' for surfaces/lines, 'in' for enclosed spaces. Fixed expressions without preposition: arrive home, reach destination, go to school, come from Delhi. Beware false pairs like 'discuss about' (wrong — discuss is transitive) versus 'talk about'.
  • CONJUNCTIONS: Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) join equals. Subordinating conjunctions (although, because, since, if, unless, when, while, as, so that, provided that) introduce dependent clauses and mark concession, cause, condition, time, or purpose. Correlative pairs must stay parallel: both…and…, either…or…, neither…nor…, not only…but also…, whether…or…. Avoid double negatives ('I don't need no help' is incorrect). Use comma before coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses; do not use comma before subordinating conjunction introducing an adverbial clause at the start of a sentence only if clarity requires it.
  • ARTICLES: Use 'a/an' with singular countable nouns mentioned for the first time or classifying. Use 'the' with second mention, unique things (the sun, the Parliament), superlatives and ordinals (the best, the first), rivers, seas, mountain ranges, plural names of countries, newspapers. Zero article with plural/uncountable nouns in general sense (Lions are dangerous; Money doesn't grow on trees), meals, languages, card games, institutions when used for their primary purpose (go to school/prison/church means the function; go to the school/church means the building). Proper nouns usually take no article except where convention attaches one (the Ganges, the United States, the Alps).
  • MODALS: Core meanings: can/could for ability and permission; may/might for possibility and formal permission; must for strong obligation or logical certainty; shall/should for future, advice, duty; will/would for future, willingness, polite request. Negative modals: mustn't = prohibition; don't have to = absence of obligation. Semi-modals: need, dare, used to, have to, be to. Sequence: modal + base form of the next verb (no 'to' after can/will/must/should except in perfect/modals of deduction forms like must have done). Note distinction: 'had better' carries strong advice/warning; 'would rather' takes base form. In reported commands/requests use infinitive constructions rather than modal misuse.
  • COMMON STRUCTURAL TRANSFORMATIONS: Positive ↔ Negative with equivalent meaning (e.g., 'He is too poor to buy a car' ↔ 'He is so poor that he cannot buy a car'); Affirmative ↔ Comparative/Superlative (e.g., 'None other than Ram is as wise as Radha' ↔ 'Ram is the wisest of the two'); 'Let us' suggestions become 'Shall we…?'; Infinitive purpose clauses replace 'so that'/'in order that'; 'No sooner…than' replaces 'as soon as' with inversion; 'Hardly…when' parallels 'No sooner…than' but uses 'when'. These transformations appear frequently in editing/error-detection items.

Important facts

  • Tense signal checklist: simple past = yesterday/last week/in 1990; present perfect = already/yet/since/for/recently; past perfect = by the time/before/already when; present perfect continuous = for/since + duration emphasizing ongoing effect.
  • Voice exceptions requiring retention of active form: transitive verbs of becoming (become, prove, turn), intransitive verbs (happen, occur, die, sleep, rise, set, arrive, live, remain, lie, sit, stand), and verbs expressing change of state or possession (have, resemble, suit, fit, lack, want, cost) generally cannot be passivised.
  • Agreement priority rules: (i) Not … but …, Neither … nor …, Either … or → verb agrees with nearer subject. (ii) The number of + plural noun → singular verb; A number of + plural noun → plural verb. (iii) Here/There + subject → verb agrees with subject following. (iv) One and a half + plural noun → singular verb. (v) Rice and fish is my favourite meal (single idea) but Rice and fish are served (separate items).
  • Preposition collocation high-frequency list: absent from, accurate in, accused of, afraid of, advantage of, aware of, because of, busy with, capable of, concerned about, confused about, different from, equal to, fond of, good at/bad at, ignorant of, impatient of, independent of, interested in, jealous of, keen on, late for, proud of, responsible for, similar to, superior to, taste for, tired of, worthy of.
  • Reported speech pivot words: say → that + statement; ask → if/whether or wh- word + statement; command/order/request → to-infinitive; exclamation → interjection (oh, alas) dropped; imperative → positive/negative infinitive.
  • Article non-use anchors: proper names of individuals and most cities/streets; abstract nouns in general sense; singular countable nouns preceded by possessives/my/this/these. Article compulsory anchors: once-only mention (a famous poet), specific reference (the poet who wrote…), unique entities, ordinal/superlative references, river/sea/range/sea/ocean names, plural country names, daily newspaper names, certain fixed institutional phrases (the court, the police, the army).
  • Modal nuance traps: must = obligation/internal duty; have to = external compulsion; mustn't = prohibition; don't have to = no obligation; should = advice; ought to = moral duty; will = volition/future; would = conditional/polite; can = ability; could = past ability/polite; may = permission/possibility; might = remote possibility. Never use 'to' directly after core modals except in 'be able to','have to','ought to'.
  • Transformation rules shortcut: 'too…to' → 'so…that…cannot'; 'only…can/could' → 'none other than…is'; 'must…not' → 'had better not'; 'let us' → 'shall we'; 'despite/in spite of' → 'although'; 'no sooner…than' ↔ 'as soon as' with inversion; participial phrases can replace subordinate clauses where subject is same.

Memory aids

  • Tense map rhyme: 'Present I do; Past I did; Perfect I have done; Continuous is going; Perfect continuous have been going — time decides which one.'
  • Voice conversion ladder: write active → underline object → pick matching 'be' form for tense → add V3 → add 'by agent' if needed. Practice by converting one sentence across seven tenses until pattern is automatic.
  • FANBOYS acronym: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So — each joins balanced elements. Remember 'Never Nor' only after a negative clause to avoid double negative error: 'He did not run, nor did he walk.'
  • Articles via 'a/an' = 'any one' of a class; 'the' = 'the specific one'. Test: replace 'a' with 'one' or 'the' with 'that/this'; if meaning stays, article choice is likely correct.
  • Subject–verb proximity rule: imagine the verb standing closest to the real subject, strip away every prepositional phrase between them, then match number. For collective nouns, ask: is the group one unit or many individuals?
  • Preposition pairing mnemonic groups: fear-related → of/from; movement/time → at/on/in hierarchy; mental state → about/of/in; responsibility → for/of. Write three example sentences per common pair to lock the habit.
  • Modals of deduction: present certainty → must be; present impossibility → can't be; past certainty → must have been; past impossibility → can't have been. This four-box grid quickly sorts out tricky inference questions.
  • Transformation shortcut matrix: 'Too…to' ↔ 'So…that…cannot'; 'Only…can' ↔ 'None other than…is'; 'Described as' ↔ 'Reputed to be'; 'It is time' ↔ 'Should/Would rather' substitution; 'Would rather' ↔ 'Had rather' archaic alternative rarely tested but useful for older texts.

Exam tips

  • Treat the English Grammar section in GPSC as a qualifying, accuracy-first task. Focus on error detection, sentence improvement, transformation, and fill-in-the-blank with correct prepositions/articles/modals, since these dominate objective practice.
  • Apply the 'strip-and-check' method for subject–verb agreement: remove all prepositional and parenthetical material between subject and verb, identify the head noun, then verify number and person before selecting the answer.
  • In voice/narration conversions, first locate the tense and mood of the main verb, then apply the corresponding transformation ladder without altering meaning. For commands and requests, use infinitive forms consistently; for questions, restore interrogative word order in the answer options.
  • When guessing prepositions, recall collocation clusters rather than isolated pairs: mental/emotional states cluster with 'of/about/in'; movement/location with 'at/on/in'; responsibility with 'for/of'. Eliminate obviously mismatched pairs first.
  • Use elimination for articles: if the noun is singular countable and nonspecific, choose 'a/an'; if specific, unique, or previously mentioned, choose 'the'; if abstract/general/plural or a proper name with conventional zero article, leave blank.
  • For modal blanks, read the full context for obligation vs possibility vs advice vs ability, and check the verb form that follows: after a core modal, look for bare infinitive; after 'to' or '-ing', suspect semi-modal or mistake.
  • Transformations require preserving meaning exactly. Check the equivalence by reversing the change: if you cannot convert your chosen transformed sentence back to the original, discard it as a distractor.
  • Avoid native-language interference patterns from Gujarati/Hindi grammar structures. Gujarati does not use articles or a passive voice morphologically identical to English; Hindi lacks a present-perfect/continuous distinction identical to English. Explicitly review English-specific category usage rather than relying on translation intuition.

Common traps

  • Confusing 'who' vs 'whom': 'who' is subject form (who called?), 'whom' is object form (whom did you see?). In formal writing and exams, preserve 'whom' after prepositions (to whom, with whom). Using 'whom' as subject is a persistent error.
  • Mixing up 'affect' (verb) and 'effect' (noun). Exception: 'effect' as verb means 'to bring about' (effect change); 'affect' as noun is rare psychological term. Default check: replace with 'affected/affected' to confirm usage.
  • Wrong preposition after adjective: 'different than' is increasingly accepted informally but formal/academic preference remains 'different from'; 'prefer X to Y' not 'prefer X than Y'; 'superior/inferior to' not 'than'; 'similar to' not 'like' in formal comparative context.
  • Misplaced modifiers: 'I saw a man with a telescope' suggests the man had the telescope; rephrase to 'With a telescope, I saw a man.' Dangling participles are a frequent source of ambiguity and error in transformation items.
  • Subject–verb agreement distraction: long intervening phrases trick the eye ('The list of items is long', not 'are'; 'The king, together with his ministers, was present'). Test by removing the interrupting phrase temporarily.
  • Reported-speech tense-shift oversight: failing to backshift when the reporting clause is past-tense; conversely, failing to keep present-tense for universal truths ('The Earth revolves around the Sun' even in reported form).
  • Article omission/comma splice confusion: omitting 'the' before unique nouns (the Sun, the Ganga) or overusing 'the' before generic plurals and abstract nouns (the lions are dangerous — wrong; Lions are dangerous — right). Similarly, comma splices joining independent clauses without coordinating conjunction or semicolon are a structural error, not an article issue.
  • Modal misuse: 'must to go', 'should going', 'can will do' are impossible combinations. Modals never pair with 'to' except in 'be able to/have to/ought to'. Also, 'used to' expresses past habit; 'be used to' means accustomed to and takes a gerund; 'get used to' means become accustomed to — confusing these alters meaning completely.
