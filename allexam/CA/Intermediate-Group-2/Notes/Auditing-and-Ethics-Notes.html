Auditing and Ethics — Complete Notes for CA Intermediate Group 2

CAEnglishNotes

1. Fundamentals of Auditing and Overall Objectives

An audit of financial statements is an independent examination of financial information of any entity, whether profit-oriented or not, and of other economic data by a competent person, with a view to expressing an opinion thereon. The term "independent" means freedom from bias, conflict of interest, or any influence that may compromise professional judgment. The person conducting the audit must be competent — possessing the requisite technical training, professional qualification, and experience.

The overall objectives of the auditor are: (a) to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework; and (b) to report on the financial statements and communicate as required by the Standards on Auditing (SAs).

Reasonable assurance is a high level of assurance but is not absolute assurance. It arises from the use of selective testing (audit sampling), the inherent limitations of internal control, and the fact that most audit evidence is persuasive rather than conclusive. Absolute assurance is neither possible nor required under the concept of reasonable assurance.

Materiality is central to the audit. Misstatements, including omissions, are considered material if they could, individually or collectively, influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements. Materiality depends on the size and nature of the misstatement judged in the surrounding circumstances. Both qualitative and quantitative factors play a role — a small misstatement could be material if it affects compliance with regulatory requirements, changes a loss into profit, or hides a change in earnings trend.

Audit risk is the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate audit opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated. It is a function of three components:
- Detection Risk (DR): The risk that the auditor's procedures will fail to detect a material misstatement that exists.
- Control Risk (CR): The risk that a material misstatement that could occur in an assertion will not be prevented or detected and corrected on a timely basis by the entity's internal control.
- Inherent Risk (IR): The susceptibility of an assertion about a class of transaction, account balance, or disclosure to a misstatement that could be material, either individually or when aggregated with other misstatements, before consideration of any related controls.
The relationship is: Audit Risk = IR × CR × DR. The auditor assesses IR and CR to determine appropriate level of detection risk, which in turn determines the nature, timing, and extent of further audit procedures.

2. Standards on Auditing (SAs) — Framework,SA 200 to SA 230

The Standards on Auditing (SAs) are issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. They provide the framework within which an auditor conducts an audit of financial statements and ensures quality of audit performance. SAs are mandatory for all CA members certifying financial statements. When a SA does not apply, the auditor should consider the guidance in related Guidance Notes issued by ICAI.

SA 200 – Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with SAs: This standard deals with the auditor's responsibility to conduct the audit in accordance with SAs, to obtain reasonable assurance, and to report on the financial statements. It requires the auditor to plan and perform an audit with professional skepticism, recognize the possibility that a material misstatement may exist regardless of whether the auditor has prior experience with the entity, and exercise professional judgment. The auditor should also maintain independence in accordance with the Code of Ethics.

SA 210 – Agreeing the Terms of Audit Engagements: This standard mandates that the auditor agree the terms of the audit engagement with management or those charged with governance. The agreed terms should be recorded in an audit engagement letter, which should include: the objective and scope of the audit, responsibilities of the auditor, responsibilities of management, identification of the applicable financial reporting framework, and reference to the expected form and content of reports. If law or regulation requires the audit, this fact should be stated. For recurring audits, the auditor should assess whether circumstances require the engagement letter to be revised and whether there is a need to remind the entity of its existence.

SA 220 – Quality Control for an Audit of Financial Statements: This standard establishes standards and provides guidance on the quality control procedures an auditor should follow for audits of financial statements. Key elements include leadership responsibilities for quality on audits, ethical requirements (especially independence), client acceptance and continuance, assignment of engagement teams, engagement performance (including review), and monitoring. Engagement partners must ensure that appropriate procedures are performed regarding the acceptance of clients and that engagement teams possess the competence and capabilities necessary. Reviews should be performed at appropriate stages — reviewing critical areas of judgment, significant risks, and unusual findings.

SA 230 – Audit Documentation: This standard requires the auditor to prepare audit documentation sufficient to enable an experienced auditor, having no previous connection with the audit, to understand the nature, timing, and extent of audit procedures performed; the results thereof; and audit evidence obtained; and the conclusions reached. Documentation should be assembled in an audit file on a timely basis after the date of the auditor's report (generally within 60 days). The auditor should retain audit documentation for a period of at least five years from the date of the auditor's report (or, if the audit is completed later, from the date of the auditor's report).

Important documentation requirements include: recording the identification, characteristics, and source of information; documenting discussions of significant matters with management, those charged with governance, and others; documenting identified exceptions and how they were resolved; and noting any significant matters that arose during the audit and their resolution.

3. Risk Assessment Standards — SA 315, SA 320, SA 330

SA 315 – Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement Through Understanding the Entity and Its Environment: This standard requires the auditor to identify and assess the risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level and assertion level through understanding the entity and its environment, including the entity's internal control. The auditor should obtain an understanding of: (i) relevant industry, regulatory, and other external factors including the applicable financial reporting framework; (ii) the nature of the entity, including its operations, ownership and governance structures, types of investments, and financing; (iii) the entity's selection and application of accounting policies; (iv) the entity's objectives and strategies and related business risks; (v) the entity's measurement and review of financial performance.

Understanding internal control involves five components: the control environment (tone at the top, integrity and ethical values, board oversight, organizational structure, assignment of authority and responsibility, HR policies); the entity's risk assessment process; the information system, including the related business processes relevant to financial reporting, and communication; control activities (authorizing and directing, safeguarding assets, performance reviews, information processing controls, segregation of duties); and monitoring of controls.

Risk assessment procedures are used to obtain the understanding of the entity and its environment. These include inquiries of management and others within the entity, analytical procedures, and observation and inspection. The auditor should also read other information available such as strategic plans, board meeting minutes, interim financial statements, and industry reports.

SA 320 – Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit: This standard requires the auditor to determine materiality for the financial statements as a whole and, if applicable, for particular classes of transactions, account balances, or disclosures. Materiality is determined based on the auditor's professional judgment considering the common financial information needs of users. Benchmark percentages commonly used include 5% of profit before tax (for profit-oriented entities), 1% of total revenue, or 0.5–1% of total assets. For loss-making entities, alternatives like total revenue or total expenses may be more appropriate.

Performance materiality is set at less than materiality for the financial statements as a whole to reduce the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds materiality. It is typically 50–75% of overall materiality.

SA 330 – The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks: This standard requires the auditor to design and perform further audit procedures whose nature, timing, and extent are based on and responsive to the assessed risks of material misstatement. For assessed risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level, the auditor should determine overall responses such as emphasizing the need for professional skepticism, assigning more experienced staff or using specialists, incorporating additional elements of unpredictability in selecting audit procedures, and making general adjustments to the nature, timing, and extent of procedures.

For assessed risks at the assertion level, the auditor should design and perform further audit procedures that are sufficiently responsive to the assessed risks. This includes tests of controls (when the auditor's risk assessment includes an expectation that controls are operating effectively or when substantive procedures alone cannot provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence) and substantive procedures (tests of details and substantive analytical procedures). The auditor should obtain audit evidence about the operating effectiveness of controls upon which risk assessments depend.

4. Audit Evidence Standards — SA 500 to SA 530

SA 500 – Audit Evidence: This standard requires the auditor to design and perform audit procedures that are appropriate in the circumstances for obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence. Sufficient appropriate audit evidence is the quality and quantity of evidence needed to afford a reasonable basis for the auditor's opinion. Audit evidence is cumulative in nature and is primarily obtained from audit procedures performed during the course of the audit, though it may also include information obtained from other sources such as previous audits, a firm's quality control procedures for client acceptance and continuance, and analytical procedures applied in the overall review stage.

Audit evidence includes information contained in the accounting records supporting the financial statements and other information. The auditor should consider the reliability of information to be used as audit evidence. Generally, audit evidence is more reliable when it is obtained from independent sources outside the entity, when it is generated internally under effective internal controls, when it is documented (written, electronic, or other physical forms), when it is obtained directly by the auditor, and when it is original documents rather than photocopies.

SA 501 – Audit Evidence — Specific Considerations for Selected Items: This standard deals with specific audit evidence requirements for items such as inventories, accounts receivable confirmations, segment information, legality and regulation, going concern assumptions, and subsequent events. For inventories, the auditor should inspect tangible inventories unless impracticable, observe counting procedures, perform test counts, and verify the completeness of inventory listings. For accounts receivable, external confirmation requests are a primary procedure unless ineffective; the auditor should consider alternative procedures such as examining subsequent cash receipts, shipping documents, and customer correspondence.

SA 505 – External Confirmations: This standard requires the auditor to consider whether external confirmation procedures are to be performed as audit evidence. Positive confirmation requests are used when individual account balances, terms of agreements, and other factors indicate a high risk of material misstatement, when a large number of alleged misstatements have been detected, or when reciprocal expectations between the entity and confirming party may cause the confirming party to not properly process confirmation requests. Negative confirmation requests may be used only when the auditor has assessed the risk of material misstatement as low, few recipients are likely to disregard the requests, and the auditor has obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence on the effectiveness of controls. The auditor should maintain control over the confirmation process including design, sending, and following up on non-responses.

SA 520 – Analytical Procedures: This standard requires the auditor to apply analytical procedures near the end of the audit on the basis of the financial statements as a whole to assist in the final review of the financial statements. Analytical procedures should also be applied as risk assessment procedures and, when they constitute substantive procedures, as substantive procedures. An analytical procedure involves evaluation of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data. Such analysis assists the auditor in assessing materiality and identifying fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information or that deviate significantly from predicted amounts.

SA 530 – Audit Sampling: This standard provides guidance on using audit sampling to obtain audit evidence. Audit sampling is the application of audit procedures to less than 100% of items within a population of audit relevance such that all sampling units have a chance of selection. Sampling risk is the risk that the auditor's conclusion may be different from the conclusion if all items were examined. Non-sampling risk arises from reasons other than sampling risk, such as using inappropriate audit procedures or misinterpreting evidence. The auditor should design samples to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence, evaluate the results of sampling, and document the sampling approach.

5. Fraud, Laws & Regulations, Going Concern — SA 240, SA 250, SA 570

SA 240 – The Auditor's Responsibilities Relating to Fraud in an Audit of Financial Statements: This standard distinguishes between fraud and error. Fraud is an intentional act by one or more individuals among management, those charged with governance, employees, or third parties, involving the use of deception to obtain an unjust or illegal advantage. Error is an unintentional misstatement in financial statements, including the omission of an amount or disclosure. The distinction is important because fraud involves intent while error does not.

Fraud involving management typically includes manipulation of financial statements, misappropriation of assets, and corruption. Fraud risk factors include incentives/pressures (such as financial stability threatened by economic conditions, ability to earn significant bonuses), opportunities (such as significant related-party transactions, strong financial pressive pressures), and attitudes/rationalizations. The auditor should maintain professional skepticism throughout the audit, recognizing the possibility that a material misstatement due to fraud may exist regardless of the auditor's past experience with the entity.

The auditor should discuss among the engagement team the susceptibility of the financial statements to material misstatement due to fraud and identify and assess the risks of material misstatement due to fraud. Management override of controls is a unique risk that applies to all entities. It may take the form of fictitious or premature recognition of revenues, misapplication of accounting principles, concealagement of liabilities, or manipulating journal entries. The auditor should respond to the risk of management override by testing journal entries and other adjustments for appropriateness, reviewing accounting estimates for biases, and evaluating the business rationale of significant unusual transactions.

SA 250 – Consideration of Laws and Regulations in an Audit of Financial Statements: This standard distinguishes between the first tier of laws and regulations (those that determine the financial reporting framework) and the second tier (those that provide a general operational framework but do not directly determine financial statement amounts and disclosures). The auditor should obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding compliance with the provisions of first-tier laws and regulations. For second-tier laws and regulations, the auditor should perform specified audit procedures to help identify instances of non-compliance, including: (i) inquiring of management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance as to whether the entity is in compliance with such laws and regulations; and (ii) inspecting correspondence, if any, with the relevant licensing or regulatory authorities.

If the auditor becomes aware of information concerning an instance or potential instance of non-compliance, the auditor should obtain further understanding of the nature of the act and the circumstances in which it has occurred and should discuss the matter with management and those charged with governance. If non-compliance is identified or suspected, the auditor should consider the implications for the audit and the possible effects on the financial statements.

SA 570 – Going Concern: This standard requires the auditor to conclude on the appropriateness of management's use of the going concern assumption in the preparation of the financial statements. The auditor should evaluate whether there are material uncertainties about the entity's ability to continue as a going concern. Management is responsible for assessing the entity's ability to continue as a going concern. The auditor should obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about whether material uncertainty exists.

Events or conditions that may cast significant doubt on going concern include: negative trends (recurring operating losses, working capital deficiency, negative cash flows), debt restructuring, legal proceedings that may result in claims, non-compliance with statutory requirements, and loss of key management without replacement. If the auditor identifies conditions or events that raise substantial doubt about going concern, the auditor should perform additional audit procedures to determine whether material uncertainty exists. The auditor should also evaluate management's plans to mitigate the situation and whether the financial statements adequately disclose the material uncertainty.

If the financial statements have been prepared on a going concern basis and the auditor concludes that a material uncertainty exists, the auditor should express an unmodified opinion with an emphasis of matter paragraph drawing attention to the disclosure, or, if the disclosure is inadequate, modify the opinion. If management is not preparing financial statements on a going concern basis, the auditor should express an adverse opinion.

6. Related Parties, Estimations, Subsequent Events — SA 550, SA 540, SA 560

SA 550 – Related Party Disclosures: This standard requires the auditor to identify, assess, and respond to the risks of material misstatement associated with related party relationships and transactions. Entities are often structured to conduct certain business through related parties for commercial, regulatory, or tax reasons. Identifying such arrangements and understanding their business purpose is a significant challenge. Related parties may use their influence over the entity to obtain benefits or engage in transactions on terms that are not at arm's length.

The auditor should obtain an understanding of the entity's related party relationships and transactions sufficient to recognize fraud-induced material misstatement arising from related parties. This includes: (i) understanding the entity's procedures for identifying, accounting for, and disclosing related party relationships and transactions; (ii) inquiring of management as to the names of related parties and the nature of the relationships; (iii) reading minutes of meetings of those charged with governance and other communications from regulatory bodies; and (iv) inspecting shareholder approval records.

If the auditor identifies significant related party transactions outside the ordinary course of business, the auditor should investigate the business rationale (or lack thereof) and evaluate whether the transaction has been appropriately accounted for and disclosed. The auditor should also evaluate the adequacy of disclosures and the fairness of the transactions.

SA 540 – Auditing Accounting Estimates, Including Fair Value Accounting Estimates, and Related Disclosures: This standard applies to accounting estimates made by management in the preparation of financial statements, including fair value estimates. Accounting estimates involve judgments that affect their recognition, measurement, and disclosure in financial statements. Examples include allowance for doubtful debts, warranty provisions, fair value of financial instruments, and useful lives of depreciable assets.

The auditor should obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about whether accounting estimates are reasonable and related disclosures are adequate. The auditor should evaluate: (i) the method used to measure the estimate; (ii) whether management has appropriately recognized the estimate in the financial statements in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework; and (iii) the assumptions used. The auditor should review events occurring after the date on which the accounting estimate was made through the date of the auditor's report that provide audit evidence relating to the estimate. The auditor should evaluate the outcome of the accounting estimate or, if applicable, reconsider the reasonableness of management's assumptions based on the result of subsequent events.

SA 560 – Subsequent Events: This standard requires the auditor to perform audit procedures designed to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence that all events occurring between the date of the financial statements and the date of the auditor's report that require adjustment of or disclosure in the financial statements have been identified. The two types of subsequent events are: adjusting events (those that provide evidence of conditions that existed at the date of the financial statements) and non-adjusting events (those that are indicative of conditions that arose after the date of the financial statements). Adjusting events require adjustment of the financial statements, while non-adjusting events require disclosure only if material.

The auditor should perform procedures to identify subsequent events up to the date of the auditor's report. After the date of the auditor's report, the auditor has no obligation to perform any audit procedures regarding the financial statements. However, if the auditor becomes aware of a fact that existed at the date of the auditor's report and that, if known at that date, might have caused the auditor to amend the report, the auditor should consider whether the financial statements require revision and discuss the matter with those charged with governance.

7. Auditor's Report — SA 700 to SA 706 and SA 720

SA 700 – Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements: This standard establishes standards and provides guidance on the auditor's responsibility to form an opinion on the financial statements and on the contents of the auditor's report. The standard reporting format includes: (i) Title — indicating independence (e.g., 'Independent Auditor's Report'); (ii) Addressee — as required by the engagement; (iii) Opinion paragraph — containing the auditor's opinion on whether the financial statements present fairly, in all material respects, the financial position, financial performance, and cash flows in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework; (iv) Basis for Opinion paragraph — explaining the basis for the auditor's opinion; (v) Key Audit Matters (KAMs) section (for listed and other public interest entities); (vi) Responsibilities of Management for the Financial Statements; (vii) Auditor's Responsibilities for the Audit of the Financial Statements; (viii) Other Information; (ix) Signature; (x) Date of report; (xi) Place of signing.

The auditor should express a clean/unmodified opinion when the auditor concludes that the financial statements are presented fairly, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework. A modified opinion may be qualified, adverse, or disclaimer of opinion, depending on the nature and pervasiveness of the matter.

SA 701 – Communicating Key Audit Matters in the Independent Auditor's Report: This standard requires the auditor to communicate key audit matters in the auditor's report. Key audit matters are those matters that, in the auditor's professional judgment, were of most significance in the audit of the financial statements of the current period. KAMs are selected from matters communicated with those charged with governance and arise from the areas of higher assessed risk of material misstatement or significant auditor judgment. Examples include: significant assessed risks of material misstatement, significant unusual transactions, accounting estimates with high degree of estimation uncertainty, and significant events or transactions that occurred during the audit.

SA 705 – Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report: This standard deals with when the auditor concludes that a material misstatement exists or inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence exists. A qualified opinion is expressed when the auditor concludes that misstatements are material but not pervasive, or when there is an inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence but the possible effects are material but not pervasive. An adverse opinion is expressed when the auditor concludes that misstatements are both material and pervasive. A disclaimer of opinion is expressed when the auditor concludes that there is an inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence and the possible effects are both material and pervasive.

SA 706 – Emphasis of Matter Paragraphs and Other Matter Paragraphs: An emphasis of matter paragraph is included when the auditor considers it necessary to draw users' attention to a matter presented or disclosed in the financial statements that, in the auditor's judgment, is of such importance that it is fundamental to users' understanding of the financial statements. The paragraph is included under the heading 'Emphasis of Matter' and refers only to information presented or disclosed in the financial statements. An other matter paragraph is included when the auditor considers it necessary to draw users' attention to a matter other than those presented or disclosed in the financial statements that, in the auditor's judgment, is relevant to users' understanding of the audit, the auditor's responsibilities, or the auditor's report.

SA 720 – The Auditor's Responsibilities Relating to Other Information: This standard requires the auditor to read other information (information, other than the financial statements and the auditor's report, that is included in documents containing audited financial statements) and determine whether there is a material inconsistency between the other information and the financial statements or knowledge obtained in the audit. If the auditor identifies a material misstatement of fact or apparent material inconsistency, the auditor should take appropriate action, including communicating with those charged with governance and considering the need to obtain legal advice.

8. Review, Comfort Letters, Group Audits — SA 710, SA 600

SA 710 – Comparative Information — Corresponding Figures and Comparative Financial Statements: This standard addresses the treatment of comparative information in financial statements and the auditor's responsibilities relating thereto. When comparative information consists of corresponding figures, the auditor's opinion refers to the current period's financial statements only, and the auditor should evaluate whether the corresponding figures agree with the amounts and other disclosure presented in the prior period. When comparative information consists of complete financial statements of a prior period, the auditor should express an opinion on the complete set of one or more financial statements of each period presented.

SA 711 – Group Audits — Special Considerations — Group Financial Statements and the Auditor's Report Thereon: This standard provides guidance on the auditor's responsibilities when auditing group financial statements. When the auditor accepts the group audit engagement, the auditor should determine whether sufficient appropriate audit evidence on group financial information relating to components will be obtainable. The group engagement team should obtain an understanding of the component managers and their work. The group auditor should decide whether to perform audit or review procedures on financial information of components, or require component auditors to perform audit procedures on financial information of components.

SA 720(R) – Supplementary Information in Relation to the Financial Statements — Other Information: This standard addresses situations where supplementary information that is not part of the audited financial statements is included in the document containing the audited financial statements. The auditor should evaluate whether the supplementary information is properly presented and whether it is consistent with the audited financial statements.

Comfort Letters: These are letters issued by auditors at the request of underwriters or issuing companies in connection with securities offerings. The content of comfort letters is guided by Guidance Notes on Comfort Letters issued by ICAI. Comfort letters typically include positive assurance on matters covered by the auditor's report and negative assurance on financial information changes. They also include details of procedures performed and findings. The level of assurance provided should be clearly specified.

Reviewer's Reports under SEBI LODR: For listed entities, a half-yearly financial statement review by the statutory auditor is required. SA 910 (Review Engagements) provides guidance on review engagements. The reviewer's report should state that the review was conducted in accordance with SA 910 and should include a conclusion on whether anything has come to the reviewer's attention to cause them to believe that the half-yearly financial statements are not prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.

9. Internal Audit, Internal Control, and CARO 2020

Internal Audit: SA 610 – Using the Work of Internal Audit (Revised) deals with the auditor's responsibility to have a sufficient understanding of internal audit to identify activities that may be systematically examined by the external auditor and to evaluate the adequacy of the scope and functioning of the internal audit function. The external auditor should determine whether the internal audit function is likely to be adequate to assist the external auditor. Factors to consider include: the organizational status of the internal audit function, the application of a systematic and disciplined approach, the competence of the internal audit staff, and the extent to which the internal audit function is free from bias.

If the external auditor plans to use the work of internal audit, the external auditor should assess the objectivity and competence of the internal audit function and determine the nature, timing, and extent of the external auditor's procedures. The external auditor should read and evaluate the internal audit reports and related correspondence and perform audit procedures on a sample of items already examined by the internal audit function or on a representative portion of transactions handled similarly by the entity.

Internal Control: Under Section 134(5)(e) of the Companies Act, 2013, every annual report of the Board must include a statement that the Board has framed proper internal control systems for ensuring the integrity of accounting records and safeguarding assets. Under SA 315, the auditor must obtain an understanding of internal control relevant to the audit. However, the auditor is not required to obtain a full understanding of all aspects of internal control — only those relevant to forming an opinion on the financial statements.

CARO 2020: The Companies (Auditor's Report) Order, 2020 specifies matters that the auditor of every company must include in the auditor's report. CARO 2020 applies to every company (except Nidhi companies, Section 8 companies, and companies whose paid-up share capital and reserves exceed ₹25 crore) registered under the Companies Act, 2013. The auditor must comment on: (i) fixed assets and stock verification; (ii) loans, investments, guarantees, and security given; (iii) corporate guarantee and loan and security given to firms/concerns in which directors are interested; (iv) receipt of funds from foreign entities or by private placement; (v) cost records maintenance; (vi) deposit acceptance; (vii) regularity in deposits; (viii) fresh subscriptions made; (ix) payment of dues to employees; (x) undisputed dues to tax authorities; (xi) fraud reported under Section 143(12); (xii) managerial remuneration; (xiii) company not being a nidhi company — ratios of operational lease/ hired vehicles; (xiv) transactions with related parties under Section 188; (xv) term loans applied for intended purpose; (xvi) loans taken by body corporate against shares; (xvii) provision for taxes; (xviii) non-compliance of provisions of Companies Act; (xix) fraud; (xx) rotation of auditors; (xxi) roation of partner auditors; (xxii) financial ratios and profitability; (xxiii) accumulation of losses; (xxiv) unsecured loans; (xxv) provision under Accounting Standards; (xxvi) residual values of fixed assets; (xxvii) advances from/due from suppliers; (xxviii) non-supply of documents/information to auditor; (xxix) reporting under Section 197(16).

10. Corporate Governance, Ethics, and Professional Obligations

Corporate Governance: Under the Companies Act, 2013, corporate governance provisions are contained in Sections 177–178, Section 395, and Schedule V. Section 177 mandates constitution of Audit Committees for listed companies and certain classes of companies. The Audit Committee must have at least three directors, with a majority being independent directors, and the chairperson must be an independent director. The Committee's terms of reference include: overseeing financial reporting, recommending appointment/removal of auditors, reviewing internal audit, investigating instances of fraud, and reviewing inter-corporate loans and investments.

Section 178 requires every listed company and other prescribed class of companies to constitute a Nomination and Remuneration Committee (NRC). Section 178(3) requires the Board to formulate a policy on directors' appointment and remuneration. Section 179(3)(d) requires the Board to meet at least four times a year with a maximum gap of 120 days between two meetings.

Related Party Transactions under Section 188 require approval of the Board (for certain transactions) and shareholders (for specified classes of transactions). Prior approval of the Board is mandatory for transactions with related parties. Section 188(2) provides that failure to comply with this section renders the contract voidable at the option of the company.

Code of Ethics (effective April 1, 2024): The new Code of Ethics issued by ICAI is structured around five fundamental principles: Integrity, Objectivity, Professional Competence and Due Care, Confidentiality, and Professional Behavior. The conceptual framework requires the auditor to: (i) identify threats to compliance with fundamental principles; (ii) evaluate the significance of threats identified; (iii) apply safeguards to eliminate threats or reduce them to an acceptable level; and (iv) document the matter.

Threats are classified as: self-interest threat, self-review threat, advocacy threat, familiarity threat, and intimidation threat. Safeguards include: (i) safeguards created by the profession, legislation, or regulation; (ii) safeguards in the work environment; (iii) safeguards applied by the firm; and (iv) safeguards applied in relation to the specific engagement.

Key safeguards for independence include: rotating senior audit personnel, having an experienced reviewer, using separate teams for different services, and disclosing independence-related matters to those charged with governance. The Conceptual Framework for Independence (applicable from April 1, 2024) provides a holistic approach to independence requirements, replacing the earlier list-based approach.

11. Practical Application: Audit Procedure Design, Working Papers, and Common Errors

Design of Audit Procedures: Based on assessed risks, the auditor designs audit procedures that are responsive to those risks. For example:
- Revenue: Test cut-off, reconcile sales returns after year-end to revenue recorded, agree shipments to invoices, perform analytical procedures comparing revenue to prior periods and industry data.
- Inventory: Attend physical counting, trace from count sheets to final inventory, test pricing, perform cut-off tests between goods received and accounts payable.
- Accounts Receivable: Send positive confirmations, examine subsequent cash receipts, review credit notes issued after balance sheet date.
- Fixed Assets: Inspect physical assets, verify title deeds, recompute depreciation, examine additions/disposals during the year.
- Provisions and Contingencies: Examine correspondence with legal counsel, review board minutes for pending litigation, assess adequacy of disclosures.

Working Paper Systems: A well-organized audit working paper system should contain cross-references, index numbers, and a logical arrangement. Top-level summaries should tie to schedule totals. Each working paper should show the source of information, the audit procedure performed, the finding, and the conclusion drawn. Reference to supporting documentation should be precise enough to allow independent verification.

Common Mistakes and Areas of Confusion:
- Confusing Detection Risk with Audit Risk: Detection risk is controllable by the auditor; audit risk is the overall risk that includes inherent and control risk which the auditor assesses but does not control.
- Using negative confirmations when positive would be more appropriate: Negative confirmations provide less evidential value and should only be used when risk is low.
- Materiality calculation errors: Forgetting to use performance materiality in determining sample sizes; using inappropriate benchmarks.
- Ignoring qualitative materiality: Focusing solely on quantitative thresholds while overlooking qualitative factors that could make misstatements material.
- Not performing sufficient follow-up on non-responses to confirmation requests: Simply assuming agreement when confirmations are not returned.
- Overlooking subsequent events: Failing to perform appropriate procedures for events occurring between the balance sheet date and the auditor's report date.
- CARO 2020 non-compliance: Failing to comment on all twenty-nine clauses of CARO 2020, or providing vague/boilerplate responses instead of specific observations.

Important facts

  • Overall objectives of auditor: obtain reasonable assurance (high but not absolute) and express an opinion.
  • Audit risk = Inherent Risk × Control Risk × Detection Risk.
  • Materiality is both quantitative and qualitative; performance materiality is set lower than overall materiality.
  • External confirmations: positive confirmations provide more reliable evidence than negative confirmations.
  • SA 240 requires specific response to fraud risk, especially management override of controls.
  • Going concern assessment covers at least 12 months from the reporting date.
  • Key Audit Matters must be disclosed in the auditor's report for listed entities and public interest entities.
  • Modified opinions: Qualified (material but not pervasive), Adverse (material and pervasive), Disclaimer (material and pervasive inability to obtain evidence).
  • CARO 2020 applies to most companies except Nidhi, Section 8, and large companies (paid-up + reserves > ₹25 crore).
  • Five fundamental principles of Code of Ethics: Integrity, Objectivity, Professional Competence and Due Care, Confidentiality, Professional Behavior.
  • Five types of threats: Self-interest, Self-review, Advocacy, Familiarity, Intimidation.
  • Audit Committee (Section 177): minimum 3 directors, majority independent, chairperson must be independent director.
  • SA 500: audit evidence is cumulative; reliability hierarchy — external > internal, documentary > oral, original > copy.
  • Engagement letter terms must be recorded per SA 210; for recurring audits, assess whether circumstances require revision.
  • SA 560: adjusting events affect the financial statements; non-adjusting events require disclosure only.
  • Section 188: related party transactions require Board approval (and shareholder approval for specified classes).
  • Performance materiality is typically 50-75% of overall materiality.
  • Confirmation procedures require auditor to maintain control over design, sending, and follow-up.

Formulas and relationships

Audit Risk = Inherent Risk (IR) × Control Risk (CR) × Detection Risk (DR)
Detection Risk = Audit Risk / (Inherent Risk × Control Risk)
Materiality benchmarks (common practice): 5% of Profit Before Tax; 1% of Total Revenue; 0.5-1% of Total Assets
Performance Materiality ≈ 50-75% of Overall Materiality
Going Concern Period = At least 12 months from the reporting date
Sample Size (attributes sampling) formula: n = (Z² × P × (1-P)) / E² where Z = confidence level, P = expected deviation rate, E = tolerable deviation rate
Audit Evidence Reliability Hierarchy: External independent source > Internal; Original > Copy; Documentary > Oral; Generated under effective IC > Weak IC
