Cost Classification and Fundamental Terminology

Cost accounting begins with systematic classification of costs, which is essential for accurate product costing and decision-making.

Classification by Behaviour:
- Variable Costs: Change in direct proportion to output volume (e.g., raw materials, direct labour). Per unit remains constant; total varies.
- Fixed Costs: Remain unchanged regardless of output volume within relevant range (e.g., rent, insurance, depreciation on plant). Total is constant; per unit decreases with increased production.
- Semi-variable/Mixed Costs: Contain both fixed and variable components (e.g., telephone bill with fixed rental plus per-minute charges). Separation techniques: high-low method, scatter diagram, regression analysis.
- Step-fixed Costs: Remain constant up to a certain level of activity, then jump to a higher level (e.g., supervisor salary increasing when production exceeds capacity).
- Stepped-variable Costs: Change in steps proportional to output but not continuously (rare; usually grouped under step-fixed).

Classification by Traceability:
- Direct Costs: Can be identified with and charged to a specific cost unit/object (materials, labour, expenses).
- Indirect Costs: Cannot be directly identified with a cost unit; allocated/apportioned via overhead distribution (factory rent, power, supervision).

Classification by Normalcy:
- Normal Costs: Incurred under ordinary operating conditions and are inherent in production.
- Abnormal Costs: Arise due to unusual, unexpected, or avoidable circumstances (abnormal idle time, abnormal spoilage, fire loss). Treated as period costs and debited to Costing Profit & Loss Account.

Key Terminology:
- Cost Unit: Quantitative unit of product/service to which costs are ascertained (e.g., per kg, per unit, per batch, per course, per bed-day in hospitals).
- Cost Centre: Location, person, or item of equipment for which costs are ascended (may be control centres).
- Cost Sheet: Statement showing breakdown of total cost into prime cost, factory/works cost, cost of production, and cost of sales with per-unit figures.
- Prime Cost = Direct Materials + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses.
- Works/Factory Cost = Prime Cost + Factory Overheads - Opening WIP + Closing WIP.
- Cost of Production = Works Cost + Administrative Overheads - Opening Finished Goods + Closing Finished Goods.
- Cost of Sales = Cost of Production + Selling & Distribution Overheads.
- Conversion Cost = Direct Labour + Manufacturing Overheads (cost of converting raw material into finished goods).
- Throughput Cost = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Variable Overheads.

Methods and Techniques of Costing

ICAI syllabus distinguishes between methods (where costing is based on nature of product/process) and techniques (tools for cost control and decision-making).

Methods of Costing:
1. Job Costing: Used for work executed to specific orders/customer requirements. Each job is distinct and traced individually. Examples: printing, construction, repair workshops, custom manufacturing.
2. Contract Costing: A specialized form of job costing for large contracts extending over long periods. Key features: site accounts maintained, progress certificates issued, work-in-progress valuation includes only certified work, anticipated losses booked immediately, profit-reserve created for unfinished contracts.
3. Batch Costing: Costs ascertained for a batch of identical products before moving to next stage. Cost per unit = Total batch cost / Number of units in batch. Common in pharmaceuticals, electronics, food processing.
4. Process Costing: Costs accumulated for each process/department over a period. Used where production is continuous and homogeneous. Joint products, by-products, and waste treatment required. Equivalent units concept applied for WIP.
5. Operation Costing: Combination of job and process costing; costs collected for each operation in manufacture. Used in assembly-line production.
6. Service/Operating Costing: Applied where output is service rather than tangible goods. Cost unit may be per km, per patient-day, per seat-km, per tonne-km. Used in hospitals, transport, hotels, power plants, cinemas.
7. Multiple/Composite Costing: Used where final product is assembled from several component parts produced through different processes (automobiles, aircraft, machinery). Total cost = sum of costs of all components and assemblies.

Techniques of Costing:
- Standard Costing: Pre-determined costs compared with actual costs to determine variances for control.
- Budgetary Control: Planning and control through budgets; variances investigated.
- Marginal Costing: Decisions based on contribution (sales minus variable cost); fixed costs treated as period costs.
- Activity-Based Costing (ABC): Overheads allocated based on activities and cost drivers rather than traditional volume-based absorption.
- Target Costing: Cost set according to market price minus desired profit margin; used during product design phase.
- Life Cycle Costing: Costs incurred over entire life of product from R&D to disposal.
- Throughput Accounting: Focuses on maximizing conversion through bottleneck resources; throughput = sales minus totally variable costs.

Material Cost Control

Effective material management minimizes costs and prevents wastage through systematic control measures.

Purchasing Considerations:
- Selective purchasing from reliable suppliers after evaluation of price, quality, delivery, payment terms.
- Use tender/quote systems for bulk purchases.
- Evaluate cash discount vs. cost of borrowing; decide whether to avail discount.
- Consider purchase quantity discounts but weigh against holding costs.

Inventory Levels and Reorder Quantity:
- Minimum Level = Minimum Reorder Level - (Average Usage × Average Lead Time) OR average of minimum and maximum levels.
- Maximum Level = Reorder Level + Ordering Quantity - (Minimum Usage × Minimum Lead Time).
- Reorder Level = Maximum Usage × Maximum Lead Time.
- Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) = √(2 × Annual Demand × Ordering Cost per Order / Carrying Cost per Unit per Annum).
- Carrying/ Holding Cost includes: storage, insurance, depreciation, obsolescence, interest on capital, handling.
- Ordering Cost includes: clerical, telephone, receiving inspection, administrative costs per order.

Stock Valuation Methods:
- FIFO (First-In First-Out): Older items issued first; closing stock valued at recent prices. Preferred when prices rising.
- Weighted Average Price (WAP): Total cost of material available ÷ Total quantity available. Updated after each purchase.
- LIFO (Last-In First-Out): Not permitted under IND AS but may be used for cost records if ICAI allows; rarely tested now.
- Simple Average Price: Arithmetic mean of prices; generally not recommended.
- HPPN (Highest Price in First Out): Rarely used; issues at highest price first.
- Fix Price Method: Materials issued at predetermined price; eliminates frequent revaluation.

Materials Variance Analysis:
- Material Cost Variance (MCV) = (Standard Quantity × Standard Price) - (Actual Quantity × Actual Price).
- Material Price Variance (MPV) = Actual Quantity × (Standard Price - Actual Price).
- Material Usage Variance (MUV) = Standard Price × (Standard Quantity - Actual Quantity).
- Material Mix Variance (MMV) = Standard Price × (Actual Mix Quantity - Revised Standard Quantity).
- Material Yield Variance (MYV) = Standard Cost per Unit × (Actual Yield - Standard Yield from Actual Input).
- Note: MCV = MPV + MUV + MMV + MYV.
- MPV can be computed on quantity purchased or quantity consumed; basis must be stated clearly.

Labour Cost Control

Labour accounting involves tracking wages, incentives, idle time, and overtime while controlling labour costs.

Wages Payment Methods:
- Time Rate: Payment based on time worked; simple but does not motivate productivity.
- Piece Rate: Payment per unit produced; incentivizes output.
- Time Rate with Guarantee: Worker guaranteed minimum time rate earnings even if piece rate yields lower amount.
- Halsey Plan: Wage = Time Rate for hours worked + 50% of time saved × Time Rate.
- Rowan Plan: Wage = Time Rate for hours worked + (Time Saved / Standard Time) × Time Rate for hours worked.
- Barr Plan: 20% bonus on time saved in addition to time rate.
- Emerson's Efficiency Plan: Progressive bonus starts at 10% efficiency; full bonus at 100% efficiency; no bonus below 66⅔%.
- Taylor's Differential Piece Rate: Two rates; higher rate for meeting/exceeding standard, lower rate for falling short.

Direct vs. Indirect Labour:
- Direct Labour: Can be wholly attributed to a cost unit (machine operators, assemblers).
- Indirect Labour: Supports production indirectly (supervisors, maintenance, store keepers); apportioned as factory overheads.

Idle Time and Overtime:
- Normal Idle Time: Expected in normal operations; cost absorbed through overhead absorption rate (increase rate by dividing by percentage of productive time).
- Abnormal Idle Time: Due to strikes, power failure, weather; written off to Costing P&L Account.
- Normal Overtime: Charged to overheads and absorbed through overhead rate; if attributable to specific job, charged directly.
- Abnormal Overtime: Charged directly to job/order; not absorbed as overhead.
- Premium Rate System: Extra amount paid for overtime at premium; usually treated as direct expense if traceable to specific job.

Labour Variance Analysis:
- Labour Cost Variance (LCV) = (Standard Cost for Actual Output) - (Actual Cost).
- Labour Rate Variance (LRV) = Actual Hours × (Standard Rate - Actual Rate).
- Labour Efficiency Variance (LEV) = Standard Rate × (Standard Hours for Actual Output - Actual Hours).
- Labour Mix Variance (LMV) = Standard Rate × (Actual Mix Hours - Revised Standard Hours).
- Labour Yield Variance (LYV) = Standard Cost per Hour × (Actual Yield Hours - Standard Yield Hours from Actual Input).
- LCV = LRV + LEV + LMV + LYV.
- Adverse rate variance may result from using more skilled workers than planned, leading to improved efficiency.

Overhead Analysis and Absorption

Overhead management covers collection, allocation, apportionment, distribution, and absorption with accuracy critical for product costing.

Collection and Classification:
- Overheads collected by department/cost centre.
- Classified as factory, administrative, selling, distribution, research & development, and non-operating.

Steps in Overhead Absorption:
1. Allocation: Direct charging of item to cost centre (e.g., depreciation of machinery charged to machine room).
2. Apportionment: Distributing common costs among cost centres on equitable basis.
- Rates of hire: Machinery, furniture, buildings.
- Floor area: Rent, rates, lighting, heating.
- Number of lights: Lighting, cooling.
- Number of workers: Canteen, hospital, supervision.
- Value of material: Stores handling, quality control.
- Direct Labour/Worker hours: Power, supervision.
- Machine hours: Machine maintenance, depreciation.
- Percentage of direct material cost: Insurance, handling.
- Percentage of direct labour cost: Canteen, hospital.
- Percentage of prime cost/turnover: Administrative overheads.
3. Redistribution (Re-allocation): Transfer of service department costs to production departments using direct, step-down, or reciprocal (simultaneous equation/algebraic) methods.
4. Absorption: Charging overheads to cost units using Overhead Absorption Rate (OAR).

Overhead Absorption Rates (OAR):
- Straight Line Basis: Equal charge per period regardless of output variation. Causes over/under-absorption when volume changes.
- Production Cost Unit Basis: Charge per unit produced.
- Percentage of Direct Material/Labour/Cost: Simple but distorts when input costs fluctuate.
- Direct Labour Hour Rate: Total overheads ÷ Total direct labour hours.
- Machine Hour Rate: Total overheads ÷ Total machine hours. Machine hour rate calculation includes:
a) Fixed Machine Cost (annualized) ÷ Estimated machine hours.
b) Variable Machine Cost per hour (power, consumables).
- Units of Output Method: Total overheads ÷ Total units.
- Gross Profit Ratio: Used when selling prices uniform but costs vary.

Under and Over Absorption:
- Under-absorption = Actual overheads > Absorbed overheads; closing entry credits Costing P&L.
- Over-absorption = Absorbed overheads > Actual overheads; closing entry debits Costing P&L.
- Treatment: Apportion between COGS and closing WIP/finished goods if material; write entirely to Costing P&L if immaterial.

Activity-Based Costing (ABC)

ABC assigns overheads to products based on activities consumed, providing more accurate costing than traditional volume-based absorption, especially in automated/complex environments.

Core Concepts:
- Activity: Any event, action, or process that consumes resources (e.g., setting up machines, inspecting quality, processing purchase orders).
- Cost Pool: Collection of overhead costs associated with a particular activity.
- Cost Driver: Factor that causes change in cost of an activity (e.g., number of setups, number of inspections, machine hours).
- Cost Object: Product, service, customer, or project to which costs are assigned.

Steps in ABC:
1. Identify major activities performed in organization.
2. Assign resource costs to each activity to形成 cost pools.
3. Identify cost drivers for each activity.
4. Calculate cost driver rate = Cost pool / Total cost driver units.
5. Assign activity costs to cost objects using cost driver rate × driver units consumed.

Comparison with Traditional Costing:
- Traditional: Uses single plant-wide rate based on direct labour hours or machine hours; may over-cost high-volume simple products and under-cost low-volume complex products.
- ABC: Multiple cost pools and drivers; reflects actual consumption of resources; reveals true product profitability.

Advantages:
- More accurate product costing and pricing decisions.
- Identifies non-value-added activities for elimination.
- Better overhead control through activity management.
- Useful for customer profitability analysis.

Limitations:
- Complex and costly to implement.
- Requires extensive data collection.
- Subjectivity in identifying activities and drivers.
- May produce borderline decisions due to estimation errors.

ABC vs. Time-Driven ABC (TDABC):
- TDABC simplifies by estimating time required per transaction and capacity cost per time unit; avoids detailed activity surveys.

Standard Costing and Variance Analysis

Standard costing establishes pre-determined costs for materials, labour, and overheads; actual costs are compared against standards to compute variances for control.

Setting Standards:
- Ideal Standards: Perfect conditions, no waste, maximum efficiency; rarely achievable.
- Attainable/Practical Standards: Based on efficient operations with normal allowances; preferred for motivation.
- Basic Standards: Long-term reference standards not updated; used for trend analysis.
- Current Standards: Reflect current conditions; useful for short-term control.

Material Variances (repeated briefly with emphasis):
- Material Cost Variance = SQ×SP - AQ×AP.
- Material Price Variance = AQ×(SP - AP).
- Material Usage Variance = SP×(SQ - AQ).
- Material Mix Variance = SP×(Actual Mix - Revised Standard Mix).
- Material Yield Variance = SC per unit × (Actual Yield - Standard Yield).

Labour Variances:
- Labour Cost Variance = SH×SR - AH×AR.
- Labour Rate Variance = AH×(SR - AR).
- Labour Efficiency Variance = SR×(SH - AH).
- Labour Mix Variance = SR×(Actual Mix - Revised Standard Mix).
- Labour Yield Variance = SC per hour × (Actual Yield - Standard Yield).

Overhead Variances:
- Total Overhead Variance = Standard Overhead for Actual Output - Actual Overhead.
- Variable Overhead Cost Variance = (Standard Hours for Actual Output × Standard Variable OH Rate) - Actual Variable OH.
- Split into Variable OH Spending Variance and Variable OH Efficiency Variance.
- Fixed Overhead Cost Variance = Absorbed Fixed OH - Actual Fixed OH.
- Split into Fixed OH Volume Variance, Expenditure Variance, Capacity Variance, and Efficiency Variance.
- Fixed OH Volume Variance = (Actual Hours - Budgeted Hours) × Standard Fixed OH Rate per Hour.
- Fixed OH Capacity Variance = (Actual Hours - Budgeted Hours) × Standard Fixed OH Rate.
- Fixed OH Efficiency Variance = (Budgeted Hours for Actual Output - Actual Hours) × Standard Fixed OH Rate.
- Fixed OH Expenditure Variance = Budgeted Fixed OH - Actual Fixed OH.
- Reconciliation of Cost and Financial Accounts: Adjust for differences like direct taxes, fines, items accounted only in financial books ( dividends, interest), and over/under-absorption of overheads.

Budgetary Control and Flexible Budgeting

Budgetary control uses budgets to plan, coordinate, and control operations; performance measured by comparing actual results with budgeted figures.

Types of Budgets:
- Planning Budget: Prepared for expected level of activity.
- Flexible Budget: Adjusted for actual level of activity; shows budgeted costs at different output levels.
- Fixed Budget: Prepared for single level of activity; becomes ineffective when actual differs.
- Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB): Every expense justified from zero base; no reference to previous budgets; used for discretionary expenses.
- Rolling/Lcontinuous Budget: Constantly updated by adding new period as current period expires.
- Master Budget: Comprehensive budget covering all functions (sales, production, materials, labour, overheads, cash, capital expenditure, projected financial statements).
- Operating Budget: Covers revenue and expense activities.
- Financial Budget: Covers cash, capital expenditure, balance sheet items.
- Capital Budget: For long-term investment decisions.

Flexible Budget Mechanics:
- Separate costs into fixed, variable, and semi-variable components.
- Prepare flexible budget for actual output level.
- Compare actual costs with flexible budget costs to compute flexible budget variances.
- Formula: Flexible Budget Cost = Fixed Cost + (Variable Cost per Unit × Actual Output).
- Semi-variable cost separation needed before flexibility calculation.

Budget Preparation Steps:
1. Determine objectives and policies.
2. Identify key factor/limiting factor (usually sales demand, labour, material, or machine capacity).
3. Prepare Sales Budget first (starting point for most budgets).
4. Prepare Production Budget based on sales and inventory policy.
5. Prepare budgets for materials, labour, overheads, and cash.
6. Compile into master budget.
- Limiting Factor Analysis: Identify constraint; maximize contribution per unit of limiting factor; formulate optimal production plan.
- Cash Budget: Estimates cash inflows and outflows; prepared in money terms; helps in liquidity management; formats include receipts/payments columnar, summary, and reconciliation methods.

Marginal Costing and Decision-Making Tools

Marginal costing separates costs into fixed and variable components; contribution (sales minus variable cost) is key metric for decision-making.

Key Concepts:
- Marginal Cost = Variable cost per unit.
- Contribution = Sales Price - Marginal Cost.
- P/V Ratio (Profit Volume Ratio) = (Contribution / Sales) × 100.
- Break-even Point (BEP) = Fixed Costs / P/V Ratio = Fixed Costs / Contribution per Unit.
- Margin of Safety = Actual Sales - Break-even Sales.
- Angle of Incidence: Angle between sales line and BEP line in break-even chart; indicates profit potential.

Break-even Chart Types:
- Break-even Diagram: Shows BEP graphically with total cost and total revenue lines.
- Profit Graph: Shows profit at various output levels.
- Multi-product BEP: Requires assumption of constant sales mix; weighted average contribution per unit used.

Decision-Making Applications:
- Make or Buy: Compare cost of making internally with purchase price including any avoidable fixed costs; consider qualitative factors.
- Acceptance of Special Order: Accept if order price exceeds variable cost and spare capacity exists; fixed costs ignored unless incremental.
- Shut Down Decision: Continue if contribution covers some fixed costs; shut down only if contribution negative or alternative use yields higher contribution.
- Key Factor/Limiting Factor Analysis: Calculate contribution per unit of limiting factor; rank products accordingly; maximize total contribution.
- Product Mix Optimization: Linear programming used when multiple constraints exist (machine hours, labour hours, material availability).
- Relevant Costing: Only future costs that differ between alternatives are relevant; sunk costs, committed costs, and unavoidable fixed costs are irrelevant.
- Opportunity Cost: Benefit foregone by choosing one alternative over another; included in relevant cost analysis when capacity constraint exists.
- Target Profit Analysis: Required Sales = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit) / P/V Ratio.

Comprehensive Problem-Solving Framework and Exam Strategy

Success in CA Intermediate Cost and Management Accounting requires systematic approach to numerical problems and clear understanding of theory.

Problem-Solving Sequence:
1. Read question carefully; identify information given and asked.
2. Classify data: fixed vs variable, direct vs indirect, relevant vs irrelevant.
3. Draw diagrams/layouts where helpful (break-even charts, variance analysis layouts, ABC flow diagrams).
4. Apply correct formulas with proper units and signs.
5. Interpret results: favorable/adverse variance, acceptable/rejection decision, impact on profit.
6. Check reasonableness of answer.

Common Pitfalls:
- Confusing pricing and usage variances bases (purchase quantity vs consumption quantity).
- Using wrong material for mixture/yield calculations (should use standard mix proportions).
- Ignoring opportunity costs in decision-making problems.
- Incorrectly separating semi-variable costs in flexible budgeting.
- Applying fixed overhead absorption rates without considering capacity/base changes.
- Misidentifying relevant costs (including sunk costs or excluding avoidable fixed costs).
- Arithmetic errors in EOQ and variance computations.

Exam Preparation Tips:
- Practice numerical problems daily; speed and accuracy are essential.
- Memorize all formulas with understanding of derivation logic.
- Learn distinction between similar concepts (e.g., marginal costing vs absorption costing impact on profit).
- Revise theory regularly; ICAI expects definition-based answers alongside numericals.
- Attempt previous years' questions to understand pattern and difficulty level.
- Time management: allocate time proportionally to marks; do not spend excessive time on single problem.
- Show workings clearly; partial marks awarded for correct methodology even if final answer wrong.
- In theory questions, use headings, sub-headings, and bullet points for clarity.

Important facts

  • EOQ formula minimizes total ordering and carrying costs: √(2DS/H) where D=annual demand, S=ordering cost per order, H=holding cost per unit per year.
  • Contribution = Sales - Variable Cost; P/V Ratio = (Contribution/Sales) × 100.
  • Break-even Point in units = Fixed Costs / Contribution per Unit.
  • Margin of Safety = Actual Sales - Break-even Sales.
  • In standard costing, Material Cost Variance = Material Price Variance + Material Usage Variance (+ Mix and Yield if applicable).
  • Fixed Overhead Volume Variance arises due to difference between budgeted and actual production level.
  • Zero-Based Budgeting requires justification of every expense from zero; no carry-forward from previous budgets.
  • ABC assigns overheads based on activities and cost drivers rather than traditional volume measures.
  • Limiting factor analysis maximizes contribution per unit of constrained resource.
  • Relevant costs are future differential costs; sunk costs and committed costs are irrelevant.
