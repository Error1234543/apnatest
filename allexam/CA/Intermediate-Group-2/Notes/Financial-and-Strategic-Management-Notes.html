Capital Budgeting: Introduction and Decision Rules

Capital budgeting refers to the process of evaluating and selecting long-term investments that are consistent with the firm's goal of maximizing owner wealth. Every major capital decision involves an outflow of funds today in exchange for anticipated future inflows over several years.

Key characteristics of capital budgeting decisions:
• Long-term commitment of funds
• Irreversibility — once made, reversal is costly
• Involve risk and uncertainty about future cash flows
• Affect the firm's competitive position

The hierarchy of capital budgeting techniques:
1. Non-discounted (traditional) methods: Payback Period and Accounting Rate of Return. These ignore the time value of money and are used only as rough screening tools.
2. Discounted (modern) methods: Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Profitability Index (PI), Discounted Payback Period, and Modified IRR (MIRR). These explicitly account for the time value of money and are the primary decision tools.

Decision rules — NPV method:
• Accept the project if NPV > 0 (adds value)
• Reject if NPV < 0 (destroys value)
• For mutually exclusive projects, choose the one with the highest positive NPV

Decision rules — IRR method:
• Accept if IRR > required rate of return (cost of capital)
• Reject if IRR < required rate of return
• For mutually exclusive projects, conflicts with NPV may arise; always prefer NPV in such cases

Decision rules — PI method:
• Accept if PI > 1
• Reject if PI < 1
• PI = PV of future cash inflows / Initial investment

Important caveat: When comparing mutually exclusive projects of different sizes, NPV is the superior criterion because IRR can give misleading rankings when cash flow patterns differ (e.g., one project has larger early cash flows while another has larger later cash flows).

Net Present Value (NPV) — Calculation and Interpretation

NPV is the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a project's life, discounted at the firm's required rate of return (cost of capital).

Formula:
NPV = Σ [CFt / (1 + r)^t] − C0
where CFt = net cash flow at time t, r = discount rate (cost of capital), t = time period, C0 = initial investment.

Step-by-step procedure for calculation:
1. Determine the initial investment (outflow at t = 0)
2. Estimate annual net cash inflows for each year of the project's life
3. Identify the terminal cash flow (salvage value, recovery of working capital)
4. Select the appropriate discount rate (WACC or project-specific hurdle rate)
5. Compute the present value of each cash inflow using PV factors
6. Sum the present values of all inflows and subtract the initial investment
7. Apply the decision rule

Cash flow estimation principles:
• Use incremental cash flows — only those that change as a result of accepting the project
• Include opportunity costs and side effects (erosion/cannibalization)
• Ignore sunk costs — they are irrelevant to the decision
• Consider the impact on working capital requirements
• Account for tax on salvage value: Tax on salvage = (Salvage value − Book value) × Tax rate; if book value > salvage value, there is a tax benefit
• Depreciation tax shield: Annual depreciation × Tax rate increases cash flow

Interpretation nuances:
• A positive NPV means the project earns more than the required rate of return
• NPV measures absolute value creation in monetary terms
• NPV assumes reinvestment of interim cash flows at the cost of capital (a realistic assumption)
• When capital is rationed, NPV per rupee invested (or PI) may be more informative

Internal Rate of Return (IRR) and Profitability Index (PI)

IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV of a project equal to zero. It represents the project's inherent rate of return.

Finding IRR — Interpolation Method:
When cash flows are uneven, IRR is found by trial and error:
1. Compute NPV at a low discount rate (likely positive)
2. Compute NPV at a higher discount rate (likely negative)
3. Interpolate: IRR = r1 + [NPV1 × (r2 − r1)] / (NPV1 − NPV2)
where r1 = lower rate, NPV1 = NPV at r1 (positive), r2 = higher rate, NPV2 = NPV at r2 (negative)

For annuity-type cash flows:
PVIFA(IRR, n) = Initial Investment / Annual Cash Inflow
Locate the PVIFA factor in the annuity table for n periods; the corresponding rate is approximate IRR. Interpolate if the exact factor falls between two rates.

Conflicts between NPV and IRR:
These arise primarily with mutually exclusive projects. The conflict occurs because:
• IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR itself (unrealistically high)
• NPV assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital (realistic)
• Projects may have different scales or different timing of cash flows
• Crossover rate exists where the NPV profiles intersect
Resolution: Always select based on NPV when a conflict arises.

Profitability Index (PI):
PI = Present value of future cash inflows / Initial investment
PI > 1 → Accept; PI < 1 → Reject
For capital rationing, rank projects by PI in descending order and select combinations that fit the budget constraint.

Modified IRR (MIRR):
MIRR addresses the reinvestment rate assumption problem. It assumes cash inflows are reinvested at the firm's cost of capital (not at IRR). Steps:
1. Compound all cash inflows to the end of the project at the reinvestment rate to find TV (terminal value)
2. Discount all cash outflows to time 0 at the finance rate to find PV(outflows)
3. MIRR = (TV / PV(outflows))^(1/n) − 1

Payback Period and Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

Payback Period is the time required for cumulative cash inflows to equal the initial investment. It measures liquidity and risk recovery.

Case 1 — Even (annuity) cash flows:
Payback Period = Initial Investment / Annual Cash Inflow

Case 2 — Uneven cash flows:
Prepare a cumulative cash flow column. The payback period is the year just before the cumulative cash flow turns positive plus the fraction of that year needed to recover the remaining amount:
Payback Period = Last year with negative cumulative CF + |Negative cumulative CF at that year| / Cash inflow of next year

Discounted Payback Period:
Same as payback period but using discounted (present value) cash flows. It is always longer than the regular payback period. While it accounts for the time value of money, it still ignores cash flows beyond the payback cutoff.

Limitations of Payback Period:
• Ignores the time value of money (regular payback)
• Ignores cash flows occurring after the payback period
• Does not measure profitability, only liquidity
• Requires an arbitrary cutoff period set by management
• Biased against long-term projects

Accounting Rate of Return (ARR):
ARR = Average annual accounting profit / Average investment (or Initial investment)
Average investment = (Initial investment + Salvage value) / 2
Note: Accounting profit = Net income after depreciation and tax, not cash flow.

Limitations of ARR:
• Uses accounting profit instead of cash flows
• Ignores the time value of money
• No objective criterion for accepting or rejecting a project
• Can give different results depending on whether average or initial investment is used — both methods are accepted, but the exam will specify which to use

When both ARR and Payback are used together with a discounted method, they serve as supplementary screening criteria rather than final decision tools.

Cost of Capital — Concepts, Components and WACC

Cost of capital is the minimum rate of return that a firm must earn on its investments to maintain the market value of its shares. It is also the opportunity cost of employing funds in a particular project rather than in alternative investments of equivalent risk.

Types of cost of capital:
• Specific/Individual cost: The cost of a particular source of finance (debt, equity, etc.)
• Weighted average cost of capital (WACC): The overall cost reflecting the proportionate weights of each source
• Marginal cost of capital: The cost of raising one additional rupee of capital
• Opportunity cost of capital: The return foregone by investing in a project rather than the best alternative

Cost of Debt (Kd):
After-tax cost of debt = i × (1 − t)
where i = coupon rate or yield to maturity, t = tax rate
If flotation costs exist: Kd = [I × (1 − t)] / [NP × (1 − f)] where I = annual interest, NP = net proceeds, f = flotation cost %
For redeemable debt, use IRR approach: equate present value of interest + principal repayment to net proceeds.

Cost of Equity (Ke):
Using Dividend Discount Model (Gordon's Model):
Ke = (D1 / P0) + g
where D1 = expected dividend per share next year, P0 = current market price, g = constant growth rate in dividends
If new shares are issued with flotation cost f: Ke = (D1 / [P0 × (1 − f)]) + g

Using CAPM:
Ke = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf)
where Rf = risk-free rate, β = beta coefficient, Rm = market return, (Rm − Rf) = market risk premium

Cost of Preference Shares (Kp):
Kp = Dp / P0 (perpetual)
Kp = Dp / [P0 × (1 − f)] (new issue with flotation cost)
where Dp = annual preference dividend

Retained Earnings (Kr):
Same as cost of equity but without flotation cost: Kr = (D1 / P0) + g

Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC):
WACC = wd × Kd × (1 − t) + we × Ke + wp × Kp
where wd, we, wp are the weights of debt, equity and preference capital respectively in the target capital structure.
The weights should reflect market value proportions, not book values, for decision-making purposes.

Capital Structure Theories and Leverage

Capital structure refers to the mix of long-term sources of finance — equity shares, preference shares, debentures, and long-term debt — used by a firm.

Traditional View (Trade-off Theory):
There exists an optimal capital structure that minimizes WACC and maximizes firm value. As debt increases, WACC initially falls (cheaper debt, tax shield), reaches a minimum, and then rises as financial risk increases and the cost of equity climbs sharply.

Modigliani-Miller (MM) Hypothesis — Without Taxes:
• Proposition I: Value of the firm is independent of its capital structure. VL = Vu. The total value depends only on operating income, not on how it is financed.
• Proposition II: Cost of equity rises linearly with leverage: Ke = Ka + (Ka − Kd) × (D/E)
where Ka = cost of capital for all-financially-equity firm, D/E = debt-equity ratio
Implication: The increase in cost of equity exactly offsets the benefit of cheaper debt, leaving WACC unchanged.

MM Hypothesis — With Taxes:
• Proposition I: Value of levered firm = Value of unlevered firm + Tax shield on debt. VL = Vu + T × D
where T = corporate tax rate, D = debt
• Proposition II: Ke = Ka + (Ka − Kd) × (1 − T) × (D/E)
The tax deductibility of interest makes debt cheaper, so WACC decreases with leverage and firm value increases.
• Implication: Optimal capital structure is 100% debt (theoretical extreme; not practical due to bankruptcy costs).

Real-world refinement: Pecking Order Theory suggests firms prefer internal financing first, then debt, and equity last — contrary to MM's suggestion of maximum debt.

LeVERAGE:
Operating Leverage (DOL) = Contribution / EBIT = % change in EBIT / % change in sales
Measures the effect of fixed operating costs on profitability. High operating leverage magnifies the impact of sales changes on EBIT.
Financial Leverage (DFL) = EBIT / EBT = % change in EPS / % change in EBIT
Measures the effect of fixed financial charges (interest, preference dividend) on earnings per share.
Combined Leverage (DCL) = DOL × DFL = Contribution / EBT
Measures the total risk exposure — sensitivity of EPS to changes in sales.
Higher leverage increases both potential returns and risk of financial distress.

Dividend Decisions and Theories

A dividend decision determines what portion of earnings is distributed to shareholders versus retained for reinvestment. The key trade-off is between current dividends and future capital appreciation.

Relevant theories:

Walter's Model:
P = [D + (r × (E − D))] / k
where P = market price per share, D = dividend per share, E = earnings per share, r = rate of return on investment, k = cost of equity
• If r > k (growth firm): Retention is optimal; dividend payout should be zero; price rises with retention
• If r < k (mature firm): Full payout is optimal; price falls with retention
• If r = k: Dividend policy is irrelevant; price is unaffected by payout ratio
Assumptions: No external financing, constant r and k, constant life of firm, all earnings either distributed or reinvested immediately.

Modigliani-Miller (Irrelevance) Model:
Under perfect market assumptions (no taxes, no transaction costs, symmetric information, rational investors), dividend policy does not affect firm value or share price. P0 = [(D1 + S1) / (1 + k)] where S1 is the price at end of period adjusted for new issue/reinvestment.
Investors are indifferent between dividends and capital gains — they can create homemade dividends by selling shares.

Gordon's Model:
Aligns with Walter's but uses the present value approach:
P = D1 / (k − g) where g = r × b, b = retention ratio
Same classification: growth, mature, and normal firms based on r vs k relationship.

Residual Dividend Model:
Dividends are paid only from residual earnings after financing all acceptable investment opportunities. This ensures the firm maintains its target capital structure while minimizing the need for external financing. Ideal for firms with stable investment opportunities.

Factors influencing dividend policy:
• Liquidity position and cash flow stability
• Access to capital markets and borrowing capacity
• Growth opportunities and investment requirements
• Shareholder expectations and signal effect
• Legal constraints (Companies Act provisions on dividend distribution)
• Contractual restrictions (debt covenants)

Working Capital Management

Working capital management involves managing the firm's short-term assets and liabilities to ensure sufficient liquidity while maximizing profitability.

Concept of Working Capital:
• Gross Working Capital = Current Assets (CA)
• Net Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities (CL)
A positive NWC indicates the firm can meet short-term obligations; negative NWC signals potential liquidity stress.

Working Capital Cycle (Cash Conversion Cycle):
CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO
where DIO = Days Inventory Outstanding (inventory holding period), DSO = Days Sales Outstanding (debtor collection period), DPO = Days Payables Outstanding (creditor payment period)
A shorter CCC means faster conversion of resources into cash and lower working capital requirement.

Estimation of Working Capital Requirements:
Typical approach (used in CA exams):
1. Estimate annual operating costs (raw material, labour, overheads, admin, selling & distribution)
2. Determine the average collection period for debtors and average payment period for creditors
3. Calculate the working capital needed for each component: Raw material stock = (Material cost / 365) × Days of raw material inventory; Work-in-progress = (Conversion cost / 365) × Days of WIP; Finished goods = (Total cost / 365) × Days of FG inventory; Debtors = (Credit sales / 365) × Collection period; Creditors = (Credit purchases / 365) × Payment period; Receivables from subcontractors, advance tax, etc.
4. Gross working capital = sum of all current asset components
5. Net working capital = Gross WC − Current liabilities (excluding bank loans/overdrafts that are part of financing)

Management of individual components:
• Inventory: EOQ = √[2 × D × O / C] where D = annual demand, O = ordering cost per order, C = carrying cost per unit per annum. Also consider safety stock, JIT, and ABC analysis.
• Receivables: Evaluate credit policy using the cost-benefit approach — marginal benefit of increased sales vs. marginal cost of carrying receivables (interest cost + bad debts + administration). Factor rediscounting and factoring as sources of short-term finance.
• Payables: Trade credit is a natural source of finance. Evaluate whether early payment discounts are beneficial: implicit cost of not availing discount = [Discount % / (100 − Discount %)] × [365 / (Full period − Discount period)].
• Cash management: Use the Miller-Orr model or cash budgeting. Maintain optimum cash balance balancing transaction motive, precautionary motive, and speculative motive.

Strategic Management — Framework and Tools

Strategic management is the systematic formulation, implementation, and evaluation of cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its long-term objectives. It involves setting a vision, analysing the environment, choosing strategies, executing them, and measuring outcomes.

The Strategic Management Process:
1. Environmental Scanning: External (opportunities and threats via PESTLE analysis — Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental factors) and Internal (strengths and weaknesses via resource-based view, value chain analysis)
2. Strategy Formulation: Mission and vision statement, setting objectives, generating strategic alternatives at corporate level (growth, stability, retrenchment, combination), business level (cost leadership, differentiation, focus — Porter's generic strategies), and functional level
3. Strategy Implementation: Structuring organization, allocating resources, managing change, aligning culture, performance measurement through balanced scorecard
4. Strategy Evaluation and Control: Comparing actual performance with targets, identifying deviations, taking corrective action

Porter's Five Forces Model (industry analysis):
1. Threat of new entrants — determined by barriers to entry (capital requirement, economies of scale, brand loyalty, regulatory hurdles)
2. Bargaining power of suppliers — high when few suppliers, unique inputs, high switching costs
3. Bargaining power of buyers — high when few buyers, standardized products, low switching costs
4. Threat of substitutes — products/services that fulfil the same need from a different angle
5. Rivalry among existing competitors — intensity depends on number of competitors, industry growth rate, product differentiation, fixed costs
Strong five forces = attractive industry; weak five forces = unattractive industry.

SWOT Analysis:
Strengths and Weaknesses are internal factors; Opportunities and Threats are external. SWOT is used to match internal capabilities with external conditions to derive viable strategies (e.g., SO strategies leverage strengths to exploit opportunities; WT strategies minimize weaknesses and avoid threats).

BCG Matrix (Portfolio Analysis):
Categorises business units into four quadrants based on market growth rate and relative market share:
• Stars: High growth, high share — require heavy investment to maintain position
• Cash Cows: Low growth, high share — generate surplus cash, minimal investment needed
• Question Marks: High growth, low share — need significant investment to become stars, or divest
• Dogs: Low growth, low share — candidates for divestiture or harvest
This tool guides resource allocation across multiple business units.

Important facts

  • NPV is the most reliable capital budgeting criterion; it measures absolute value creation and assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital
  • IRR is the discount rate at which NPV equals zero; conflicts with NPV in mutually exclusive projects should be resolved in favour of NPV
  • After-tax cost of debt = Pre-tax cost × (1 − tax rate) because interest is tax-deductible
  • MM with taxes implies firm value increases with leverage due to the interest tax shield; WACC decreases as debt proportion rises
  • MM without taxes states capital structure is irrelevant to firm value under perfect market assumptions
  • Walter's model classifies firms into growth (r > k), mature (r < k), and normal (r = k) based on the relationship between return on investment and cost of equity
  • Gordon's model expresses share price as P = D1 / (k − g), where g = r × b (retention ratio)
  • DOL = Contribution/EBIT, DFL = EBIT/EBT, DCL = DOL × DFL = Contribution/EBT
  • Cash Conversion Cycle = DIO + DSO − DPO; a negative CCC indicates the firm is financing operations with suppliers' money
  • EOQ = √(2DO/C) minimizes total inventory cost by balancing ordering cost and carrying cost
  • Porter's Five Forces determine industry attractiveness: threat of entrants, supplier power, buyer power, substitute threat, and competitive rivalry
  • In SWOT, strengths and weaknesses are internal; opportunities and threats are external
  • BCG matrix uses market growth rate (vertical) and relative market share (horizontal) to classify business units
  • Sunk costs are irrelevant to capital budgeting decisions; only incremental and opportunity costs matter
  • Retained earnings have an opportunity cost equal to the cost of equity, not a zero cost

Common mistakes

  • Using accounting profit instead of cash flows in capital budgeting — NPV and IRR must be based on incremental cash flows, not accounting income
  • Including sunk costs in the analysis — past expenditures that cannot be recovered are irrelevant to the decision
  • Ignoring the tax shield on debt when calculating after-tax cost of debt — the formula is Kd×(1−t), not just Kd
  • Confusing cost of retained earnings with cost of new equity — retained earnings have no flotation cost, while new equity does
  • Selecting a project with higher IRR over one with higher NPV in mutually exclusive projects — always follow NPV
  • Using book value weights instead of market value weights for WACC calculation in decision-making
  • Applying Walter's or Gordon's model without checking whether r > k, r < k, or r = k — the conclusion reverses depending on this condition
  • Double-counting inflation — if cash flows are estimated in nominal terms, the discount rate must also be nominal; do not mix real cash flows with nominal rates
  • Forgetting to include the recovery of working capital at the end of a project's life in terminal cash flow
  • Calculating DOL, DFL, or DCL using accounting figures instead of contribution and PBT — DOL uses contribution (sales minus variable cost), not gross profit
  • In working capital estimation, deducting depreciation from operating costs before computing working capital needs — depreciation is a non-cash expense and should not reduce the working capital requirement
  • Miscalculating the cash conversion cycle by adding DPO instead of subtracting it — payables delay cash outflow, so DPO reduces the cycle
