Financial and Strategic Management — Quick Revision | CA Intermediate Group 2

CAEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Working Capital Management: The management of a firm's short-term assets and liabilities to ensure sufficient liquidity for day-to-day operations while optimizing return on those assets.
  • Capital Structure: The mix of long-term permanent funds comprising debt (secured loans, debentures), preference capital, and equity/shareholders' funds employed in a business.
  • Cost of Capital: The minimum rate of return that a firm must earn on its investments financed by a particular source of capital; it represents the opportunity cost of funds.
  • Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC): The composite cost of capital calculated as the weighted average of the individual component costs of each source of long-term finance.
  • Net Present Value (NPV): The difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows over a project's life; projects with NPV > 0 are acceptable.
  • Internal Rate of Return (IRR): The discount rate at which the NPV of a project equals zero; it is the project's breakeven cost of capital.
  • Payback Period: The number of years required for cumulative cash inflows to equal the initial investment; it ignores the time value of money unless discounted.
  • Profitability Index (PI): The ratio of present value of future cash inflows to the initial investment; PI > 1 indicates acceptance.
  • Leverage: The use of fixed-cost sources of finance (debt or preference capital) to magnify returns to equity shareholders. Three types exist: Operating Leverage, Financial Leverage, and Combined Leverage.
  • Dividend Policy: The framework guiding decisions on the proportion of earnings distributed to shareholders as dividends versus retained for reinvestment.
  • Strategic Management: The science and art of formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its long-term objectives.
  • SWOT Analysis: A strategic planning tool identifying internal Strengths and Weaknesses alongside external Opportunities and Threats facing an organization.
  • PESTEL Analysis: A framework scanning the macro-environment across Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal factors.
  • Value-Based Management: An approach to strategic management that focuses on maximizing shareholder value, using metrics such as Economic Value Added (EVA) and Market Value Added (MVA).
  • Economic Value Added (EVA): A measure of residual wealth computed as Net Operating Profit After Tax (NOPAT) less a charge for the cost of capital employed.
  • Exchange Rate Determination (PPP Theory): Purchasing Power Parity posits that exchange rates between currencies adjust to offset differences in price levels between countries.
  • Interest Rate Parity (IRP): A no-arbitrage condition stating that the difference in interest rates between two countries equals the expected change in exchange rates between their currencies.
  • Futures Contract: A standardized agreement traded on an exchange to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price on a specified future date.
  • Options Contract: A derivative giving the buyer the right (but not the obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put) an underlying asset at a specified strike price before or on expiry.
  • Portfolio Theory (Markowitz): An investment framework asserting that risk-averse investors can construct efficient portfolios that maximize expected return for a given level of risk through diversification.

Key concepts

  • Formula — Cost of Debt (Kd) after tax: Kd = i × (1 − t), where i = coupon/interest rate and t = corporate tax rate. For redeemable debt with flotation cost f: approximate Kd ≈ [I(1−t) + (Re − Ne)/n] / [(Re + Ne)/2], where Re = redemption value, Ne = net proceeds, n = years to redemption.
  • Formula — Cost of Preference Share (Kp): Kp = Dp / Np, where Dp = annual preference dividend and Np = net proceeds. For redeemable preference: Kp ≈ [Dp + (Re − Np)/n] / [(Re + Np)/2].
  • Formula — Cost of Equity (Ke) — Dividend Approach: Ke = (D1 / Np) + g, where D1 = expected dividend per share next period, Np = net proceeds per share, g = constant growth rate.
  • Formula — Cost of Equity (Ke) — CAPM: Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf), where Rf = risk-free rate, β = systematic risk coefficient, Rm = expected market return, (Rm − Rf) = market risk premium.
  • Formula — WACC: WACC = Wd × Kd(1−t) + Wp × Kp + We × Ke, where Wd, Wp, We are the target/proportional weights of debt, preference, and equity respectively.
  • Formula — Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL): DOL = Contribution / EBIT = [Q(P−V)] / [Q(P−V) − F], where Q = quantity, P = price, V = variable cost per unit, F = fixed cost.
  • Formula — Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL): DFL = EBIT / (EBIT − Interest) = EBIT / PAT before tax. Measures sensitivity of EPS to changes in EBIT.
  • Formula — Degree of Combined Leverage (DCL): DCL = DOL × DFL = Contribution / (EBIT − Interest). Measures total business and financial risk.
  • Formula — Operating Leverage & Break-even: BEP (units) = Fixed Cost / Contribution per Unit. Margin of Safety = Actual Sales − Break-even Sales. MOS% = MOS / Actual Sales × 100.
  • Concept — Trade-off Theory of Capital Structure: Firms balance the tax shield benefit of debt against the increasing costs of financial distress and bankruptcy as leverage rises, targeting an optimal capital mix.
  • Concept — MM Hypothesis (No Tax): Under perfect markets with no taxes, capital structure is irrelevant to firm value; V_L = V_U regardless of debt-equity mix.
  • Concept — MM Hypothesis (With Tax): With corporate tax, debt tax shields increase firm value; V_L = V_U + T × D, implying 100% debt is optimal (theoretically).
  • Concept — Pecking Order Theory: Firms prefer internal financing first, then debt, and only issue equity as a last resort due to information asymmetry and adverse selection costs.
  • Concept — Net Income vs Net Operating Income Approaches: NI approach assumes Kd < Ke and that overall cost of capital falls with debt, raising value. NOI approach assumes constant K0 and that value is independent of capital structure.
  • Concept — EOQ Model: Optimal order quantity = √[(2 × D × S) / H], where D = annual demand, S = ordering/setup cost per order, H = holding cost per unit per year. Minimizes total inventory cost.
  • Concept — Cash Budgeting: A projection of cash receipts and payments over a period, used to forecast surpluses/deficits and plan short-term borrowing or investment.
  • Concept — Receivables Management: Involves credit policy setting (standards, terms), aging analysis, collection efficiency, and quantitative models for credit limits based on marginal profit vs bad debt cost.
  • Concept — Inventory Management (ABC, FSN,VED): ABC classifies by annual consumption value (A = high value, B = medium, C = low); FSN uses Fast/Slow/Non-moving movement; VED uses Vital/Essential/Dessential for criticality.
  • Concept — Capital Budgeting Steps: (1) Identification of viable proposals, (2) Development of cash flow estimates, (3) Evaluation using NPV/IRR/PI/Payback, (4) Selection and prioritization, (5) Implementation, (6) Post-audit review.
  • Concept — Mutually Exclusive vs Independent Projects: Mutually exclusive projects compete for the same resource and accepting one excludes others; independent projects do not conflict and all acceptable ones may be accepted if capital is available.
  • Concept — Capital Rationing: When available capital is limited, projects are ranked by PI and selected in descending order until the budget is exhausted (hard rationing), or allocation is optimized via linear programming (soft rationing).
  • Concept — Dividend Relevance (Walter's Model): P = (D × (a−r)/(k−r)) + (E/(k−r)), where a = internal rate of return, r = cost of equity, k = growth rate. If a > k (growth), payout should be zero; if a < k, payout should be 100%.
  • Concept — Dividend Irrelevance (MM Hypothesis): In perfect markets without taxes, transaction costs, or information asymmetry, dividend policy does not affect share price or firm value; only investment policy matters.
  • Concept — Strategic Groups Mapping: Within an industry, firms cluster into strategic groups sharing similar strategies (product range, price, distribution). Analysis identifies mobility barriers between groups and potential strategic moves.
  • Concept — Porter's Five Forces: Competitive rivalry, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, bargaining power of suppliers. Determines industry attractiveness and long-run profitability.
  • Concept — VRIO Framework: Evaluates resources as Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Organized to capture value. Only resources satisfying all four criteria yield sustained competitive advantage.
  • Concept — Core Competence: A harmonized combination of multiple resource and skill sets distributed across the enterprise that is fundamental to a company's competitiveness and differentiation.
  • Concept — Balanced Scorecard: A strategic performance management tool measuring across four perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Business Processes, and Learning & Growth.
  • Concept — Hedge Accounting: An accounting treatment under which gains/losses on qualifying hedging instruments offset gains/losses on hedged items, reducing P&L volatility.
  • Concept — Forward vs Futures vs Options: Forward is customized/OTC with counterparty risk; Futures is standardized/exchange-traded with daily mark-to-market margin; Options give right without obligation, requiring premium payment.
  • Concept — Systematic vs Unsystematic Risk: Systematic (market) risk affects all assets and cannot be diversified away; unsystematic (firm-specific) risk can be eliminated through portfolio diversification. Only systematic risk commands a risk premium.
  • Concept — Beta Interpretation: β = 1 means asset moves with market; β > 1 is more volatile than market (aggressive); β < 1 is less volatile (defensive); β = 0 implies no correlation with market movements.
  • Concept — PPP and IRP Arbitrage: If PPP holds, nominal interest differential equals expected depreciation; if IRP holds, covered interest differential eliminates arbitrage. Deviations signal arbitrage opportunities.

Important facts

  • ICAI Study Material for Financial and Strategic Management covers: Working Capital Management, Capital Structure & Cost of Capital, Dividend Decision, Capital Budgeting, Long-term Financing, Risk Management in Financial Management, International Financial Management, and Strategic Management.
  • Key Formula Sheet: DOL = Contribution/EBIT; DFL = EBIT/(EBIT−Interest); DCL = Contribution/(EBIT−Interest); EOQ = √(2DS/H); BEP(units) = FC/CPU; MOS = Actual Sales − BEP Sales.
  • Cost of Capital ordering rule: In WACC computation, use market-value weights (not book-value weights) for a theoretically accurate result. After-tax cost of debt is always lower than pre-tax cost due to tax deductibility of interest.
  • Operating Leverage is created by fixed operating costs; Financial Leverage is created by fixed financial charges (interest, preference dividend). Both amplify EPS volatility relative to EBIT changes.
  • Working Capital Cycle (Cash Conversion Cycle) = DIO + DSO − DPO, where DIO = Days Inventory Outstanding, DSO = Days Sales Outstanding, DPO = Days Payable Outstanding. A shorter cycle improves liquidity.
  • Cash Budget Components: Opening balance + Cash Receipts (sales collections, other income) − Cash Disbursements (payments for materials, wages, overheads, tax, dividends) = Closing balance/surplus or deficit.
  • Credit Policy Variables: Credit standards (who gets credit), Credit period (how long), Cash discount (incentive for early payment), Collection policy (how aggressively to pursue). Tightening standards reduces sales but lowers bad debts.
  • Inventory Costs: Ordering cost (setup, administrative), Carrying/holding cost (storage, insurance, opportunity cost of capital tied up), Shortage cost (stockout losses, rush orders). EOQ balances ordering vs carrying cost.
  • Capital Budgeting Discount Factors: PV of annuity factor = [1 − (1+r)^−n]/r. Use when cash flows are equal every period; use PV of single sum [1/(1+r)^t] for uneven cash flows.
  • NPV Rule Hierarchy: Accept if NPV > 0; reject if NPV < 0; indifferent if NPV = 0. For mutually exclusive projects, choose the one with the highest positive NPV (not necessarily highest IRR).
  • Modified IRR (MIRR) Assumption: Reinvests positive cash flows at the firm's cost of capital rather than at IRR itself, eliminating the multiple-IRR problem for non-conventional cash flows.
  • Dividend Indifference Point (Walter): Where a = k, dividend policy is irrelevant because internal return equals cost of equity; growth rate g does not appear in Walter's model explicitly (embedded in a).
  • Miller-Orr Model: Used when cash flows are uncertain. Sets upper limit (U), lower limit (L), and optimal return point (Z). Z = (3 × σ² × F / (4 × C))^(1/3) + L, where σ² = variance of cash flow, F = fixed transaction cost, C = opportunity cost per unit.
  • Strategic Management Process Flow: Environmental Scanning → Strategy Formulation → Strategy Implementation → Strategy Evaluation & Control. It is iterative and continuous.
  • Ansoff Matrix: Four growth strategies — Market Penetration (existing/product), Market Development (new market), Product Development (new product), Diversification (both new). Risk increases from penetration to diversification.
  • BCG Matrix Categories: Stars (high growth, high share) require investment; Cash Cows (low growth, high share) generate cash; Question Marks (high growth, low share) need selective investment; Dogs (low/low) may be divested.
  • GE-McKinsey Matrix: Uses Industry Attractiveness vs Business Strength on a 3×3 grid to prioritize portfolio allocation across six cells (invest/grow, selectivity/earnings, harvest/milk).
  • TOWS Matrix: Extends SWOT by matching external Opportunities and Threats with internal Strengths and Weaknesses to generate SO, WO, ST, WT strategies (not just listing factors).
  • Porter's Generic Strategies: Cost Leadership (lowest cost producer), Differentiation (unique product/service), Focus (cost focus or differentiation focus on a narrow segment). Attempting all three simultaneously leads to 'stuck in the middle.'
  • VRIO Criteria Summary: Value (exploits opportunity/neutralizes threat), Rarity (few competitors possess), Imitability (costly/difficult to copy), Organization (firm has structure to exploit). Each missing criterion weakens competitive position.
  • Risk Management Framework (COSO): Internal Environment → Objective Setting → Event Identification → Risk Assessment → Risk Response → Control Activities → Information & Communication → Monitoring.
  • Foreign Exchange Risk Types: Transaction risk (exposure from settled foreign currency transactions), Translation/Accounting risk (consolidation of foreign subsidiaries), Economic/Operating risk (long-term competitive impact of FX movements).
  • Hedge Ratio: Proportion of exposure hedged = Exposure Amount / Notional Amount of Derivative. A hedge ratio of 1.0 is a full hedge; less than 1.0 is a partial hedge.
  • Call Option Payoff = Max(S − X, 0); Put Option Payoff = Max(X − S, 0), where S = spot price at expiry, X = strike price. Maximum loss for buyer is limited to premium paid.
  • Effective Frontiers & Efficient Portfolios: The set of portfolios offering maximum expected return for each level of risk forms the efficient frontier. Rational investors choose portfolios on or above this frontier.
  • Systematic Risk Measurement: Beta (β) measures systematic risk relative to the market portfolio. Portfolio beta = Σ(wi × βi), where wi is the weight of asset i in the portfolio.
  • EVA Calculation Steps: (1) Calculate NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax rate). (2) Determine Total Capital Employed. (3) Calculate Capital Charge = Total Capital × WACC. (4) EVA = NOPAT − Capital Charge.

Memory aids

  • DCL mnemonic: 'Deeply Careful Companies Determine Combined Leverage' → DCL = DOL × DFL, where DOL = operating (fixed COGS), DFL = financial (fixed interest/preference dividend).
  • Working Capital Cycle shortcut: 'Dog Sleeps On Poor ground' = DIO + DSO − DPO. Remember: you hold inventory (Dog days), collect from customers (Sleeps late/Onto), but pay suppliers quickly (Poor credit — you get credit too).
  • PPM mnemonic for PESTEL: 'Please Eat Smooth Tacos Every Lunch' → Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal.
  • BCG Matrix positions: Star = bright (needs feeding/funding), Cash Cow = milking (low growth, generates cash), Question Mark = puzzled (needs decision — invest or divest?), Dog = stray (harvest/divest).
  • Ansoff risk ladder: Market Penetration (lowest risk) → Market Development → Product Development → Diversification (highest risk).
  • MM No-Tax irrelevance: 'Debt doesn't matter when there's no tax and no bankruptcy cost' — think Modigliani-Miller as the purest theoretical benchmark, later adjusted by real-world frictions.
  • WACC components rhyme: 'We All Create Wealth' → Wd×Kd + Wp×Kp + We×Ke. Remember: after-tax debt appears first because it's the cheapest conventional source.
  • PESTEL expansion mnemonic: 'Paul's Elegant Social Treaty Ensures Legal compliance' — each word maps to one factor for easy recall during case study analysis.
  • Portfolio diversification principle: 'Don't put all your eggs in one beta' — unsystematic risk disappears with ~20-30 stocks; systematic risk (beta) remains and is what matters for pricing.
  • Hedge types summary table for revision: Transaction → cash flow lock; Translation → balance sheet revaluation lock; Economic → strategic positioning lock. Each demands a different management approach.
  • Five Forces forcefulness mnemonic: 'Really Strong Suppliers Buy Substitute' → Rivalry, Suppliers, Buyers, Substitutes, New Entrants (read left to right for ease).
  • Dividend theories contrast: 'Walter wants payout to matter; Miller and Modigliani say it doesn't' — Walter = relevance (a vs k comparison drives policy); MM = irrelevance (perfect market assumption).
  • EOQ formula derivation memory: Think 'square root of twice demand times setup over holding' — the numerator balances ordering frequency, denominator penalizes carrying too much.
  • Strategy implementation checklist: 'People, Process, Performance, Politics' — align people/culture, define processes, set KPIs/measurement, navigate organizational politics and change resistance.
  • Risk response strategies: 'Avoid, Reduce, Transfer, Accept' — the four fundamental responses. Avoid eliminates risk; Reduce mitigates; Transfer shifts to third party (insurance/hedge); Accept acknowledges and monitors.

Exam tips

  • CA Intermediate numerical questions carry significant weight — practice at least 15–20 diverse problems per topic (WACC, capital budgeting, leverage, EOQ, forward/futures pricing) to build speed and accuracy under exam conditions.
  • For definition-based theory questions, structure answers with: (1) precise definition, (2) formula if applicable, (3) brief explanation of significance, (4) a one-line example or context — this earns maximum marks in descriptive format.
  • In case studies, distinguish between Financial Management issues (liquidity crunch, excessive leverage, sub-optimal dividend policy) and Strategic Management issues (competitive positioning, environmental threats, resource gaps) before suggesting remedies.
  • When calculating WACC, always show the weight calculation explicitly (market-value basis preferred). ICAI evaluators award step marks; omitting the weight derivation is a common reason for partial or no marks.
  • For IRR/NPV comparison questions, write the conclusion in both financial and strategic terms — e.g., 'Project A is financially superior (higher NPV) and strategically aligned with the company's expansion objective.'
  • In Strategic Management sections, use diagrams wherever possible: PESTEL grid, SWOT matrix, BCG matrix, Porter's Five Forces diagram, Ansoff matrix, VRIO table. Even a hand-drawn sketch earns presentation marks.
  • Leverage questions often ask for interpretation, not just calculation. Always state: 'A DOL of 2.5 means a 1% change in sales produces a 2.5% change in EBIT' before proceeding to numerical parts.
  • International Financial Management: memorize the forward rate formula F = S × (1+rd)/(1+rf) and remember to identify which currency is domestic (home) vs foreign to avoid inversion errors.
  • Hedging questions frequently test the concept of basis (Basis = Spot − Futures). Remember: Basis risk exists because spot and futures prices do not move perfectly in tandem; basis convergence happens at expiry.
  • For essay-type strategic management questions, follow the structure: Situation Analysis → Strategy Formulation (with tools) → Implementation Plan → Monitoring & Evaluation. This mirrors the strategic management process and scores well.
  • Recent ICAI exam trends show increasing integration of Financial and Strategic Management with Ethics and Professionalism — always mention ethical considerations when advising on dividend policy, takeover decisions, or aggressive hedging.
  • Quick revision priority order: (1) WACC & Cost of Capital formulas → (2) Capital Budgeting techniques → (3) Leverage calculations → (4) Working Capital models (EOQ, Miller-Orr) → (5) Strategic frameworks (Porter, BCG, Ansoff, VRIO) → (6) Risk Management & Forex.
  • In the exam, allocate approximately 40% of paper time to numerical/problem-solving questions and 60% to analytical/theory questions for this subject, reflecting the typical weightage pattern in ICAI tests.
  • When a question asks 'critically examine' or 'evaluate,' provide both supporting and opposing viewpoints before reaching a conclusion — this demonstrates the analytical depth expected at the CA Intermediate level.
  • For dividend policy questions, always compute the impact on share price using either Walter's or Gordon's model when data permits, rather than relying solely on qualitative arguments.

Common traps

  • Trap — Using pre-tax cost of debt in WACC without applying the (1−t) adjustment. Always remember: interest is tax-deductible, so the relevant cost of debt is always after-tax.
  • Trap — Confusing Degree of Operating Leverage with Degree of Financial Leverage. DOL uses Contribution/EBIT (operating costs); DFL uses EBIT/(EBIT−Interest) (financial charges). Mixing them reverses the risk insight.
  • Trap — Applying Miller-Orr model when cash flows are deterministic and regular. Miller-Orr is designed for uncertain/stochastic cash flows; for predictable cash flows, the Baumol model (EOQ analogy) is more appropriate.
  • Trap — Choosing the project with higher IRR over the one with higher NPV in mutually exclusive projects. IRR can give misleading rankings due to scale differences and timing of cash flows; NPV is the theoretically superior criterion.
  • Trap — Assuming MM propositions (no tax) apply directly to real-world decisions. MM no-tax results are theoretical benchmarks; in practice, tax shields, bankruptcy costs, agency costs, and information asymmetry make capital structure decisions highly relevant.
  • Trap — Forgetting flotation/issue costs in cost of equity calculations. If shares are issued at a discount to market or with underwriting fees, net proceeds are less than market price — this increases the effective cost of equity.
  • Trap — Treating sunk costs as relevant in capital budgeting. Sunk costs (already incurred and unrecoverable) must be excluded from incremental cash flow analysis; only future incremental cash flows matter.
  • Trap — Ignoring inflation when combining nominal discount rates with real cash flows (or vice versa). The Fisher equation states: (1+r_nominal) = (1+r_real)(1+inflation). Cash flows and discount rates must be consistent in real or nominal terms.
  • Trap — Overlooking that dividend payout ratio affects retention ratio (Retained Earnings = 1 − Payout Ratio). In Walter's model, the growth rate g is derived from Retention Ratio × Return on Equity, not chosen independently.
  • Trap — Misapplying DCF techniques to projects with different lives. Use Equivalent Annual Annuity (EAA) or Chain Replication method to compare projects with unequal useful lives.
  • Trap — Assuming hedging eliminates all risk. Hedging removes unwanted directional exposure (e.g., FX rate moves) but the hedging instrument itself carries basis risk, counterparty risk, and opportunity cost of favorable movements.
  • Trap — Using accounting profit instead of cash flows in capital budgeting. Depreciation is a non-cash expense but provides a tax shield; add depreciation back and subtract tax shield effects, never use accrual-based accounting profit directly.
  • Trap — Forgetting that in PEAD (Post-Earnings Announcement Drift), abnormal returns continue after announcements. While this is more behavioural-finance, it can appear in strategic management as market inefficiency observation.
  • Trap — Confusing strategic control (feedback loop) with operational control. Strategic control questions whether the strategy is still valid given changed conditions; operational control ensures activities conform to plans.
  • Trap — Overlooking that a negative EVA does not necessarily mean the company is unprofitable. EVA can be negative even with positive accounting profit if the capital charge (WACC × capital employed) exceeds NOPAT.
  • Trap — Treating all four forces in Porter's model as equally powerful in every industry. In reality, one or two forces often dominate (e.g., airline industry — intense rivalry and high supplier power). Context determines weighting.
