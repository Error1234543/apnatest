Card 1

Define Cost Accounting and list three primary objectives of a cost accounting system in an industrial concern.

Cost Accounting is the process of ascertaining costs and controlling them through systematic recording, classification, and allocation of expenditure. Primary objectives: 1. Ascertainment of actual cost — to determine unit cost and total cost of production/service. 2. Cost control — establishing standards and comparing actuals to identify deviations for corrective action. 3. Cost reduction — systematic effort to reduce cost per unit without impairing quality or utility. 4. Determination of selling price — providing reliable cost data for price fixation and bidding. 5. Profit measurement by activity — evaluating profitability of products, departments, processes, and contracts.

Card 2

Distinguish between Cost Control and Cost Reduction. Give one practical example of each.

Cost Control: - Aims at keeping costs within predetermined standards. - Focuses on comparison of actual vs standard and taking corrective action on variances. - Example: A factory sets a standard material usage of 5 kg per unit; if actual usage exceeds this, the cost controller investigates and takes action to bring it back to standard. Cost Reduction: - Aims at reducing the unit cost itself through permanent and lasting improvements. - Involves design changes, process re-engineering, elimination of non-value-added activities. - Example: Redesigning a component to use less material while maintaining specifications, thereby lowering the cost per unit permanently.

Card 3

What are the five main types of cost used in management decisions? Define each briefly.

1. Direct cost — traceable directly to a cost centre/product (e.g., raw material, direct wages). 2. Indirect cost — not directly traceable; requires apportionment (e.g., factory rent, supervision). 3. Fixed cost — remains constant in total irrespective of output level within a relevant range (e.g., building rent). 4. Variable cost — varies in direct proportion to output (e.g., raw material, packing cost). 5. Semi-variable (mixed) cost — contains both fixed and variable components (e.g., telephone bill = fixed line rent + variable call charges).

Card 4

Explain the ABC classification method for inventory control. How does it help in material cost control?

ABC Analysis classifies inventory items based on their annual consumption value (unit cost × annual usage). Class A: ~10–20% of items but ~70–80% of total value — require tight control, frequent review, accurate records, and low safety stock. Class B: ~20–30% of items and ~15–20% of value — moderate control. Class C: ~50–70% of items but only ~5–10% of value — loosest control, larger safety stocks acceptable. Application to material cost control: - Focus managerial attention and resources on Class A items (the 'vital few'). - Reduce working capital lock-up by optimising Class A orders. - Maintain simple records for Class C items. - Use EOQ mainly for A and B items; apply periodic review for C items.

Card 5

State and derive the Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) formula. What assumptions underlie its use?

Formula: EOQ = √(2S·D / C) or √(2S·D / Cc) Where: S = Ordering/set-up cost per order D = Annual demand (units) C or Cc = Carrying/holding cost per unit per annum Derivation (calculus approach): Total annual cost TC = Purchase cost + Ordering cost + Carrying cost TC = P·D + (D/Q)·S + (Q/2)·C (where Q = order quantity) To minimise TC, differentiate w.r.t. Q and set equal to zero: d(TC)/dQ = –(D·S)/Q² + C/2 = 0 → Q² = 2DS/C → Q = √(2DS/C) which is EOQ. Key assumptions: • Demand is known, constant, and uniform throughout the year. • Lead time is constant and known. • Price per unit is constant (no discounts). • No stock-outs occur. • Ordering cost and carrying cost are constant per unit. • Only one product is considered in isolation.

Card 6

A company uses 10,000 units of a raw material annually. Each order costs ₹150 and the carrying cost per unit per annum is ₹12. Calculate EOQ, number of orders per annum, and total annual ordering + carrying cost at EOQ.

Given: D = 10,000 units; S = ₹150/order; C = ₹12/unit/year EOQ = √(2 × 150 × 10,000 / 12) = √(3,000,000 / 12) = √250,000 ≈ 500 units Number of orders per annum = D / EOQ = 10,000 / 500 = 20 orders Annual ordering cost = (D/EOQ) × S = 20 × 150 = ₹3,000 Annual carrying cost = (EOQ/2) × C = 250 × 12 = ₹3,000 At EOQ, ordering cost = carrying cost (a useful verification check). Total annual ordering + carrying cost = ₹3,000 + ₹3,000 = ₹6,000.

Card 7

Define Labour Cost and explain how idle time and overtime are typically treated in costing.

Labour Cost represents the remuneration paid to employees for work performed. It includes basic wages, allowances, incentives, and employer's contributions (PF, gratuity, etc.). Treatment: • Idle time: – Normal idle time (expected, e.g., weekly holidays): included in overheads (factory/shop overhead) by inflating the idle-time rate; never charged directly to cost of production. – Abnormal idle time (struck, weather, power failure): treated as a period cost — written off to Costing Profit & Loss Account, NOT to cost of production. • Overtime: – Overtime premium caused by usual business fluctuations: absorbed as part of direct wages (added to rate per hour) so that normal jobs bear this cost. – Overtime premium due to special/customer urgency: charged directly to the specific job/order benefiting from it. – Overtime due to inefficient working/managerial decision: treated as an abnormal cost → written off to Costing P&L Account.

Card 9

What are the two main systems of collecting overheads? Explain how each system works and when it is preferred.

1. Single Overhead Rate System: • All overheads are pooled into one aggregate cost pool and apportioned to cost units using one universal allocation base (e.g., direct labour hours, machine hours, or percentage of direct material cost). • Preferred when the manufacturing process is homogeneous — similar products using similar resources — making a single base reasonable. • Simple and cheap to operate but can distort product costs when products consume overheads differently. 2. Multiple Overhead Rate System: • Overheads are grouped into several cost pools (e.g., material handling, machining, inspection, setup), each with its own allocation base appropriate to the cost driver. • Preferred in diversified manufacturing where different products consume different types of overheads in varying proportions. • More accurate costing and better decision-making, though more complex to administer.

Card 10

What is Absorption (Departmental) Rate? Show how it is computed using an example with two departments.

An Absorption Rate is the rate at which overheads are charged to each cost unit. It is computed as: Overhead Absorption Rate = Estimated overhead for the period / Estimated base for the period If multiple departments exist, each department has its own rate based on the most appropriate base for that department. Example: Department A (Machining) — estimated overheads = ₹6,00,000; estimated machine hours = 1,00,000 → ADR (Dept A) = ₹6,00,000 / 1,00,000 MH = ₹6 per machine hour Department B (Assembly) — estimated overheads = ₹3,00,000; estimated direct labour hours = 50,000 → ADR (Dept B) = ₹3,00,000 / 50,000 DLH = ₹6 per labour hour For a job that required 50 MH in Dept A and 30 DLH in Dept B: Overhead absorbed = (50 × ₹6) + (30 × ₹6) = ₹300 + ₹180 = ₹480.

Card 11

Explain the difference between Under-absorption and Over-absorption of overheads. How is the opening/closing WIP adjustment made before calculating overhead absorption rate?

Under-absorption: When actual overheads incurred exceed overheads absorbed/applied to production. Over-absorption: When overheads absorbed exceed actual overheads incurred. Both arise because absorption rates are usually pre-determined using estimates, while actual costs differ from estimates. Opening/Closing Work-in-Progress (WIP) Adjustment: When computing the overhead absorption rate, overheads applicable only to WIP (not to completed units) must be excluded to avoid distorting the rate. Adjusted overhead = Total overhead – Overheads absorbed in closing WIP + Overheads absorbed in opening WIP Alternatively: Overheads to be absorbed by completed production = Overheads incurred – (Closing WIP × % completion) + (Opening WIP × % completion) This adjusted figure is then divided by the chosen base (output units, machine hours, labour hours) to arrive at the correct absorption rate.

Card 12

What is the fundamental principle behind Inter-firm Cost Comparison? Why is it difficult to achieve in practice?

Inter-firm Cost Comparison is the systematic comparison of cost data between two or more firms operating in the same industry to identify areas of inefficiency and potential savings. Principle: • If firm B has lower cost per unit than firm A for similar output, A can benchmark against B to identify cost-saving opportunities — even if A's own costs are historically unchanged. Difficulties in practice: 1. Differences in product mix, quality, and specifications make unit-to-unit comparison misleading. 2. Variation in accounting policies and conventions (valuation of inventory, depreciation methods). 3. Cost data is proprietary; firms are reluctant to share sensitive information. 4. Differences in scale of operations — economies/diseconomies of scale affect unit costs differently. 5. Geographic factors — wage rates, material availability, and transport costs vary regionally. 6. Different levels of automation and technology adoption. Therefore, standardised cost sheets and common accounting conventions (as promoted by ICAI's cost audit rules) are essential prerequisites.

Card 13

In Job Costing, how do you handle (i) work-in-progress at beginning and end of period, (ii) abnormal loss, and (iii) material returned to store but not re-issued?

(i) Work-in-Progress: • Opening WIP costs are carried forward and added to current period costs for jobs not yet completed. • Closing WIP is valued at actual cost incurred so far (or at an agreed percentage of completion) and carried to next period as opening WIP. • Completed jobs transfer their total cost to finished goods stock. (ii) Abnormal loss: • Any loss above the normal expected loss in a job is treated as abnormal loss. • It is valued at the full cost per unit (material + labour + overheads) and written off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account — it must NOT inflate the cost of the good output. (iii) Material returned to store but not re-issued: • The cost of returned materials is credited to the specific job cost sheet. • This reduces the job's material cost to reflect the actual consumption; failure to credit would overstate the job cost.

Card 14

What is Contract (Long-term Construction) Costing? Describe the Treatment of retention money and the WAPAG method of profit recognition.

Contract Costing is used for long-term construction contracts (buildings, bridges, ships, roads) spanning more than one accounting period. Retention Money: • A portion of the contract price (typically 5–10%) is withheld by the client as security until defects liability period expires. • Treatment: Recognise retention money as a receivable; include in contract revenue when the right to receive becomes unconditional. WAPAG Method (Work Accelerated Percentage of Agreement — i.e., percentage-of-completion method): • Profit is recognised progressively as work progresses, based on the stage of completion. • Stage of completion = Certified work / Contract price (or costs incurred to date / total estimated costs). • Profit to date = Total estimated profit × Stage of completion • Profit transferred to P&L = Profit to date – Profit already recognised in earlier periods • Unascertained/contingent profit is excluded from calculation. Important CA Intermediate rule: If the outcome of the contract cannot be estimated reliably, no profit should be recognised (complete-contract basis or nil until completion).

Card 15

In Process Costing, how is normal loss treated differently from abnormal loss and abnormal gain? Show with a numerical illustration.

Treatment in Process Costing: Normal Loss: • Inevitable loss during processing; no cost is assigned to it (or it is valued at scrap value if any). • The cost of normal loss is borne by the good output — effectively increasing the cost per unit of good production. • Not shown separately in the Costing P&L Account. Abnormal Loss: • Loss beyond normal; valued at full cost per unit of good output. • Transferred to Costing Profit & Loss Account as a debit (loss). • Reduces output available for sale. Abnormal Gain: • Output higher than expected (less loss than normal); valued at cost per unit. • Transferred to Costing Profit & Loss Account as a credit (gain). • Increases output available for sale. Numerical Illustration: Input: 1,000 units at ₹5,000 + ₹3,000 labour + ₹2,000 overhead = ₹10,000 total cost Normal loss: 10% = 100 units, scrap value = ₹5/unit = ₹500 Expected output: 900 units Actual output: 850 units (so abnormal loss = 900 – 850 = 50 units) Cost per unit (excluding scrap) = (₹10,000 – ₹500) / 900 = ₹9,500 / 900 = ₹10.56/unit (approx) Value of abnormal loss = 50 × ₹10.56 = ₹527.78 → debited to Costing P&L Account. Value of actual output = 850 × ₹10.56 = ₹8,972.22.

Card 16

Explain the concept of Equivalent Production (Units) with a numerical example. Why is it critical in Process Costing?

Equivalent Production converts partially completed closing WIP into an equivalent number of fully completed units, enabling accurate cost per unit calculation. Why critical: • Closing WIP may be, say, 60% complete for materials and 40% for conversion costs. • Without equivalent units, costs would be incorrectly spread over fewer physical units, inflating per-unit cost. Example: Opening WIP: nil Started this period: 10,000 units Completed & transferred: 8,000 units Closing WIP: 2,000 units (60% complete for material, 40% for labour & overhead) Normal loss: nil Equivalent units (weighted average method): Materials: 8,000 (completed) + 2,000 × 60% = 8,000 + 1,200 = 9,200 units Conversion (labour + OH): 8,000 + 2,000 × 40% = 8,000 + 800 = 8,800 units If material cost = ₹46,000 and conversion cost = ₹26,400: Cost per eq. unit (material) = ₹46,000 / 9,200 = ₹5 Cost per eq. unit (conversion) = ₹26,400 / 8,800 = ₹3 Valuation of closing WIP: Material: 1,200 × ₹5 = ₹6,000 Conversion: 800 × ₹3 = ₹2,400 Total closing WIP = ₹8,400
