Quick Revision — Business Laws (CA Foundation)

CAEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Contract: An agreement enforceable by law. Every promise and every set of promises, forming consideration for each other, is a contract (Section 2(h), Indian Contract Act, 1872). Essential elements: offer and acceptance, lawful consideration, competent parties, free consent, lawful object, certainty of terms, possibility of performance, intention to create legal obligation.
  • Agreement: Every promise and every set of promises forming consideration for each other (Section 2(e)). Agreement = Offer + Acceptance. All contracts are agreements but not all agreements are contracts — only those enforceable by law qualify.
  • Promise: An accepted offer. Section 2(b) — when a proposal is accepted, it becomes a promise.
  • Proposal (Offer): When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining assent, such statement is called a proposal (Section 2(a)).
  • Consideration: 'Quid pro quo' — something in return. Section 2(d) defines consideration as when a party, at the desire of the promisor, does/abstains from doing something. Key rule: consideration may move from any person (not necessarily the promisee). Past, present, and future consideration are all valid under Indian law (unlike English law).
  • Bailment: Delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall be returned or disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them (Section 148, Sale of Goods context; defined separately under Contract Act for bailment). Bailor = one who delivers; Bailee = one to whom delivery is made.
  • Pledge: A bailment of goods as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise. Only a pledgee can acquire a special property right. Pawnbroker is a common pledgee.
  • Goods: Every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money. Includes stock, shares, growing crops, things attached to land, and agrarian interests. 'Future goods' = goods to be manufactured/produced/acquired by seller after the contract of sale is made.
  • Sale of Goods: A contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer property in goods to the buyer for a price. Distinct from an agreement to sell — sale transfers ownership immediately; agreement to sell transfers ownership at a future time or subject to conditions.
  • Partnership: The relation between persons who have agreed to share profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all (Section 4, Partnership Act, 1932). Key feature: mutual agency — every partner is both an agent and a principal for the firm and fellow partners.
  • Limited Liability Partnership (LLP): Incorporated vehicle wherein partners' liability is limited to their agreed contribution. LLP has a separate legal entity distinct from its partners (Section 3, LLP Act, 2008). Designated partners are individuals responsible for compliance matters.
  • Negotiable Instrument: A promissory note, bill of exchange, or cheque payable either to order or to bearer (Section 13, Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881). 'Negotiable' means transferable by delivery or endorsement and delivery; transferee gets a better title (nemo dat quod non habet exception applies here — holder in due course gets good title even if transferor had defective title).
  • Holder: Person entitled to bring suit upon the instrument in his own name (Section 8). Holder in due course = holder who took the instrument for value, in good faith, without belief that any defect existed in the title of the person who negotiated it (Section 9).
  • Cheque: Bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and not expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand (Section 6). Must be payable on demand only — post-dated cheques are still 'cheques' under the Act but liability shifts.
  • Promissory Note: Instrument (not a bank-note or a currency-note) where a person makes an unconditional promise to pay a certain sum only to, or to the order of, a certain person (Section 4). Must contain: written signature, unconditional promise, certain parties, certain sum, no other conditions.
  • Bill of Exchange: Instrument in writing containing an unconditional order signed by maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to a certain person or to order of/payee (Section 5). Three parties: drawer (makes the bill), drawee (ordered to pay), payee (receives payment).
  • Protest: Formal certificate of dishonour prepared by a notary public upon dishonour of a bill of exchange or promissory note (Section 100). Necessary to hold prior endorsers liable on foreign bills.
  • Consumer: (i) Any person who hires/avails of services for consideration; (ii) Any person who buys goods for consideration including via electronic means — includes use of such goods for resale only if such use was intended by the buyer. Excludes re-sale users.
  • Unfair Trade Practice: Any trade practice which, for the purpose of promoting the sale/use of goods or provision of services, adopts any false or misleading representation (Section 2(47), Consumer Protection Act, 2019). Covers misbranding, false pricing, spurious goods, hoarding, etc.
  • Deficiency in Service: Any act of omission or commission which is faulty, imperfect, or shortcoming in the quality, nature, and manner of performance of any service which is required to be performed by a trader or any other person under any law or contract (Section 2(11), CPA 2019).

Key concepts

  • For a contract to be enforceable, six essential ingredients must coexist: (1) Offer and lawful acceptance — communicated to each other (Sections 4–5); (2) Lawful consideration — must be real, not illusory, must not be something prohibited by law (Section 23); (3) Capacity to contract — every person is competent except minors (under 18), persons of unsound mind, and persons disqualified by any law (Section 11); (4) Free consent — consent must not be caused by coercion (Section 15), undue influence (Section 16), fraud (Section 17), misrepresentation (Section 18), or mistake (Section 20–22); (5) Lawful object — object must not be illegal, immoral, fraudulent, or opposed to public policy (Section 23); (6) Certainty and possibility of performance — terms must be clear, not vague (Section 29), and performance must be possible (Section 56). All agreements declared void by the Act are specifically listed in Sections 24–30.
  • Section 24: Agreement with ambiguous meaning — void. Section 25: Agreement without consideration is void — four exceptions: (a) natural love and affection (written, registered, between near relatives); (b) compensation for past voluntary service; (c) promise to pay a time-barred debt (written and signed); (d) promise to pay a wholly or partly discharged surety. Section 26: Agreement in restraint of trade — void (reasonable commercial restrictions in sale of goodwill are an exception under Section 27). Section 27: Agreement in restraint of marriage — void (except reasonable restrictions for protection of interests). Section 28: Agreement in restraint of legal proceedings — void (arbitration agreements are valid exceptions). Section 29: Vague agreements — void. Section 30: Wagering agreements — void (but insurance contracts, betting prizes under law, etc., are not wagering). Wagering agreements are collateral transactions — nothing is recoverable under them.
  • Condition: A stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract; breach of condition gives right to repudiate the contract + claim damages (Section 12). Warranty: A stipulation collateral to the main purpose; breach of warranty gives right to claim damages but NOT right to reject goods (Section 13). Treatment of breach: Under Section 55 of the 2030 Act, breach of condition may be treated as breach of warranty by agreement of parties, but breach of warranty cannot be treated as breach of condition. Goods sold by description must correspond with description; if samples are shown, goods must correspond with both sample and defects hidden in sample which reasonable examination would not reveal. Implied conditions under Section 14: (a) title to sell, (b) right to sell, (c) freedom from encumbrances, (d) sale by description, (e) sale by sample, (f) fitness for purpose when buyer relies on seller's skill/judgment.
  • Section 16: Property passes when intended to pass —ascertained/generic goods require appropriation to the contract. Section 17: Rule 1 — unascertained goods: property passes when property intended to pass is appropriated. Rule 2 — goods delivered on approval/sale or return: property passes when buyer signifies approval OR retains goods beyond reasonable time without rejection. Rule 3 — sale on sale or return: same as Rule 2. Rule 4 — goods in deliverable state: property passes when contract is made. Rule 5 — goods not in deliverable state: property passes when seller puts goods in deliverable state and buyer has notice. Rule 6 — sale by sampling: property passes when buyer approves sample. Section 22: 'Risk follows ownership' — risk passes with property, not necessarily with possession. Section 24: Unpaid seller's rights —(a) lien, (b) stoppage in transit, (c) right of resale. Transit ends when buyer's agent holds as bailee, goods are delivered to carrier bound to deliver to buyer, or carrier acknowledges holding for buyer.
  • Formation: Partnership arises from contract (not birth, not operation of law); may be oral or written; minimum 2 partners, maximum 50 (Companies Act limit). Sections 14–18 cover partnership deed essentials. Rights of partners (Section 12–13): equal sharing of profits and losses unless agreed otherwise; interest on capital @ 6% p.a. if no agreement; no interest on drawings unless agreed; manage affairs jointly. Duties: carry on business to greatest common advantage, be just and faithful, render true accounts, indemnify for loss due to fraud. Retirement (Section 32): with consent of all partners. Dissolution by agreement (Section 40). Holding out (Section 28): person representing himself as partner is estopped from denying partnership liability to third parties who have given credit on such representation. Registered vs. unregistered firm: unregistered firm can sue only if plaintiff sues as partner of registered firm and registration is proved.
  • Dishonour by non-acceptance (Section 91) or by non-payment (Section 92). Notice of dishonour must be given to prior parties (drawer, endorser) within reasonable time; failure discharges prior parties (Section 93–99). Acceptor, drawee in course of usual business, holder making demand on payee of a promissory note — discharge. Holder in due course protection:Section 58 — if holder takes instrument in good faith, for value, before maturity, without notice of defect, he gets a good title even if transferor's title was defective. Presumption (Section 118): every negotiable instrument is presumed to be made for consideration, accepted in good faith, before maturity. Cheque validity:1 year from date (Section 31A). Crossed cheques:Section 123—general crossing vs. special crossing (to a particular banker). Not negotiable crossing restricts further transfer. Protest required for foreign bills.
  • Separate legal entity: LLP is a body corporate (Section 3). Limited liability: partners' liability limited to agreed contribution (Section 28). Designated partners: minimum 2, at least 1 must be resident in India (Section 7). Name: must end with 'LLP'. Compliance: file annual returns (Form 11) by 30 May each year; file statement of accounts (Form 8) by 30 October. Incorporation: file incorporation document (Form 9) with Registrar. LLP agreement: may be oral or written; governs mutual rights/duties. Change in designated partners: intimation within 30 days (Form 4). Audit thresholds: turnover >₹40 lakhs or capital >₹25 lakhs requires statutory audit. These thresholds are revised periodically by government notification.
  • Replaced 1986 Act. E-filing allowed. Mediation mechanism introduced (Section 74). Consumer facilitation centre at district level. Online/e-commerce entities included as sellers/traders. Product liability action introduced (Chapter V). Punishment for unfair trade practices and mis-selling (Section 21). Class action suits now permitted. Jurisdiction: District Commission (up to ₹50 lakh), State Commission (₹50 lakh to ₹2 crore), National Commission (above ₹2 crore). Commissions can grant: cease and desist orders, refund, withdrawal of goods, discontinuance of unfair practices, compensation, punitive damages. Misleading advertisements attract penalties including imprisonment up to 2 years and/or fine up to ₹10 lakh; for repeated offences, imprisonment up to 5 years and fine up to ₹₹50 lakh.
  • Quantum meruit ('as much as earned'): Right to claim remuneration when performance is completed/done by one party but not accepted by other — Section 7, Section 9, Section 10 of Contract Act. Applies when: (a) agreement is void, (b) promisee prefers to receive performance, (c) agreement is dischargeable on happening of contingent event. Contingent contracts (Sections 30–36): enforceable only on occurrence/non-occurrence of uncertain future event. Time-bound contingent contracts if event becomes impossible. Impossible events: if dependent on acts of parties, void if such act is impossible; if dependent on happenstance, void when event becomes impossible. Impossibility of performance frustrates contract (Section 56).

Important facts

  • Indian Contract Act 1872 extends to whole of India except Jammu & Kashmir; operative parts came into force 1 September 1872.
  • Age of minority: 18 years (minority Act, 1875); for marriage purposes, 21 years for males and 18 years for females.
  • Maximum number of partners: 50 (Rule 10 of Companies (Miscellaneous) Rules, 2014) — exceeding requires central government permission.
  • Sale of Goods Act 1930: 'Buyer' means person buying goods; 'Seller' means person selling goods.
  • Section 64A (added by Amendment 2018): In contracts of guarantee, creditor shall inform principal debtor of variance in terms of guarantee after any variation.
  • Guarantee (Section 126): three parties — surety, principal debtor, creditor. Collateral contract. Surety's liability is co-extensive with principal debtor (Section 128) unless contract states otherwise.
  • Counterfeit (Section 480 IPC reference in NI Act): Making a false representation of a negotiable instrument.
  • Hundi vs. Promissory Note: Hundi is a traditional indigenous instrument governed by local usages; promissory note is statutory under NI Act.
  • Bills of Exchange: Must be in writing; signed by drawer; unconditional order; certain payee; certain sum; payable on demand or after fixed period.
  • Crossed cheque — Section 123: 'Account Payee' crossing is not a direction to the collecting banker to credit only the account of the payee but operates as a warning to exercise greater care.
  • LLP registration fee and stamp duty vary by state; base limit for filing Form 3 is ₹1 lakh authorised capital/turnover.
  • Under CPA 2019, complaint can be filed online (E-daakhil system) or offline. Mediation must be attempted before hearing.
  • Limitation for filing complaint under CPA 2019: 2 years from date of cause of action; can be condoned for sufficient reason.
  • Doctrine of Ultra Vires: Acts beyond the objects clause of memorandum are void and cannot be ratified.
  • Memorandum of Association (MoA): States the scope and powers of the company; contains 6 clauses — Name, Registered Office, Objects, Liability, Capital, Subscription.
  • Articles of Association (AoA): Internal rules for management; subject to MoA and Companies Act.
  • One Person Company (OPC): Section 2(62) of Companies Act 2013 — single member company with separate legal entity.
  • Liquidating damages: Clause in contract pre-fixing damages for breach — enforceable only if proven as genuine pre-estimate (Section 74, Indian Contract Act). Penalty clause is not enforceable.
  • Minor's agreement: Void ab initio (Mohori Bibi v. Dharmodas Ghose, 1903) — cannot be ratified on attaining majority; no restitutio integrum.
  • Contingent contract — Section 30: Enforceable only when event happens; wagering agreements are explicitly void.
  • Novation (Section 62): Substitution of new contract for old contract — requires consent of all parties. Rescission cancels contract. Alteration changes terms.
  • Impossibility (Section 56): Initial impossibility voids contract; subsequent impossibility discharges parties.
  • Anticipatory breach (Section 39): When a party refuses performance before due date, other party may treat contract as discharged immediately.
  • Quasi-contract (Sections 68–72): Obligations resembling contracts but not actual contracts — necessary expenditure by interested person, payment by interested person, finder of goods, goods supplied, money paid.
  • Insurable interest: Must exist at time of loss for insurance contract to be enforceable (Marine Insurance Act principle applied broadly).

Memory aids

  • Consideration exceptions mnemonic: 'NLIP' — Natural love and affection, Previous consideration, Compensating past service, Inviting time-barred debt payment. Four exceptions to Section 25 (no consideration = void).
  • Conditions precedents to liability under NI Act: 'D-A-N-H-C-P' — Demand, Notice, Acceptance, Holder in Due Course, Protest, Presentment.
  • Essential elements checklist: 'OC-CF-LO-CP' — Offer Acceptance, Consideration, Capacity, Free Consent, Lawful Object, Performance possible, Legal obligation intended.
  • Partnership maximum partners: 'C-O-M-P-A-N-I-E-S' → Count 50. Remember: 'Compelling Partners' — not more than 50 without government approval.
  • Sale of Goods implied conditions: 'T-S-F-D-S-S' — Title, Sale by description, Freedom from encumbrance, Description/Sample conformity, Fitness for purpose, Quality/Soundness.
  • Types of void agreements: 'STRAW-VVWW' — Stale proposal? No. Use: 'SV-WW-WT-WR' — Suffocation (illegal), Void agreement, Without consideration, Wagering, Restraint of trade/marriage/proceedings, Vague.
  • LLP filing deadlines: 'M-O' → May for annual return (Form 11), October for statements (Form 8). Think: 'May returns, October accounts.'
  • Consumer Commission limits: 'D-S-N' → District (upto ₹50 L), State (₹50L–2Cr), National (>2Cr). Mnemonic: 'District Small, National Large.'
  • Guarantee parties: 'S-P-C' → Surety, Principal Debtor, Creditor. Liability of surety is co-extensive (Section 128).
  • Negotiable instruments characteristics: 'T-E-R-U' → Transferable by delivery/endorsement, Evidence of debt, Right to sue in own name, Universally accepted.
  • Minor's position: 'V-R-N-C' → Void, Ratification impossible, No restitution, Cannot be agent.

Exam tips

  • Always distinguish between 'shall' and 'may' in statutory provisions. 'Shall' is mandatory; 'may' is discretionary. Questions frequently test this distinction.
  • For numerical/legal calculation questions: remember limitation periods, penalty provisions, and monetary jurisdiction limits. These are direct recall questions worth easy marks.
  • In multi-statement questions, read each statement independently. A statement may be partially correct but wrong in the legal consequence — eliminate options carefully.
  • Case law references are occasionally asked. Memorise landmark cases: Mohori Bibi (minor's contract), Lumley v Gye (breach of contract/tort overlap), Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball (invitation to world), Donoghue v Stevenson (neighbour principle).
  • For 'match the following' type questions in Business Laws: pair sections with their correct provisions. Create a section-provision table for quick revision.
  • Assertion-Reason questions: Assertion alone is not enough — verify Reason correctly explains the legal principle behind the assertion. Incorrect legal reasoning in the 'Reason' makes the option wrong even if both statements are factually true.
  • Under CPA 2019, new provisions include product liability, mediation, and class actions — these are high-yield topics for recent exams. Check for any amendments up to 2024–2025.
  • LLP Act questions often focus on designated partners, filing deadlines, and liability exposure. Remember the difference between 'partner' and 'designated partner'.
  • For NI Act, always check whether the instrument is 'to order' or 'to bearer' — this determines negotiability method. 'To order' requires endorsement + delivery; 'to bearer' requires delivery only.
  • Quick elimination strategy: In MCQs, eliminate obviously wrong options first. Two options can usually be eliminated by identifying contradictions with basic principles (e.g., a void agreement cannot be enforced by either party).

Common traps

  • Agreement vs Contract: Students often say 'all agreements are contracts.' The correct statement is 'all contracts are agreements' but not vice versa. An agreement needs enforceability to become a contract.
  • Past Consideration: Many think past consideration is invalid. Under Indian law, past voluntary service IS valid consideration (Section 25(b)). This differs from English common law.
  • Sale vs Agreement to Sell: Confusion between property passage — in sale, property passes immediately; in agreement to sell, it passes later. Risk follows property, not possession.
  • Breach of Condition vs Warranty: Students often confuse remedies — breach of condition allows repudiation + damages; breach of warranty allows only damages. However, parties can agree to treat breach of condition as breach of warranty.
  • Minor's Agreement: The trap is saying a minor's contract is 'voidable.' It is VOID AB INITIO (Mohori Bibi case). No estoppel against a minor; no restitution under Section 65.
  • Holder vs Holder in Due Course: Every holder is not a holder in due course. HIDC requires for value, in good faith, without notice of defect, before maturity. This distinction is critical for NI Act questions.
  • Crossed Cheque 'Account Payee': Trap — it does NOT mean the collecting banker must credit only the named account. It is merely a direction/warning to exercise extra care.
  • LLP Liability: Trap — partners' liability is limited to their agreed contribution, NOT to zero. If capital contribution is unpaid, liability extends to that amount.
  • Quantum Meruit: Mistakenly applied where there is a valid express contract fixing remuneration. Quantum meruit applies ONLY when there is no contract or contract is void/dischargeable.
  • Notice of Dishonour: Trap — not required to be in writing in formal sense, but must convey information of dishonour clearly. Also, notice is not required when acceptor/drawee is bankrupt or when drawer has countermanded payment.
  • Arbitration Agreement: Students confuse void wagers with valid arbitration. Wagering agreements are void; arbitration agreements are specifically exempt from the wagering prohibition under Section 28.
  • Contingent Contract vs Hope: A contingent contract depends on an uncertain FUTURE event. A mere hope or motive is not a contingent contract. Also, impossibility of event must make the contract void — not just unlikely.
  • Consumer Definition Trap: A person buying goods for resale is NOT a consumer. However, someone buying goods for self-use AND for resale (mixed purpose) — the self-use portion qualifies.
  • Omission vs Commission in Deficiency: Deficiency includes both acts of omission AND commission. Silence can be deficiency if there is a duty to speak/disclose.
  • Novation vs Rescission vs Alteration: Novation = NEW contract replacing old; Rescission = cancellation; Alteration = modification of existing terms. Each requires different consents.
