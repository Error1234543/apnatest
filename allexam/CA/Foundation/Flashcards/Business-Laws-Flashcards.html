CAEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define 'Contract' as per Section 2(h) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

An agreement enforceable by law is a contract. Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract. Formula: Agreement + Enforceability by Law = Contract.

Card 2

What is the difference between an Agreement and a Contract?

Agreement (Section 2(e)): Every promise and every set of promises forming consideration for each other. Contract (Section 2(h)): An agreement enforceable by law. All contracts are agreements, but only agreements which satisfy the essentials of a valid contract become contracts.

Card 3

List the essentials of a valid contract as per Sections 10-30 of the Indian Contract Act.

1. Offer and Acceptance (Section 2-9) 2. Lawful Consideration (Section 2(d), 23-24) 3. Capacity of Parties (Section 11) 4. Free Consent (Section 13-22) 5. Lawful Object (Section 23) 6. Agreement not expressly declared void (Sections 24-30) 7. Certainty and Possibility of Performance (Section 29, 30) 8. Legal Relations (Balfour v Balfour principle)

Card 4

Define 'Offer' / 'Proposal' as per Section 2(a). What are its essentials?

When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal. Essentials: 1. Must be communicated 2. Must be definite and certain 3. Must intend to create legal relations 4. Must not be ambiguous 5. Must differ from a mere invitation to treat

Card 5

Distinguish between Invitation to Treat and Offer. Give examples.

Invitation to Treat: A preliminary step inviting others to make offers. Not legally binding. Examples: Price tags in shops, advertisements, auction calls, tender notices, display of goods. Case: Partridge v Crittenden — Advertisements are invitations to treat, not offers. Offer: A definite promise to be bound on specified terms. Case: Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co — An advertisement offering a reward was held to be a unilateral offer because it showed intention to be bound.

Card 6

What is 'Acceptance' as per Section 2(b)? Give its rules/essentials.

When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted. Essentials: 1. Must be absolute and unconditional (Mirror Image Rule) — Hyde v Wrench 2. Must be communicated 3. Must be in prescribed mode (if specified) 4. Silence cannot be treated as acceptance — Felthouse v Bindley 5. Must follow lapsed proposal 6. Must be communicated before revocation

Card 7

Explain the rules regarding Revocation of Proposals and Acceptances under Sections 4-5.

Revocation of Proposal (Sec 4-5): 1. By notice — communicated to offeree 2. By lapse of time — reasonable time if no time fixed 3. By failure of condition precedent 4. By death/insanity of proposer (if accepted before knowledge) Revocation of Acceptance: 1. By notice — must reach proposer before dispatch of acceptance letter (postal rule exception) 2. Must be before proposal is completed into a contract Postal Rule: Acceptance complete against proposer when posted; against acceptor when received.

Card 8

Define 'Consideration' as per Section 2(d). What are its rules?

When at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person does or abstains from doing something, such act/abstinence is consideration. Key Rules: 1. Consideration may move at desire of promisor (not necessarily promisee) — Dharmapuri v Murugappa 2. Consideration must be lawful (Sec 23) 3. Past, present or future consideration all valid in India 4. Adequacy — law does not ask adequacy, only presence (Sec 25) 5. Consideration must be real, not illusory

Card 9

What is 'No Consideration, No Contract'? Explain exceptions under Section 25.

General Rule: Every contract must be supported by consideration. Exceptions to this rule: 1. Love and affection — written, registered, between near relatives (Sec 25(1)) 2. Compensation for past voluntary service — capacity to contract + past service (Sec 25(2)) 3. Promise to pay time-barred debt — written + signed by debtor (Sec 25(3)) 4. Gift — already delivered (Sec 25 proviso) 5. Completed gift 6. Statutory exceptions — e.g., agency (Sec 185), bailment (Sec 148)

Card 10

Who has the 'Capacity to Contract' under Section 11? Who is competent to contract?

Every person is competent to contract who: 1. Is of the age of majority (18 years / 1 year upon publication of guardianship order under Minority Guardians Act) 2. Is of sound mind 3. Is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject Incompetent persons: Minors, persons of unsound mind, intoxicated persons, disqualified persons. Minor's agreement is void ab initio — Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose.

Card 11

What is the legal position of a Minor's agreement? Cite Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose.

A minor's agreement is void ab initio (non est). It creates no legal rights or obligations. No restitutio in integrum — money paid to minor cannot be recovered even for fraud. The guardian cannot pledge minor's property except for necessaries. A minor can be a beneficiary (e.g., trustee, promisee). A minor can be a co-promoter. A minor cannot ratify an agreement upon attaining majority.

Card 12

Define 'Free Consent' under Section 13. When is consent said to be free?

Consent is said to be free when it is not caused by: 1. Coercion (Section 15) 2. Undue Influence (Section 16) 3. Fraud (Section 17) 4. Misrepresentation (Section 18) 5. Mistake (Section 20-22) Consent = When two or more persons agree upon the same thing in the same sense ( consensus ad idem ).

Card 13

Define 'Coercion' (Dunniya) under Section 15. What are its effects?

Committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the IPC, or unlawfully detaining/ threatening to detain property, with intent to cause any person to enter into an agreement. Examples: Threat with weapon, unlawful detention of property, suicide threat. Effect: Contract is voidable at the option of theaggrieved party. The party must prove coercion. Can involve third party. Rescinded contract — restitution of benefits (Sec 64).

Card 14

Define 'Undue Influence' (Prahnya Grahan) under Section 16. Distinguish from Coercion.

When the relations between the parties are such that one party is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage. Realms of dominance: (a) fiduciary relationships (father-daughter, doctor-patient), (b) where party can make reasonable inquiry. Burden of proof shifts to dominant party to show fairness. Difference from Coercion: Coercion involves illegal act/threat; undue influence involves abuse of position. Coercion burden of proof on aggrieved party.

Card 15

Define 'Fraud' under Section 17. List the ingredients of fraud.

Fraud means and includes any of the following acts committed by a party to a contract or with his connivance or by his agent with intent to deceive or to induce another party to enter into contract: 1. Suggestion of fact which is not true, by one who has no reasonable ground for believing it true 2. Active disclosure of a material fact known to party but not known to other party, where disclosure would be honest 3. Any promise made without intention of performing it 4. Any other act fitted to deceive 5. Any such act or omission as law declares to be fraudulent Key: Intent to deceive is essential.

Card 16

Define 'Misrepresentation' under Section 18. How is it different from Fraud?

Misrepresentation includes: 1. Positive assertion of a fact in breach of duty (without intent to deceive) — where such assertion is not true 2. Unwittingly making a statement which is untrue 3. Breach of any duty which gains an advantage to the person committing it at expense of other Difference from Fraud: No intent to deceive in misrepresentation. Innocent misrepresentation — contract is voidable. Fraud — contract is voidable and damages may also be claimed. Fraud involves dishonest intent; misrepresentation is innocent.

Card 17

What is 'Mistake'? Classify under Sections 20-22 with examples.

Section 20: Mistake of both parties to a matter of fact — agreement is void (e.g., both parties believe a horse exists, but it died before contract). Section 21: Mistake as to Indian law — not void (mistake of law is no excuse). Section 22: Mistake as to foreign law — treated as mistake of fact. Unilateral Mistake — generally not a ground for voiding unless fraud or misrepresentation involved. Important case: Cooper v Phibbs — Mistake of foreign law makes contract voidable.

Card 18

When is an agreement void under Sections 24-30? List the categories.

1. Section 24: Agreements with unlawful consideration/object — void 2. Section 25: Without consideration (exceptions apply) 3. Section 26: In restraint of marriage — void (but partial restraint may be valid) 4. Section 27: In restraint of trade — void (reasonable restrictive covenants after employment end may be valid under special laws) 5. Section 28: In restraint of legal proceedings — void (arbitration clauses excepted) 6. Section 29: Ambiguous agreements — void 7. Section 30: Agreements by way of wager — void 8. Section 31: Contingent contracts — see separate card

Card 19

What is an 'Illegal Agreement'? How does it differ from a 'Void Agreement'?

Illegal Agreement: Forbidden by law, opposed to public policy, involves harm to others, or involves a crime. Consequences: nothing can be recovered, collateral transactions also void, courts will not assist either party. Void Agreement: Not enforceable by law but not necessarily illegal. May be void ab initio or become void due to subsequent events. Key Difference: All illegal agreements are void but all void agreements are not illegal. Illegality is a deeper vice.

Card 20

Define 'Contingent Contract' under Section 31. What are its rules (Sections 32-36)?

A contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something if some event collateral to the contract does or does not happen. Rules: Sec 32: Enforceable when event occurs — cannot be enforced when event becomes impossible Sec 33: If event will never happen — void Sec 34: Time fixed for occurrence — must wait until time expires Sec 35: Impossible event — void Sec 36: Events uncertain to happen — proportional performance based on probability Example: Insurance contracts are contingent contracts.

Card 21

What is 'Quasi-Contract'? Explain with illustrations under Sections 68-72.

Quasi-contracts are certain relations resembling those created by contract, imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment. Sections 68-72: Sec 68: Claim by person supplying necessaries to incapacitated person Sec 69: Reimbursement of person paying money due by another in which he is interested Sec 70: Obligation of person enjoying benefit of non-gratuitous act Sec 71: Responsibility of finder of goods Sec 72: Person finding goods must not appropriate them These are not true contracts but legal obligations imposed to prevent unjust enrichment.

Card 22

What is 'Performance of Contracts'? Explain rules under Sections 37-46.

Section 37: Parties must perform or offer to perform promises unless law exempts. Time is essence — if fixed, must perform at that time. Section 38: Where time is not specified — within reasonable time. Section 39: Refusal of party to perform — other party may rescind and claim damages. Section 40: Application of performance — must be in mode/time directed by promisee. Section 41: Obligation of promisee whose consent cannot be obtained due to absence/ incapacity — discharge on payment to legal representative. Sections 43-46 cover joint promises, several promises, alternative promises, mutual errors, and order of performance.

Card 23

What is 'Breach of Contract'? Differentiate between actual and anticipatory breach (Sections 39, 73-75).

Actual Breach: Occurs when a party fails to perform on the due date or performs defectively. Anticipatory Breach: Occurs when before due date, a party refuses to perform or renders himself unable to perform. Remedy: Aggrieved party may sue immediately without waiting for due date — Hope v De La Bere. Damages: Sec 73 — compensation for loss arising naturally or ought to have been known. Sec 74 — liquidated damages — court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding specified amount. Sec 75 — right to compensation on rescission.

Card 24

What are the 'Remedies for Breach of Contract' under Sections 73-75?

1. Damages (Sec 73): Compensation for loss arising naturally in usual course of things, or which parties knew when making contract. Nominal damages, Special damages (must plead and prove), Exemplary/Punitive damages (rare in contract). 2. Liquidated Damages (Sec 74): Court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding amount named. 3. Specific Performance (Specific Relief Act): Court orders actual performance when damages inadequate. 4. Injunction: Court restrains breach of negative covenant. 5. Quantum Meruit: Reasonable remuneration when contract is partially performed or discharged.

Card 25

How does a contract get 'Discharged'? Explain all modes.

1. Discharge by Performance — actual or tender 2. Discharge by Agreement — novation, alteration, rescission, remission (Sec 63), waiver, release 3. Discharge by Impossibility — impossibility at outset (void u/s 56), supervening impossibility 4. Discharge by Operation of Law — death, merger, assignment, obliteration, insolvency 5. Discharge by Breach — actual or anticipatory Doctrine of Frustration (Sec 56): Supervening impossibility discharges contract when performance becomes impossible or unlawful.

Card 26

Define 'Partnership' under Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.

Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Key Elements: 1. Two or more persons 2. Agreement (contractual basis) 3. Carrying on business 4. Sharing of profits (not decisive alone — Carnac v Udny) 5. Mutual agency — 'carried on by all or any of them acting for all' Note: Co-ownership of property ≠ partnership (Subramania v Raja).

Card 27

What is a 'Partnering Firm'? How is it registered and what are the effects?

Firm: Collective name of persons carrying on business together. Registered Firm: One entered in Register maintained by Registrar. Registration (Sec 58-69): • Application on Form 'A' with firm name, principal place, partners' details • Submitted by all partners or agent Effects of Registration: • Suit against firm or partners maintainable • Firm can sue in its own name • Right to claim fees above Rs. 100 • Immunity from prosecution for unauthorized use of firm name

Card 28

What are the 'Rights of a Partner' under Sections 12-18?

1. Right to take part in conduct of business (Sec 12(a)) 2. Right to be consulted (Sec 12(b)) 3. Right to share profits equally (Sec 12(c)) 4. Right to interest on capital — only if deed provides (Sec 12(d)) — currently 6% p.a. if no deed 5. Right to interest on drawings — only if deed provides (Sec 12(e)) 6. Right to act in emergency (Sec 13(b)) 7. Right to indemnity for acts done in good faith (Sec 13(c)) 8. Right to use partnership property only for partnership purposes (Sec 13(d)) 9. Right to assign share — only right to share profits (Sec 29)

Card 29

What are the 'Duties of Partners' under Sections 9-14?

1. Duty to carry on business to common advantage (Sec 11) 2. Duty to render true accounts and full information (Sec 12) 3. Duty to be faithful and disclose gains (Sec 13(b)) — secret profits must be disclosed 4. Duty not to compete with firm (Sec 13(c)) 5. Duty to indemnify for loss caused by fraud (Sec 13(e)) 6. Duty to indemnify for loss caused by gross negligence (Sec 13(f)) 7. Mutual agency — every partner is both agent and principal

Card 30

What is the position of a 'Minor' in a Partnership Firm under Section 11?

A minor may be admitted to the benefits of partnership (not as a full partner) with consent of all partners for minimum 6 months. Rights of Minor Partner: • Access to accounts (Sec 11(3)(a)) • Share in property and profits (Sec 11(3)(b)) • Sue for account and his share upon attaining majority (Sec 11(4)) On attaining majority: • Must give public notice within 6 months — if not, deemed continuing partner • On notice, liable only for acts done after election, entitled to share of profits from admission Liability of minor limited to his share in partnership property only.

Card 31

How is a Partnership Dissolved? Differentiate Dissolution of Firm vs Dissolution of Partnership.

DISSOLUTION OF PARTNERSHIP: Change in relationship among existing partners (e.g., retirement, admission, death of one partner) — firm continues. DISSOLUTION OF FIRM: All partners cease to associate in business as partners — firm ceases to exist. Causes of Dissolution of Firm (Sec 40-44): • By agreement (Sec 40) • Compulsory (Sec 41) — insolvent partners, all or substantially all business illegal • On completion of undertaking (Sec 42) • By notice (Sec 43) — only for partnerships at will • By Court (Sec 44) — lunacy, permanent incapacity, misconduct, persistent breach, transfer of interest, business running at loss, just and equitable grounds

Card 32

What is a 'Minimum Partnership'? What are the rules regarding number of partners?

Minimum number of partners: 2 (Sec 5). Maximum: • Banking business: 10 partners (Banking Regulation Act) • Other business: 20 partners (Companies Act) If partners exceed maximum, it is an illegal association. However, partnership can have more than 20 if formed under Companies Act provisions.

Card 33

What is 'Sale of Goods Act, 1930'? Explain its applicability.

The Act extends to whole of India except Jammu & Kashmir (Sec 1). It applies to contracts for sale of goods made before, on or after commencement (Sec 3). The Act is split into 11 chapters covering definitions, formation of contract, goods, price, performance, rights of unpaid seller, suit for breach, general provisions, and definitions. Note: This Act deals with sale of movable property (goods), not immovable property.

Card 34

Define 'Goods' under Section 2(7). Classify them.

Goods means every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money. Includes: Stocks, shares, growing crops, grass, things attached to land but agreed to be severed before sale. Classification: • Existing Goods: Present ownership or future acquisition (Sec 3) • Future Goods: Goods to be manufactured/produced/acquired after contract (Sec 3) • Specific Goods: Identified and agreed upon at time of contract (Sec 4) • Unascertained Goods: Not identified at time of contract (Sec 4) • Ascertained Goods: Those identified after contract

Card 35

Distinguish between 'Sale' and 'Agreement to Sell' under Sections 4-5.

SALE: Transfer of property in goods from seller to buyer for a price. Property passes immediately. Risk passes with property. Seller can sue for price. Buyer can sue for damages for non-delivery. Seller has right of unpaid seller on buyer's insolvency. AGREEMENT TO SELL: Agreement to transfer property at a future time or subject to conditions. Property passes later. Risk remains with seller. Seller can only sue for damages. Buyer gets only right to sue for damages. Unpaid seller's rights are more limited. Key Difference: Sale transfers property now; agreement to sell transfers later.

Card 36

What is the 'Transfer of Property' rule? When does property pass (Sections 18-24)?

Specific ascertained goods: Property passes when intended to pass (Sec 20). Rule 1 (Sec 22): Unconditional contract for specific goods in deliverable state → property passes when contract made. Rule 2 (Sec 23): Specific goods in deliverable state subject to condition — property passes when condition fulfilled. Rule 3 (Sec 24): Specific goods where seller must do something to ascertain state — property passes when done and buyer notified. Rule 4 (Sec 25): Goods delivered on approval/sale or return — property passes on adoption or retention beyond return time. Rule 5 (Sec 26): Goods not in deliverable state — property passes when delivered in deliverable state.

Card 37

Define 'Condition' and 'Warranty' under Sections 12-14. What is the effect of breach?

CONDITION (Sec 12): Essential term going to root of contract. Breach gives right to repudiate contract + claim damages. WARRANTY (Sec 12): Collateral term subsidiary to main purpose. Breach gives right to claim damages only, NOT to reject goods. Sec 13: Terms may be condition or warranty by election. If breach of condition treated as breach of warranty, or vice versa depends on facts/circumstances. Guarantee (Sec 126): A promise to answer for the debt/default of another. Distinct from warranty in sale.

Card 38

What are the Rights and Duties of the Seller and Buyer under Sections 31-53?

SELLER'S DUTIES: • Deliver goods according to contract (Sec 31) • Deliver quantity as per contract (Sec 32) • Liability for quality defects if conditions not met (Sec 33) BUYER'S DUTIES: • Accept delivery (Sec 37) • Pay price according to time/terms (Sec 38) SELLER'S RIGHTS: • Right to sue for price (Sec 55) • Right of unpaid seller (Sec 46-54) BUYER'S RIGHTS: • Sue for damages for non-delivery (Sec 57) • Sue for specific performance (Sec 58) • Right to goods on seller's insolvency (Sec 56)

Card 39

What are the Rights of an 'Unpaid Seller' under Sections 45-54?

Unpaid Seller: Seller who has not been paid/partly paid (Sec 45). Rights (Sec 46): 1. Right of Lien (Sec 47-49) — retain goods until payment 2. Right of Stoppage in Transit (Sec 50-52) — stop goods during transit if buyer insolvent 3. Right of Resale (Sec 54) — resell if buyer defaults 4. Suit for Price (Sec 55) 5. Damages for non-acceptance (Sec 57) Note: These rights persist even after property passes to buyer, until price is paid.

Card 40

When does an Unpaid Seller lose his Right of Lien?

Right of lien is lost when: 1. Seller delivers goods to carrier without reserving right of disposal (Sec 49) 2. Buyer or agent lawfully obtains possession (Sec 49) 3. Seller waives the right (Sec 49) 4. Buyer becomes bankrupt/insolvent while goods are in possession After loss of lien, seller may exercise right of stoppage in transit if goods are still in transit.

Card 41

What is the Doctrine of 'Caveat Emptor'? What are its exceptions?

Caveat Emptor = Let the buyer beware. Buyer is responsible for checking quality/fitness of goods. Seller has no duty to disclose defects. Exceptions: 1. Fraudulent misrepresentation by seller (Sec 16) 2. Silence constituting fraud (Sec 17) 3. Non-disclosure of material defect known to seller and inaccessible to buyer 4. Sale by description — goods must correspond with description (Sec 15) 5. Sale by sample — bulk must correspond with sample (Sec 16) 6. Merchantable quality — where buyer makes purpose known (Sec 16)

Card 42

What is 'Auction Sale' under Section 64-65?

Auction Sale: Public sale where goods are sold to highest bidder. Sec 64: Seller may bid personally unless otherwise stated. Auctioneer may call for bids. Sec 65: Sale is complete when auctioneer announces completion. Before announcement, any bidder may retract bid. Auction advertised to have right to bid must disclose it. 'Puffs' (absurd bids to raise price) are not binding. If goods put up in lots, each lot is a separate sale.

Card 43

What is 'nstamped Instrument' under the Indian Stamp Act, 1899?

Section 2(1)(b): 'unstamped instrument' means an instrument not stamped in accordance with this Act. Instruments not duly stamped are inadmissible in evidence (Sec 35) and cannot be acted upon, admitted or given effect to. Penalty: Deficient stamp duty must be paid along with penalty. Rectification: Possible under Section 17 by paying deficit duty + penalty. Section 33: If instrument appears unstamped, officer may examine and require payment of proper duty.

Card 44

What is the 'Legal Framework' for CA Foundation Business Laws paper?

Acts covered: 1. Indian Contract Act, 1872 (most weightage ~40-45%) 2. Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (~20-25%) 3. Sale of Goods Act, 1930 (~15-20%) 4. Indian Stamp Act, 1899 (minor, ~5%) Question types: MCQs, True/False, Match Following, Case Study-based questions. Difficulty: Direct application of sections with case references. Key focus: Definitions, section numbers, essentials, distinctions, case laws, and numerical problems on partnership calculation, stamp duty, and damages.

Card 45

What is 'Novation', 'Rescission', 'Alteration' of contract? Cite relevant sections.

Novation (Sec 62): When parties agree to substitute new contract for original. Original contract discharged. Requires fresh consideration. Rescission (Sec 62): When parties agree to cancel the contract. Obligations cease to be enforceable. Alteration (Sec 62): When one or more parties agree to modify terms without changing parties. New contract for modified terms. All three require mutual consent of all parties. Mere variation without consent of all parties does not constitute novation.

Card 46

What is 'Stoppage in Transit'? Under what conditions can an unpaid seller exercise it?

Stoppage in Transit (Sec 50-52): Right of unpaid seller to stop goods while in transit after buyer becomes insolvent. Conditions: 1. Seller must be unpaid (wholly or partly) 2. Buyer must be insolvent 3. Goods must be in transit (not yet reached buyer/agent) 4. Must give notice to carrier/bailee Transit ends when: • Buyer/agent takes delivery • Carrier acknowledges holding for buyer • Goods arrive at destination and buyer takes delivery

Card 47

Explain 'Joint and Several Liability' of partners with an example.

Joint and Several Liability (Sec 25): Each partner is liable jointly with other partners and severally (individually) for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner. Example: A, B, C are partners. A commits fraud in course of business. All three are liable jointly and severally to the victim. Victim can sue any one, all, or any combination. Exception: In recovery of penalty, liability is only joint (not several) under Sec 25(2).

Card 48

What is a 'Debt' vs 'Actionable Claim'? Are they 'Goods' under Sale of Goods Act?

Debt: A sum certain payable at a future time or on demand. It is a simple obligation. Actionable Claim: A claim to any debt (other than a secured debt) or to any beneficial interest in movable property not in possession. Under Sec 2(7) of Sale of Goods Act: 'movable property' EXCLUDES actionable claims and money. Therefore, neither debt nor actionable claim is 'goods' and cannot be sold under this Act.

Card 49

What are the 'Rules of Interpretation' in Partnership deed? Cite relevant sections.

Sec 11: Minor can only be admitted to benefits (not as full partner). Sec 12: Rights of partners unless contrary intention shown. Sec 13: Duties of partners unless contrary intention shown. Sec 40: Dissolution by agreement unless contrary intention shown. Sec 42: Continuation of partnership on death — unless contrary intention shown. Sec 44: Court can order dissolution on grounds listed. The general presumption is that partnership deed governs; in absence of deed, provisions of Act apply.

Card 50

What is 'Estoppel' and 'Affirmation' under Sections 41-42?

Estoppel (Sec 41): When one person has, by his declaration, act or omission, caused another to believe a thing to be true and to act upon such belief, neither he nor his representative shall be allowed to deny the truth of that thing. Prevents a person from going back on his word. Affirmation (Sec 42): When a voidable contract is affirmed by the aggrieved party with full knowledge of facts, he loses the right to rescind. Affirmation must be with full knowledge of all circumstances.

Card 51

What is the difference between 'Conditional Sale' and 'Hire Purchase Agreement'?

CONDITIONAL SALE: • Property passes on fulfillment of condition • Buyer gets full title on payment • Risk passes with property • Default → seller recovers goods and may sue for price HIR PURCHASE: • Goods are let on hire with option to purchase • Property does not pass until option exercised • Hirer can return goods at any time before exercising option • Risk remains with owner during hire period • On default, owner can repossess goods without further claim for price Key difference: In hire purchase, risk and ownership remain with owner until option exercised.

Card 52

Explain the concept of 'Liquidated Damages' vs 'Penalty' under Section 74.

Liquidated Damages (Sec 74): Specified sum reasonable estimate of actual loss. Court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding the specified amount. Penalty: Excessive sum imposed to deter breach. In case of penalty, court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding the penalty amount. Landmark case: Fateh Chand v Bharat Kumar — In cases of liquidated damages, court may award less. In penalty cases, court awards reasonable compensation. Note: Sec 74 applies only when a sum is named as liquidated damages/penalty in contract. General damages u/s 73 also apply.

Card 53

What is 'Restitution' upon rescission of a void contract? Cite Section 64 and 65.

Section 64: When a person avoids a contract due to coercion/undue influence/fraud/misrepresentation, he must restore any benefit received. Section 65: When an agreement is discovered to be void, every person who has received any advantage under such agreement must restore it or compensate for it. Example: If A coerces B to deliver goods worth Rs. 10,000, B can recover the goods. If goods cannot be returned, B must pay reasonable compensation. Restitution aims to restore parties to original position.

Card 54

What is the significance of 'Consensus Ad Idem'? Cite a relevant case.

Consensus ad idem means 'meeting of minds' — parties must agree upon the same thing in the same sense. It is fundamental to a valid contract. Case: Coggs v Bernard — Different meanings of same words mean no consensus. Case: Raffles v Wichelhaus (Peerless case) — Two ships named Peerless existed; parties meant different ships. No consensus ad idem — contract void. Importance: Without meeting of minds, there is no valid agreement and hence no valid contract.

Card 55

What are the essentials of a 'Valid Acknowledgement of Debt' for extending limitation period under Section 19 of Limitation Act?

Essentials of valid acknowledgment: 1. Must be in writing and signed by the debtor or his authorized agent 2. Must acknowledge a subsisting debt/ liability 3. Must be made before expiry of limitation period 4. Must relate to specific debt/liability Effect: Fresh limitation period starts from date of acknowledgment. The acknowledgment relates back to the original transaction. Oral acknowledgment is not sufficient.

Card 56

What is a 'Bill of Exchange' vs 'Promissory Note'? Are they 'Goods' under Sale of Goods Act?

BILL OF EXCHANGE (Negotiable Instruments Act, Sec 5): • Signed by drawer ordering drawee to pay • Three parties: drawer, drawee, payee • May be accepted by drawee PROMISSORY NOTE (Sec 4): • Signed by maker promising to pay • Two parties: maker, payee • No acceptance needed Neither bill of exchange nor promissory note are 'goods' under Sale of Goods Act as they are actionable claims / documents of title, not movable property.

Card 57

What is 'Transit' under Sections 50-52? When does it begin and end?

Transit begins when seller delivers goods to carrier/bailee for transmission to buyer, and ends when buyer or his agent takes delivery from carrier. Transit is NOT ended when: • Buyer's agent receives goods on seller's behalf • Carrier acknowledges holding for buyer (unless buyer directs redelivery to seller) Transit IS ended when: • Buyer takes delivery • Carrier wrongfully refuses delivery • Goods arrive at destination • Bailee acknowledges holding for buyer

Card 58

What are the key differences between 'Agency' (Contract Act) and 'Partnership'?

AGENCY: • Created by agreement (express/implied) • Agent acts on behalf of principal • Principal bound by agent's acts within authority • Agency is contractual relationship PARTNERSHIP: • Created by agreement (contractual basis) • Every partner is agent AND principal • Each partner binds all others by acts within authority • Partnership is a relation, not a contract itself (Sec 4) Key Difference: In agency, one party represents another. In partnership, all partners represent each other mutually. Agency can exist without partnership; partnership implies mutual agency.

Card 59

What is the 'Right of Set-Off' available to an Unpaid Seller?

Set-off is the right of a seller to adjust the amount due from the buyer against any other amount owed by the buyer to the seller. Under Sec 49 of Sale of Goods Act, an unpaid seller exercising right of lien may retain goods till payment of price, and if buyer defaults, seller may sell goods. Additionally, under Contract Act principles and Commercial Practice, where two mutual debts exist between same parties, set-off is permissible. In partnership, partners' personal debts cannot be set off against partnership debts without consent.

Card 60

What is a 'Standing Offer'? When does it cease to be binding? Cite relevant case law.

Standing Offer: An offer which remains open for a definite period or until revoked. Tenders are typical examples. Case: Routledge v Grant — Offeror can revoke at any time before acceptance, even if promised to keep open. Case: Harvela v Royal Trust — Irrevocable offer in sealed bid tender must be honored if terms clearly indicate. Key Point: A standing offer can be revoked at any time by giving notice. Acceptance must be communicated before revocation reaches offeree. Once revoked, subsequent acceptance is无效.

Card 62

What are the 'Special Damages' under Section 73? When can they be claimed?

Special Damages: Losses arising from special circumstances peculiar to the particular contract, beyond the ordinary course of things. Requirements: 1. Special circumstances must have been communicated/know to both parties at time of contract 2. Must be proven specifically (not presumed) 3. Must be directly attributable to breach Example: A sells machine to B knowing B needs it for a specific order. If A breaches, B can claim loss of profit from that specific order as special damages. General damages (natural losses) are presumed; special damages must be pleaded and proved.

Card 63

What is the effect of 'Impossibility of Performance' under Section 56? Explain with cases.

Section 56 — Agreement to do impossible act: 1. Initial Impossibility: Agreement to do impossible act is void ab initio (e.g., agreement to discover treasure, impossible task) 2. Supervening Impossibility (Doctrine of Frustration): • A contract to transport by ship which was destroyed before shipment — void • Excelsior Manufacturing Co v Parry Corp — impossibility due to war • Satyabrata Ghose v Mugneeram Bangur — flood preventing performance frustrates contract • Krell v Henry — coronation procession cancelled frustrates hire of viewing room Effect: Contract becomes void; parties discharged from further performance; obligations to perform already accrued may remain.

Card 64

What are the 'Essential Elements of a Valid Contract' that distinguish it from a social/agreement?

Essential Elements: 1. Offer and Acceptance — lawful and communicated 2. Consideration — lawful and real 3. Capacity — parties must be competent (major, sound mind, not disqualified) 4. Free Consent — not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, mistake 5. Lawful Object — not forbidden, not defeated by law, not immoral, not opposed to public policy 6. Intention to create legal relations — social/domestic agreements generally not enforceable 7. Legal remedies available — enforceable by law 8. Certainty and possibility of performance Note: Balfour v Balfour — domestic agreements lack intention to create legal relations.

Card 65

Explain 'Remission' under Section 63. What forms can it take?

Section 63: Every promisee may dispense with or remit (reduce/forbear) full performance of a promise, or extend time, or accept substituted performance. Acceptance of lesser sum does not discharge obligation (Pinnel's Case — payment of smaller sum on due date is no satisfaction for greater sum). Forms of Remission: 1. Accepting lesser amount at different place/time 2. Accepting substitute performance 3. Extending time for performance 4. Partial performance accepted 5. Waiver of strict compliance Once remission is accepted, it cannot be revoked unless agreed otherwise.

What is the scope of 'Indian Stamp Act, 1899' in CA Foundation? What are key penalties?

The Indian Stamp Act, 1899 deals with stamp duty on instruments. Scope in CA Foundation: • Section 2 definitions: 'instrument', 'duly stamped' • Section 3: Duty must be paid before execution • Section 17: Examination of instruments by collecting officer • Section 20: Impounding of instruments not properly stamped • Section 33: Production of unstamped instruments in court • Section 35: Stamping affects admissibility in evidence • Section 40: Penalty for using unstamped instruments Penalties: Additional stamp duty + penalty (typically double the deficit). Instruments not duly stamped are inadmissible in evidence.
