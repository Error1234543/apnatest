1. Introduction to Accounting: Concepts, Conventions, Principles and Standards

Accounting is the systematic process of identifying, measuring, recording, classifying, summarising, analysing, interpreting and communicating financial information to decision-makers. It serves three primary objectives: (a) maintaining systematic records of business transactions, (b) ascertaining profitability and financial position, and (c) providing relevant information to users for economic decisions.

Key Concepts:
• Business Entity Concept: The business and its owners are separate legal entities. Owner's capital is treated as a liability of the business.
• Going Concern Concept: The business is assumed to continue indefinitely unless there is evidence to the contrary. This justifies charging depreciation rather than revaluing assets.
• Money Measurement Concept: Only transactions expressible in monetary terms are recorded. Qualitative factors like employee morale are excluded.
• Accounting Period Concept: The indefinite life of a business is divided into equal intervals (usually one year) for periodic reporting.
• Cost Concept: Assets are recorded at their original acquisition cost, not market value. Depreciation gradually writes off this cost over the asset's useful life.
• Dual Aspect Concept: Every transaction has two aspects — debit and credit. This forms the basis of double-entry bookkeeping: Assets = Liabilities + Capital.
• Realisation Concept: Revenue is recognised when the title in goods passes to the buyer (or services are rendered), not necessarily when cash is received.
• Accrual Concept: Income and expenses are recorded when they are earned or incurred, regardless of when cash is received or paid.
• Matching Concept: Expenses incurred to earn revenue of a period must be matched against the revenue of that same period. This requires accruals, prepayments, and provisions.
• Objectivity Concept: Accounting records should be based on verifiable evidence such as invoices, vouchers and bank statements.

Key Conventions:
• Convention of Consistency: Accounting policies should remain unchanged from one period to another so that results are comparable. Changes must be disclosed.
• Convention of Conservancy (Prudence): Anticipate no profits but provide for all foreseeable losses. Creates provisions for doubtful debts, depreciation, etc.
• Convention of Materiality: Only significant items that influence decisions need strict adherence to accounting principles. Immaterial items may be treated flexibly.
• Convention of Disclosure: Full and fair disclosure of all material information in financial statements, including notes forming part of the accounts.

Accounting Standards (AS): These are authoritative statements issued by the ICAI to standardise accounting policies and practices. Key AS for Foundation level include AS-1 (Disclosure of Accounting Policies), AS-2 (Valuation of Inventories), AS-3 (Cash Flow Statements), AS-4 (Contingencies and Commitments), AS-6 (Depreciation), AS-7 (Construction Contracts), AS-9 (Revenue Recognition), AS-10 (Accounting for Fixed Assets), AS-11 (Foreign Exchange Transactions), AS-15 (Employee Benefits), and AS-16 (Borrowing Costs). Under the new NEP, Ind AS adoption is progressively being phased in for certain entities.

2. Accounting for Partners' Firms: Basics, Admission, Retirement and Death

A Partnership Firm is governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. It is defined as the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Key features: minimum 2 partners, maximum 50 (per Companies Act), mutual agency, sharing of profits, and continuity is not guaranteed.

Admission of a Partner:
When a new partner is admitted, several adjustments are required:
• Goodwill Adjustment: The old partners sacrifice a portion of their profit-sharing ratio. New partner brings goodwill in cash or adjusts it through partners' capital accounts. Treatment of goodwill: (a) retained in the firm — shown on balance sheet; (b) withdrawn by old partners — no entry needed for goodwill after adjustment; (c) distributed among old partners only.
• Revaluation of Assets and Liabilities: All assets are revalued and all liabilities (including contingent liabilities) are accounted for. The net gain or loss is transferred to old partners' capital accounts in their old profit-sharing ratio.
• Adjustment of Capital: Capitals of all partners are adjusted to reflect the new profit-sharing ratio. This can be done by introducing/withdrawing cash or by adjusting through current accounts.
• Change in Profit-Sharing Ratio: Existing partners may also change their ratio without admitting a new partner, requiring goodwill adjustment in the sacrificing ratio.

Retirement/Death of a Partner:
The retiring/deceased partner is entitled to:
• Capital balance as per the revalued balance sheet
• Share of goodwill
• Share of reserves/unappreciated assets in the old ratio
• Share of profit up to the date of retirement/death (based on sales/time basis)
• Interest on capital and salary if provided in the partnership deed
• Accumulated losses if any
The continuing partners' new ratio becomes their gaining ratio. The amount payable to the retired/deceased partner is transferred to their Loan Account, which is later paid off in installments.

3. Admission, Retirement, Death and Dissolution of Partnership Firm

Dissolution of a Partnership Firm means the termination of the relationship between all partners. There are two types: Dissolution of Partnership (among some partners only) and Dissolution of the Firm (complete wind-up).

On Dissolution — Realisation Account:
All assets and liabilities (excluding cash/bank and partners' loans) are transferred to the Realisation Account. The account is debited with unrecorded assets and credited with unsold assets taken over by partners at agreed values. Any payment made to outside creditors is debited; receipts from sale of assets are credited. The net gain or loss is transferred to partners' capital accounts in their old profit-sharing ratio (not sacrificing ratio).

Special Cases:
• Assets taken over by partners: Credit the Realisation Account with the agreed value. If no value is given, use book value.
• Liabilities taken over by partners: Debit the Realisation Account. If liabilities are not shown in the balance sheet but are taken over, the excess taken over is credited to the Realisation Account.
• Outside creditors not claimed: Credit the Realisation Account.
• Unrecorded assets: Debit the Realisation Account.
• Contingent liabilities: Provided for in the Realisation Account if they become payable; otherwise ignored.

Piecemeal Distribution:
When cash is available in installments, it should be distributed using the Maximum Loss Method (also known as the Rule in Exall's case):
Step 1: Assume all non-cash assets are worthless (maximum possible loss).
Step 2: Calculate how much each partner would receive if all assets were sold for zero.
Step 3: Distribute available cash accordingly, ensuring no partner receives more than their maximum entitlement.
This method ensures that no partner is paid more than their ultimate claim, preventing subsequent adjustments.

4. Redemption of Debentures

Debentures are long-term borrowing instruments representing a debt owed by the company. They carry a fixed rate of interest and are secured or unsecured. Redemption means repayment of debenture money to debenture holders.

Methods of Redemption:
(a) Redemption out of Profits: The company creates a 'Debenture Redemption Reserve' (DRR) out of profits equal to the amount of debentures to be redeemed. As per Section 71 of the Companies Act, 2013, DRR is created by debiting the profit and appropriation account and crediting the DRR account. The DRR can be utilised only for the purpose of redeeming debentures and is treated as a capital reserve.
Note: The requirement of DRR was waived for fully paid-up debentures issued by companies in respect of Public Issue, but still applies for private placements.
(b) Redemption out of Capital: Permitted only if the company has sufficient free reserves and the articles of association authorise it. A special resolution is required.
(c) Redemption by Purchase of Securities in the Open Market: The company purchases its own debentures in the stock market. Any difference between cost and face value is transferred to the Capital Redemption Reserve or Profit & Loss Account.
(d) Conversion into Shares: Debenture holders are issued shares in lieu of debentures.

Journal Entries for Redemption out of Profits:
1. Creation of DRR: DRR A/c Dr. To Profit & Loss A/c
2. Investment of funds: Debenture Investment A/c Dr. To Bank A/c
3. On redemption: Debentures A/c Dr. To Bank A/c
4. Transfer of investment cost: P&L A/c Dr. To Debenture Investment A/c
5. Transfer of investment gain/loss: Debenture Investment A/c Dr./To P&L A/c
6. Transfer DRR to CPR: P&L A/c Dr. To DRR A/c; then DRR A/c Dr. To Capital Redemption Reserve A/c

Important: For tax purposes, DSR (Debenture Switch Reserve) or SCD (Schedule for Conversion of Debentures) may also be relevant in specific schemes.

5. Departmental Accounts

Departmental Accounts are maintained when a business operates through multiple self-contained departments (e.g., clothing, grocery, electronics) to ascertain the profitability of each department separately.

Key Principles:
• Each department is treated as a separate business entity.
• Inter-department transfers are recorded at cost or cost plus a percentage of profit.
• Direct expenses of a department are charged entirely to that department.
• Common expenses are apportioned on a rational basis.

Apportionment Bases for Common Expenses:
• Rent, Rates, Taxes — Area occupied by each department
• Electricity — Meter reading or area
• Salaries/Wages — Number of employees or sales value
• Advertising — Sales value or space occupied
• Delivery Van — Weight or volume of goods
• Office Expenses — Sales value or floor area
• Carriage Inward — Purchase value or weight
• Carriage Outward — Sales value or number of items

Inter-Department Transfers:
• At cost: No profit is recognized; simple transfer entry is made.
• At cost plus profit: Profit is recognized in the transferring department. If transferred at cost plus x%, the profit element is eliminated in the final accounts by creating an 'Inter-Departmental Profit Elimination Account'.
Profit outstanding on unsold stock = Transfer price × [x/(100+x)]
Transfer Price = Cost × [(100+x)/100]

Final Preparation:
Prepare individual departmental Trading and Profit & Loss A/c, then a Consolidated P&L A/c. The profit of each department is shown separately in the consolidated statement.

6. Branch Accounts

Branch Accounts are maintained to track the performance of branches that are either dependent (non-independent) or independent.

Types of Branches:
• Dependent/Branch not keeping complete books: The branch maintains only a cash book and a record of goods received/sent. The head office maintains the branch ledger. Goods are sent at invoice price (cost plus profit). Stock and debtors are reported to the head office.
• Independent/Branch keeping complete books: The branch maintains its own full set of accounts. A 'Branch Account' is maintained at the head office as a personal account.

Methods of Accounting for Dependent Branches:
(a) Debtors Method: Goods sent to branch are recorded at cost. Branch debtors are adjusted for returns, bad debts, discount allowed, and cash collected. The balance of the Branch Account represents the outstanding stock at cost.
(b) Stock Method: Stock at depot and branch, along with goods in transit, are recorded. The Branch Account shows stock adjustments, closing stock, and the profit element.

Proportionate Stock Method (for goods sent at invoice price):
Stock Reserve = Closing Stock at invoice price × [GP%/Invoice Price]
Where GP% on sales = Gross Profit / Sales × 100
GP% on invoice price = GP% on cost / (100 + GP% on cost) × 100

Stock and Debtors Adjustment:
When stock and debtors are not reported timely, adjustments are needed:
Closing Stock at invoice price = Opening Stock + Goods Sent – Sales – Closing Stock (at cost)
Goods in Transit = Goods sent but not yet received by the branch.

Final Accounts: Prepare Branch Trading and Profit & Loss A/c at the head office, eliminating the stock reserve from the profit figure.

7. Consignment Accounts

Consignment is a arrangement where the owner (consignor) sends goods to another party (consignee) who sells them on behalf of the consignor. The consignee acts as an agent and is paid commission. Ownership remains with the consignor until the goods are sold.

Key Features:
• Goods remain the property of the consignor.
• Consignee earns commission on sales.
• Risk and reward belong to the consignor.
• Unsold stock remains with the consignee but belongs to the consignor.

Cost of Consignment includes: Cost of goods + Non-recurring expenses (cartage, freight, insurance, loading, warehousing) + Recurring expenses (warehouse rent, salaries, advertising). Recurring expenses are transferred to the Consignment Trading A/c.

Commission:
• Ordinary Commission: A percentage on goods sold.
• Break-down/Success Commission: A percentage on goods sold above a specified minimum value.
• Higher Commission: A percentage on the surplus profit after a basic commission is calculated.

Abnormal Loss: Loss of goods due to fire, accident, theft, etc. Valued at cost plus proportionate non-recurring expenses. Insurance claim is raised separately.
Normal Loss: Goods lost in transit or damaged are valued at zero or scrap value. The cost of normal loss is borne by the good units (increasing the cost per unit of remaining goods).

Valuation of Closing Stock: Closing stock is valued at cost plus proportionate non-recurring expenses only (not recurring expenses). If goods are sent at a markup, the stock is valued at invoice price less the unrealized profit element.
Unrealized Profit = Closing Stock at invoice price × [Profit margin %]
If profit is on cost: Profit margin % = Profit/(Cost+Profit) × 100
If profit is on invoice price: Profit margin % = Given directly.

8. Joint Venture Accounts

A Joint Venture is a temporary business association formed for a particular project or transaction. Each joint venturer contributes capital, shares profits/losses as agreed, and the venture is dissolved upon completion.

Methods of Recording Joint Ventures:
(a) Through Separate Books of Accounts: A joint venture contract is drawn up, and a separate set of books is maintained. A Joint Venture A/c is prepared as a nominal account, and a Balance Sheet is drawn to show assets and liabilities of the venture.
(b) Through Memory Records (Consignee Method): Each venturer records transactions relating to the joint venture in their own books using a 'Joint Venture A/c' (personal account). No separate books are kept.

Journal Entries (through memory records):
• Goods contributed: Joint Venture A/c Dr. To Goods Sent on JV A/c
• Cash/Bank contributed: Joint Venture A/c Dr. To Cash/Bank A/c
• Expenses paid: Joint Venture A/c Dr. To Cash/Bank A/c
• Goods purchased for JV: Joint Venture A/c Dr. To Supplier A/c
• Sales made: Buyer A/c Dr. To Joint Venture A/c
• Final settlement: Cash/Bank A/c Dr. To Joint Venture A/c

The balance in the Joint Venture A/c represents profit or loss. Goods sent on joint venture that remain unsold are shown as closing stock in the balance sheet of the respective venturer.

9. Royalty Accounts

Royalty is consideration paid for the right to use someone else's property, such as mines, patents, copyrights, or leases. The owner of the rights is the 'Lesser'; the user is the 'Lessee'.

Key Terms:
• Minimum Rent/Royal Minimum: The minimum fixed rent payable periodically regardless of output/sales. Also called Ground Rent.
• royalty: Usually a percentage of output produced or sales made.
• Watch Period: The initial period during which the lessee pays minimum rent even if royalties are lower.
• Redemption of Minimum Rent: Recovery of excess minimum rent paid over the watch period from future royalties.
• Short Working: When actual royalty is less than minimum rent.
• Short Operations: Cumulative short working over previous periods that can be recovered.

Calculation of Royalty:
Royalty Payable = Output/Volume × Rate per unit
If Royalty < Minimum Rent: Pay Minimum Rent; record Short Working.
If Royalty > Minimum Rent: Pay Royalty; recover Short Working from previous periods (if within recovery period).

Writing Back Short Working:
Short working can be written back only during the recovery period specified in the agreement (commonly 3-5 years). Amount recovered = Lower of: (a) current short working available, (b) excess of royalty over minimum rent in current period.

Journal Entries:
• For royalty payable: Royalty A/c Dr. To Lesser's A/c
• For payment: Lesser's A/c Dr. To Bank A/c
• For short working: Short Working A/c Dr. To Lesser's A/c
• For writing back: Lesser's A/c Dr. To Short Working A/c
• For minimum rent: Minimum Rent Receivable/Payable A/c Dr. To Bank/Lesser's A/c

10. Hire Purchase and Installment System Accounts

Hire Purchase (HP) Agreement: The goods are delivered to the buyer (hirer) who pays in installments. The ownership (property in goods) passes only after the last installment is paid. The seller (hire vendor) retains ownership until full payment.

Installment System: Similar to HP but ownership may pass at different stages. The key distinction in accounting is that in HP, the hirer treats goods as own asset from the beginning; in installment sale, the seller may retain title until a specified condition is met.

Important Concepts:
• Installment Due: The total amount due from the hirer including principal and interest.
• Hire Charges/Interest: Usually embedded in the installment amount. Calculated on the outstanding balance using the reducing balance method or flat rate.
• Default/Repossession: If the hirer defaults, the goods may be repossessed. The hire vendor can sue for the balance due.

Valuation of Goods Repossessed:
If goods are repossessed partially:
Amount due = Total installments due – Installments paid – Recovered amount (if any)
The goods are valued at the amount due minus any realized value.
Full repossession:
Goods are taken back and shown in the seller's books at the amount due outstanding less any realizable value. The profit/loss on repossession is transferred to the P&L A/c.

Installment Method of Calculating Interest (Annuity Approach):
For calculating the interest component in each installment, the reducing balance method is used. The interest for each period is calculated on the outstanding principal at the end of the previous period.

11. Insurance Claims under Proximate Cause, Average and Standing Loss Clauses

Insurance is a contract where the insurer agrees to indemnify the insured against specified losses in consideration of a premium.

Principles of Insurance:
• Principle of Utmost Good Faith (Uberrimae Fidei): Both parties must disclose all material facts truthfully.
• Principle of Insurable Interest: The insured must have a financial stake in the subject matter.
• Principle of Indemnity: The insured is compensated only for the actual loss suffered, not to make a profit.
• Principle of Proximate Cause: The loss must be directly caused by an insured peril.
• Principle of Subrogation: After indemnity, the insurer steps into the shoes of the insured to pursue recovery from third parties.
• Principle of Contribution: If multiple policies cover the same risk, each insurer contributes proportionately.

Loss Clauses:
• Specific Average Clause: Loss is payable only if it exceeds a specified percentage of the insured value.
• Free from Particular Average (F.P.A.): No claim for partial loss unless it exceeds a specified percentage or the vessel is stranded/sunk/burnt.
• With Average (W.A.): Loss is payable if it exceeds a specified average percentage.
• All Risks Clause: Covers all physical loss or damage except those specifically excluded.

Standing Loss Clause (Continuing Damage Clause):
When the sum insured is less than the actual value (underinsurance), and a partial loss occurs, the claim for standing/continuing loss is adjusted proportionately:
Claim = (Sum Insured / Actual Value) × (Loss at current value)
This clause protects the insured against underinsurance for losses that persist beyond the immediate claim period.

12. Bills of Exchange and Promissory Notes

A Bill of Exchange is a written instrument containing an unconditional order, signed by the drawer, directing the drawee to pay a certain sum of money to a specified person on demand or at a fixed/future date.

Key Terms:
• Drawer: The person who makes/draws the bill.
• Drawee: The person ordered to pay (becomes Acceptor upon acceptance).
• Payee: The person to whom payment is to be made.
• Tenor: The duration/duration of the bill.
• Nominee: The person appointed by the drawer to receive the amount on death of the payee.
• Noting: Formal recording of non-acceptance/non-payment by a Notary Public.
• Protest: Formal certificate of non-payment issued by the notary.

Days of Grace: Three extra days are added to the due date of a bill (except for demand bills). The due date is determined using the Rule of Completes (3 months = same date in third month).

Discounting a Bill: The holder discounts the bill with a bank before maturity. The bank deducts discount and credits the net amount. If the drawer is personally liable, an endorsement in full is made. If not, a blank or restrictive endorsement suffices.

Dishonour: If the drawee refuses to accept or pay, the bill is dishonoured. The holder must give notice of dishonour to the drawer and prior endorsers. They become liable for the amount due.

13. Accounting from Incomplete Records (Single Entry)

Single Entry System is an informal method where only cash and personal accounts are maintained. Real and nominal accounts are not regularly recorded. It is commonly used by small businesses.

Steps to Prepare Financial Statements from Incomplete Records:

Step 1: Ascertain Outstanding and Prepaid Items
Adjust outstanding expenses (add to expense), prepaid expenses (deduct), outstanding income (add), and income received in advance (deduct).

Step 2: Find out Missing Figures using Rectangular Method or Missing Figure Method:
(a) Opening Stock: Purchases + Direct Expenses – Closing Stock – COGS
(b) Closing Stock: Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses – COGS
(c) Purchases: COGS + Closing Stock – Opening Stock – Direct Expenses
(d) COGS: Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses – Closing Stock

(e) Debtors: Cash Received + Closing Debtors – Opening Debtors – Discounts/Allowances + Sales
(f) Creditors: Cash Paid + Closing Creditors – Opening Creditors – Discounts/Allowances – Purchases

Step 3: Prepare Statement of Profit & Loss (similar to a nominal account): All incomes and gains on the credit side; all expenses and losses on the debit side. The balance is net profit or net loss.

Step 4: Prepare Statement of Affairs:
Assets (at estimated realizable values) are listed on the debit side; Liabilities (at estimated amounts) on the credit side. The difference is Capital.
Opening Capital = Closing Capital + Net Loss (or – Net Profit) + Drawings – Additional Capital Introduced
This reconstructed balance sheet shows the financial position of the business.

Important facts

  • Accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital (the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping)
  • Partnership is governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932; minimum 2, maximum 50 partners
  • Goodwill on admission is adjusted in the sacrificing ratio of old partners
  • Revaluation profit/loss on admission is shared in the OLD profit-sharing ratio
  • Capital adjustment on admission is done in the NEW profit-sharing ratio
  • DRR (Debenture Redemption Reserve) is created out of profits and can only be used for redemption
  • Under Companies Act 2013, DRR is NOT required for fully paid debentures issued via public issue
  • In departmental accounts, inter-department transfers at cost plus profit require elimination of unrealized profit
  • Realisation Account is used ONLY on dissolution of a firm, not on admission or retirement
  • On dissolution, assets taken over by partners are credited at agreed value to Realisation Account
  • In consignment, closing stock is valued at cost plus proportionate NON-RECURRING expenses only
  • Normal loss in consignment increases the per-unit cost of remaining goods
  • Abnormal loss in consignment is valued at cost plus proportionate non-recurring expenses
  • In joint venture through memory records, each venturer maintains a Joint Venture A/c in their own books
  • Royalty: Short Working = Minimum Rent – Actual Royalty; write-back limited to recovery period
  • Hire Purchase: ownership passes only after the last installment; partial repossession uses amount due method
  • Insurance claim under underinsurance: Claim = (Sum Insured/Actual Value) × Loss
  • Standing Loss Clause protects against continuing losses when goods are underinsured
  • Bills of Exchange: 3 days of grace are allowed; due date computed using the Rule of Completes
  • Noting charges and protest fees are borne by the drawer/endorser when a bill is dishonoured
  • Single Entry: Missing figures are found using equations like: Debtors = Cash + Closing Debtors – Opening Debtors – Discounts + Sales
  • Statement of Affairs is the balance sheet equivalent for single entry systems
  • AS-2 (Inventories), AS-6 (Depreciation), AS-9 (Revenue Recognition) are most frequently tested
  • Maximum Loss Method (Rule in Exall's case) is used for piecemeal distribution on dissolution

Formulas and relationships

Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital
GP Ratio (%) = (Gross Profit / Sales) × 100
Profit Margin (%) = (Profit / Sales) × 100
Sales = Purchases + Closing Stock – Opening Stock + Gross Profit
COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases + Direct Expenses – Closing Stock
Debtors Ledger: Closing Debtors = Opening Debtors + Credit Sales – Cash Received – Discounts – Bad Debts
Creditors Ledger: Closing Creditors = Opening Creditors + Credit Purchases – Cash Paid – Discounts
Stock Reserve = Closing Stock at Invoice Price × [GP% on Invoice Price]
GP% on Invoice Price = GP% on Cost / (100 + GP% on Cost) × 100
Transfer Price = Cost × (100 + Markup%) / 100
Unrealized Profit in Closing Stock = Closing Stock at Invoice Price × Profit Margin%
Short Working = Minimum Rent – Actual Royalty
Royalty Payable = Output/Volume × Rate per unit
Amount Due on Partial Repossession = Total Installments Due – Installments Paid – Repo Value
Insurance Claim (Underinsurance) = (Sum Insured / Actual Value) × Loss
Claim under Standing Loss Clause = (Sum Insured / Actual Value) × Continuing Loss
Net Profit (Single Entry) = Closing Capital – Opening Capital – Additional Capital + Drawings
Closing Capital (Statement of Affairs) = Total Assets – Total Liabilities
Profit on Customs/Excise (Recovery) = Duty Paid – (Duty Payable on sales)
Sacrificing Ratio = Old Ratio – New Ratio (for partners giving up share)
Gaining Ratio = New Ratio – Old Ratio (for partners gaining share)

Common mistakes

  • Using the NEW profit-sharing ratio instead of the OLD ratio for revaluation profit/loss distribution on admission
  • Including recurring expenses in the valuation of closing stock in consignment accounts (only non-recurring expenses are included)
  • Confusing Realisation Account (used only on dissolution) with Trading Account (used for normal operations)
  • Forgetting to adjust for stock reserve when preparing final accounts with inter-departmental transfers
  • Incorrectly applying the Rule of Completes for bills: counting the starting day but not adding days of grace
  • Not distinguishing between normal loss (absorbed by good units) and abnormal loss (charged to Realisation/Trading A/c)
  • Treating Depreciation as a provision for replacement (it is a charge against profit for wear and tear, not a fund)
  • Using the wrong base for apportionment of common expenses in departmental accounts
  • Forgetting that DRR can only be transferred to Capital Redemption Reserve and cannot be used for any other purpose
  • In hire purchase, recording the goods in the hirer's books before ownership actually passes
  • Applying the underinsurance formula incorrectly — using (Actual Value/Sum Insured) instead of (Sum Insured/Actual Value)
  • Not considering the standing loss clause when a partial loss is followed by continuing damage
  • In single entry, failing to convert drawings into capital by adding them back when computing opening capital
  • In bills of exchange, confusing the term 'drawer' (maker) with 'drawee' (person ordered to pay)
  • Forgetting that noteholder can recover noting charges and protest fees from the drawer/endorsers
