1. Indian Contract Act, 1872: Introduction and History

['The Indian Contract Act, 1872 (Act 9 of 1872) is one of the most important pieces of legislation governing commercial transactions in India. It was based on English common law principles but drafted by the Indian Law Commission under the chairmanship of Sir James Fitzjames Stephen, with Sir Narayan Ganesh Randhava as a member.', "The Act received the Governor-General's assent on 1 September 1872 and came into force on 1st September 1872. It extends to the whole of India except the State of Jammu & Kashmir (prior to reorganisation) and currently does not apply to the states of Nagaland, Meghalaya, and Arunachal Pradesh in their entirety — specific notifications govern this.", 'The Act has been amended several times, notably by the Indian Contract (Second Amendment) Act, 2017, which introduced provisions relating to electronic contracts, digital signatures, and e-records. Section 10A was inserted to give legal recognition to contracts entered through electronic mode.', 'The Act is divided into five parts: Part II (Chapters I-V) deals with general principles of contract; Part III (Chapter VI) deals with contingent contracts; Part IV (Chapter VII) deals with agreements declared void; Part V (Chapters VIII-XII) deals with performance, breach, and remedies; and Parts VI-X deal with bailment, pledge, agency, partnership, and sale of goods.', 'Key distinction: The Contract Act is a general law of obligations. Other special laws like the Partnership Act, Sale of Goods Act, Insurance Act, Negotiable Instruments Act operate as special statutes that supplement the general contract principles.']

2. Indian Contract Act: Formation of a Valid Contract

["Section 2(h) defines a 'contract' as 'an agreement enforceable by law'. This two-part test — agreement + enforceability — is fundamental. Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract.", "Section 2(e) defines 'agreement' as 'every promise and every set of promises forming consideration for each other'. Section 2(b) defines 'promise' as when a proposal, once accepted, becomes a promise. Section 2(a) defines 'proposal/offer'.", 'Section 10 lays down the essentials of a valid contract: (i) Offer and acceptance — free and genuine; (ii) Intention to create legal relationship; (iii) Lawful consideration; (iv) Capacity of parties; (v) Free consent; (vi) Lawful object; (vii) Not expressly declared void; (viii) Certainty and possibility of performance; (ix) Legal formalities (writing/registration if required).', 'Essential elements explained in detail:', '• Offer and Acceptance: A valid contract requires at least one offer and its unconditional acceptance (Section 4-6). Offer must be communicated (Section 4), accepted absolutely and unconditionally (Section 7), and in prescribed mode unless waived.', '• Intention to Create Legal Relationship: Social/domestic agreements are not enforceable (Balfour v. Balfour). Commercial agreements are presumed to have legal intent (Shirlaw v. Norman). Exceptions exist where parties clearly intend otherwise.', "• Lawful Consideration (Section 25): 'Consideration' means QUID PRO QUO — something in return (Section 2(d)). It may move from promisor or any other person. Consideration must be real, lawful, and not illusory. Past consideration is generally invalid except under three exceptions: (a) written & signed, (b) voluntary, (c) made while promisor was alive. Past voluntary service cannot support a promise for remuneration after the fact unless conditions met.", "• Capacity of Parties (Section 11): Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority (18 years per Majority Act, 1875; 21 years if minority extended by court), of sound mind, and not disqualified by any law. Minors' agreements are void ab initio (Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose).", '• Free Consent (Section 13-22): Consent is said to be free when not caused by coercion (S.12), undue influence (S.16), fraud (S.17), misrepresentation (S.18), or mistake (S.20-22).', '• Lawful Object (Section 23): Object is unlawful if forbidden by law, defeats provisions of any law, fraudulent, involves injury to person/property, immoral, or opposed to public policy.', '• Certainty and Possibility of Performance (Section 29-30): Agreements must not be uncertain in meaning and must be capable of performance.']

3. Indian Contract Act: Void and Voidable Contracts

['Void Agreement (Section 2(d)): An agreement not enforceable by law is void. Void agreements include: agreement without consideration (S.25, with exceptions); agreement in restraint of marriage (S.26); agreement in restraint of trade (S.27, with exceptions for selling goodwill); agreement in restraint of legal proceedings (S.28); agreements of uncertain meaning (S.29); wagering agreements (S.30); impossible agreements (S.36).', 'Voidable Contract (Section 2(i)): A contract that becomes void due to lack of free consent. Coercion (S.12): committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by IPC, or unlawfully detaining property — consent obtained thereby is coerced. Undue Influence (S.16): relationship where one party can dominate will of another; burden shifts to dominant party to prove fairness. Fraud (S.17): includes suggestion of false fact, active concealment, promise without intention to perform, any other act designed to deceive, or any act dishonestly. Misrepresentation (S.18): innocent false statement, breach of duty without fraud, or innocent leading to mistake. Mistake (S.20-22): bilateral mistake of fact (void); unilateral mistake (generally not void unless caused by fraud/misrepresentation); mistake of law (not void unless mistake of foreign law).', 'Difference between Void and Voidable: A void agreement is null from beginning; a voidable contract is valid until avoided by the aggrieved party. A void contract ceases to be enforceable; a voidable contract remains enforceable unless rescinded. Consequences differ — restitution may apply to both but in different contexts.', 'Void Agreements vs. Void Contracts: A void agreement is never enforceable. A void contract starts as valid but becomes unenforceable due to subsequent events (e.g., supervening impossibility under S.56).']

4. Indian Contract Act: Performance and Discharge of Contracts

['Performance of Contracts (Sections 37-54): When parties must act (S.37) — time is essence of contract (S.55) in mercantile dealings. Tender of performance (S.38-39): tender must be of full amount and valid form; if refused, promisor is excused from performance.', 'Joint Promisors (S.42) and Promisees (S.43-44): Joint promisors must fulfil jointly; promisee may compel any joint promisor to bear equal share.', 'Discharge of Contract — modes of discharge:', '• By Performance (S.37-39): Actual or tender of performance discharges obligations.', '• By Agreement (S.62): Novation (replacing old contract with new), Rescission (cancelling contract), Alteration (changing terms), Remission (accepting lesser fulfillment), Waiver (voluntary放弃), Mixtures of some/all.', '• By Impossibility (S.56): Agreements to do impossible acts are void. Initial impossibility — void ab initio. Supervening impossibility (frustration) — contract becomes void when occurrence makes performance impossible or unlawful (e.g., destruction of subject matter, change of law, death of person with personal skill).', '• By Lapse of Time (Limitation Act): Obligations barred by limitation period (generally 3 years for contract claims).', '• By Operation of Law: Death (in personal service contracts), merger, obliteration, etc.', '• By Breach: Actual breach before due date or anticipatory breach allows aggrieved party to treat contract as discharged and claim damages (S.73-75).', 'Remoteness of Damages (S.73): Compensate for any loss arising naturally in usual course of things, or which parties knew at time of contract to be likely to result. Hadley v. Baxendale rule applies. Exemplary/damages for breach of honour/bodily harm are available in appropriate cases.']

5. Contingent Contracts, Quasi-Contracts, and Indemnity/Guarantee

["Contingent Contracts (Sections 31-36): Enforcement depends on happening/non-happening of an uncertain future event. Rules: Impossible events — void if happens (S.32), void if doesn't happen (S.33). 'If/whenever' language (S.34): enforceable only when event occurs within specified time. Impossible events by nature (S.35): void. Must be real and possible, not impossible acts or illegal.", "Quasi-Contracts (Sections 68-72) — also called 'Relative Contracts': The law implies obligations resembling contracts even where no actual contract exists. Five types under Indian law:", '• S.68: Claim by person supplying necessities to incapable person (minor, mentally ill, etc.) — liable from his property.', '• S.69: Reimbursement of person paying money due by another — must be interested in payment.', '• S.70: Obligation of person enjoying benefit of non-gratuitous act — must lawfully do something for another not gratuitously.', '• S.71: Finder of goods — responsibilities and rights similar to bailee.', '• S.72: Person who has paid under duress/coercion or mistake — entitled to reimbursement.', 'Indemnity (Section 124) vs Guarantee (Section 126): Indemnity involves two parties — promisor (indemnifier) and promisee (indemnified) — where one promises to save other from loss. Guarantee involves three parties — surety, principal debtor, creditor — where surety guarantees payment of debt or performance of duty.', "Key distinctions: Indemnity is always bilaterally binding; guarantee is collateral to main contract. In indemnity, obligor is liable from the start; in guarantee, surety's liability arises only upon default of principal debtor. Both require consideration (S.127).", "Rights of Surety (S.140-141): Subrogation, contribution (between co-sureties S.146-148), right to securities held by creditor (S.141), right to sue principal debtor after discharge. Discharge of surety: revocation by notice (S.130), death (S.131), variance in terms (S.133), release/novation (S.134), creditor's act impairing surety's eventual remedy (S.139)."]

6. Agency: Creation, Duties, and Termination

['Agency (Sections 182-238): An agent is a person employed to do any act for another or to represent another in dealings with third persons. Principal is the person for whom act is done. Agency arises from agreement (express/implied), necessity, ratification, estoppel, holding out.', "Creation of Agency: Express appointment (written/oral authority); Implied agency (by conduct, custom, marital relationship); Agency of necessity (emergency situation requiring action for principal's benefit — conditions: impossibility of communication, genuine necessity, prudent person standard, good faith); Apparent/stopped agency (holding out — principal representing another as agent, third party relying in good faith); Ratification (Section 196-201): ex-post-facto adoption of unauthorized act; must be of whole transaction; retroactive effect; cannot prejudice third parties; must be of lawful act; must have knowledge.", 'Duties of Agent (Sections 211-221): Diligence (reasonable care/skill); Compliance with instructions; Communication to principal; Non-delegation (delegatus non potest delegare — may delegate on custom or necessity with notice); Fidelity — no secret profits, must render accounts, not deal on own account without consent, no conflict of interest.', 'Duties of Principal (Sections 222-223): Pay agreed commission; Reimburse expenses; Indemnify for lawful acts done in good faith.', 'Rights of Agent: Right to commission/S.217 — lien on goods/documents for commission/demands. Authority of agent (S.218-220): express, implied incidental, customary.', 'Termination of Agency (S.201-212): By act of parties — revocation by principal, renunciation by agent, completion of business, expiration of term, death/incapacity of either party (S.202), insolvency. By operation of law — transformation into partnership or mutual agency, expiration of time/wrongful revocation (liable for damages).']

7. Indian Partnership Act, 1932: Introduction and Nature

["Section 4 defines partnership as 'the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all'. Key elements: (i) Two or more persons; (ii) Agreement; (iii) Business; (iv) Sharing of profits; (v) Mutual agency — the cardinal principle.", 'Mutual Agency (S.4): Each partner is both principal and agent of others. Partners bind each other by acts done in ordinary course of business. Without mutual agency, there is no partnership (even profit-sharing alone is not sufficient — see Cox v. Hickman principle applied in Indian context).', 'Partnership Deed: Not mandatory by law but highly recommended. Contains name, business nature, commencement date, duration, capital contributions, profit-sharing ratio, interest on capital/drawings, salary/commission to partners, duties, admission/retirement/illness provisions, dispute resolution, dissolution terms. Registered deed has evidentiary value under S.58.', 'Registration (S.58-59): Must be filed with Registrar of Firms along with statement containing partnership name, principal place, names of partners, duration, dates of admission/retirement, capital contributions. Effect of non-registration: Cannot sue third parties or other partners; firm itself cannot sue; defense available to registered firms even against non-registered ones. Registration is declaratory not constitutive.', 'Types of Partnership: General partnership (S.11) — unlimited liability; Limited Liability Partnership (covered separately under LLP Act, 2008).']

8. Partnership: Rights, Duties, and Dissolution

["Rights of Partners (S.12-13): Equal rights in management (S.12(a)); Right to take part in decisions (major matters need unanimity — S.12(i)); Right to be consulted; Right to express opinion on ordinary matters (majority decides); Entitlement to remuneration only if agreed (S.13(a)); Interest on capital at 6% p.a. if agreed (S.13(b)); Interest on drawings charged; Use of partnership property only for business; Priority in repayment — partners' loans rank after outside creditors but before capital.", 'Duties of Partners (S.14-15): Duty to carry on business to greatest common advantage; Duty to be just and faithful to each other; Duty to render true accounts and full information; Duty to indemnify for loss caused by fraud; Duty to indemnify for neglect of duty; Indemnify for over-payment made in good faith; Indemnify for loss caused by want of candour; Duty to indemnify for gain from competition; Duty to account for private profit from partnership transactions.', 'Dissolution of Firm (S.40-44): Dissolution means ending of partnership relation; Dissolution of partnership may occur without dissolution of firm. Modes: By agreement (S.40); Compulsory dissolution (S.41) —全部 partners bankrupt,全部 business unlawful; By notice (S.43) — partnership at will, 60 days notice; By happening of specified event (S.42); By adjudication of partner as insolvent (S.42(d)); By prohibition/order of Court (S.44) — permanent loss of capacity, transfer of entire share, continual breach,转让 to stranger, conduct making it impractical to carry on.', "Effects of Dissolution (S.46-54): Continuing authority of partners for winding up only; Settlement of accounts — losses, capital, remaining assets distributed pari passu after external debts; Unregistered firm's limitations persist post-dissolution."]

9. Indian Sale of Goods Act, 1930: Basics and Conditions/Warranties

["Section 4 defines sale of goods: 'a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price'. Essential elements: (i) Two parties (buyer & seller); (ii) Goods; (iii) Transfer/agreement to transfer property (not mere possession); (iv) Price (money consideration).", 'Types of contracts: Sale — property passes immediately; Agreement to sell — property to pass at future time or on conditions; hire-purchase (goods transferred for hire with option to purchase — not governed by SOGA), lease, bailment (no sale element).', 'Goods (Section 2(7)): Every movable property other than actionable claims and money — includes stock, shares, growing crops, things attached to land but agreed to be severed before sale. Includes existing goods (S.3) and future goods (S.3(2)).', 'Condition vs Warranty (S.12-14): Condition is a stipulation essential to main purpose of contract; breach gives right to repudiate contract + claim damages. Warranty is stipulation collateral to main purpose; breach gives claim for damages only, not rejection. Identification of condition/warranty is by substance and nature, not label. S.13: Where breach of condition treated as breach of warranty, or seller waives condition. S.14: Inferior quality / not answering description — treated as breach of condition but may be treated as warranty.', 'Transferring Property (Sections 18-23): Specific goods in deliverable state — property passes when intended (S.18); Specific goods requiring work to put in deliverable state — when work completed and buyer notified (S.19); Specific goods not in deliverable state — when in deliverable state and buyer notified (S.20); Goods sold by description from ascertained bulk — when conforming goods unconditionally appropriated (S.21); Goods on approval or return basis — when buyer signifies approval or retains without rejecting (S.22); Goods sent on approval or sale-or-return — when seller adopts the transaction.']

10. Sale of Goods: Formation, Transfer of Property, and Rights of Unpaid Seller

['Formation of Contract of Sale (S.5-7): Must satisfy Contract Act essentials plus SOGA-specific requirements. S.7: Agreement to sell becomes a sale when conditions are fulfilled or time elapses; Sale becomes agreement to sell when conditions are unfulfilled or future time fixed.', 'Transfer of Property in Goods to Buyer (S.18-24): See above section on conditions/warranties for detailed rules on passing of property.', 'Goods must exist (S.6): Future goods may be sold by agreement to sell only. Specific goods perishing before contract (S.7): Void if no fault of either party.', "Unpaid Seller's Rights (S.45-61): An unpaid seller is one who has not received full price; Partial payment still leaves status of unpaid seller. Unregistered firm cannot sue for price (exception: registration defense unavailable against registered firm).", "Seller's Liens (S.47-49): Right of unpaid seller in possession — (a) where whole price not paid/earned without credit; (b) goods sold on credit but credit period expired; (c) buyer becomes insolvent. Exercise by withholding delivery or giving notice. Loss of lien (S.49): Voluntary delivery to carrier without reserving right, buyer or agent obtaining possession lawfully.", "Stoppage in Transit (S.46, 50-52): When buyer insolvent, unpaid seller may resume possession of goods while in transit. 11. Important Doctrines and Case Laws in Contract Law

['De Minimis Non Curat Lex: Law does not concern itself with trifles — immaterial breaches may not entitle rescission.', 'Pacta Sunt Servanda: Agreements must be kept — binding nature of contracts is sacrosanct in commercial law.', "Caveat Emptor (Let the buyer beware): Buyer is responsible for checking quality/fitness of goods before purchase (applies strictly in sale of goods; modified under Consumer Protection Act). Exceptions: fraud, misrepresentation, seller's acknowledgment of fitness, merchant dealing in goods of that kind, sale by description where buyer relies on seller's skill/judgment.", 'Undue Influence (S.16): Presumed in relationships of confidence (parent-child, doctor-patient, solicitor-client, guardian-ward). Burden of proof shifts to influencing party to show transaction was fair, independent advice obtained, and no exploitation.', 'Economic Duress: Though not expressly mentioned in Indian Contract Act, courts have increasingly recognized economic pressure as a ground for setting aside contracts, especially in commercial disputes.', 'Promissory Estoppel (S.25 exception): Where one party makes a promise intending it to be acted upon, and the other acts on it, the promise is binding even without consideration — equity will not permit going back on such promise.', "Key Case Laws: Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903) — minor's agreement void ab initio; Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. (1893) — unilateral offer, advertisement as offer, acceptance by performance; Shirlaw v. Aggrieved party may either wait for performance date and sue on breach, or treat repudiation as immediate breach and sue immediately.']

13. Exam-Focused Revision: High-Yield Topics and Common Traps

['High-Yield Areas for CA Foundation:', '• Essentials of valid contract (S.10) — frequently tested directly and in caselets', '• Distinction between condition and warranty (S.12-14) — core SOGA concept', '• Rights and duties of partners (S.12-15) — direct questions + numerical problems', '• Dissolution modes (S.40-44) — especially compulsory dissolution and notice-based', "• Unpaid seller's rights (S.45-61) — lien, stoppage in transit, resale", '• Quasi-contracts (S.68-72) — finding questions matching facts to provisions', "• Minor's agreement (Mohori Bibee case) — cornerstone of capacity doctrine", '• Indemnity vs Guarantee distinction — classic comparison question', '• Agency creation and termination — practical application questions', 'Common Traps to Avoid:', '• Profit-sharing alone does not establish partnership (S.6 exclusion clause is narrowly interpreted)', '• Past consideration is generally void BUT has three exceptions under S.25', '• Registration of partnership is not mandatory but has serious consequences for litigation rights', "• In quasi-contract S.70, the act must be 'non-gratuitous' — gratuitous acts don't qualify", "• Minor's agreement is void ab initio — not merely voidable; minor cannot be bound even by ratification after attaining majority", "• Stoppage in transit requires buyer's insolvency; mere inability to pay is insufficient", '• In partnership dissolution by notice (S.43), only partnership at will can be dissolved this way — fixed-term partnerships require different grounds', "• Goods must be 'movable property' — immovable property transactions fall outside SOGA", "• Under S.45, 'unpaid seller' includes partially paid seller — partial payment doesn't remove the status", "• Contingent contracts under S.32 vs S.33 — the 'if' vs 'whenever' distinction determines enforceability timing"]

Important facts

  • Contract = Agreement enforceable by law (S.2(h))
  • Every contract is an agreement; every agreement is not a contract
  • Minor's agreement is void ab initio — Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose (1903)
  • Essentials of valid contract under S.10: offer+acceptance, intention, consideration, capacity, free consent, lawful object, certainty, not void, legal formalities
  • Consideration = Quid Pro Quo (something in return) — S.2(d)
  • Three exceptions to past consideration rule under S.25
  • Age of majority = 18 years generally; 21 years if court extends minority
  • Undue influence presumption arises in parent-child, doctor-patient, solicitor-client relationships
  • Contingent contract enforceable when event happens (S.32) or when it becomes impossible (S.33)
  • Quasi-contracts under S.68-72: necessity supply, payment by interested person, non-gratuitous benefit, finder of goods, payment under mistake/duress
  • Indemnity (S.124) = 2 parties; Guarantee (S.126) = 3 parties
  • Surety's rights: subrogation (S.140), contribution (S.146-148), right to securities (S.141)
  • Partnership = relation between persons agreeing to share profits of business carried on by all or any acting for all (S.4) — mutual agency is key
  • Partnership registration not mandatory but unregistered firm cannot sue
  • Dissolution by notice (S.43) only for partnership at will — 60 days notice required
  • Condition breach = repudiate + damages; Warranty breach = damages only
  • Caveat Emptor = buyer's duty to inspect; modified by consumer protection law
  • Property passes when intended to pass (S.18) — specific goods in deliverable state
  • Unpaid seller's rights: lien (S.47), stoppage in transit (S.46), resale (S.54)
  • Stoppage in transit requires buyer's insolvency specifically
  • LLP = separate legal entity with limited liability; minimum 2 designated partners, 1 resident in India
  • Losses on dissolution borne first from profits, then capital, then equally (S.48)

Formulas and relationships

Interest on capital = Capital × Rate × Time (default rate 6% p.a. if agreed under S.13(b))
Profit-sharing ratio determines capital return and loss bearing on dissolution (unless agreed otherwise)
Unpaid seller's lien: Seller retains possession when whole price not paid/earned (S.47(a))
Damages for non-delivery = Difference between contract price and market price at time of breach (S.57)
Surety's right of contribution: Each co-surety liable to contribute equally (S.148)
Partnership account on dissolution: External debts → Partners' loans → Capital → Profits (S.48-49)

Common mistakes

  • Confusing void agreement with voidable contract — void is null from beginning; voidable is valid until avoided
  • Assuming profit-sharing automatically means partnership — S.6 excludes certain profit-sharing arrangements
  • Thinking minor's agreement is merely voidable — it is void ab initio; minor cannot ratify after majority
  • Forgetting that past consideration has three exceptions under S.25
  • Mixing up S.32 and S.33 contingent contracts — 'if' vs 'whenever' language is critical for enforcement timing
  • Assuming registration of partnership is mandatory — it is optional but affects litigation rights
  • Treating warranty breach as giving right to reject goods — only condition breach allows rejection
  • Assuming stoppage in transit applies whenever buyer cannot pay — buyer must be insolvent
  • Thinking partnership dissolves automatically on death — notice must be given to third parties to prevent holding-out liability
  • Confusing indemnity (2 parties) with guarantee (3 parties) — the structure and liability sequence differ fundamentally
  • Assuming LLP partners have unlimited liability — LLP provides limited liability to all partners
  • Forgetting that S.70 requires the act to be 'non-gratuitous' — gratuitous acts do not attract quasi-contract liability
