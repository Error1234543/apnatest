1. Introduction to Business Economics

["Economics is the social science that studies how individuals, businesses, governments, and societies allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants. 'Business Economics' applies economic theory and tools to solve practical business problems and support managerial decision-making. It bridges abstract economic theory and real-world enterprise management.", 'Microeconomics examines the behaviour of individual economic units — a consumer, a firm, an industry — and deals with price determination, output decisions, factor pricing, and market structures. Macroeconomics looks at the economy as a whole, analysing aggregate variables such as national income, employment, inflation, economic growth, money supply, and international trade.', "Positive economics states 'what is' — it deals with factual, testable propositions (e.g., 'An increase in price reduces quantity demanded'). Normative economics states 'what ought to be' — it involves value judgments and policy recommendations (e.g., 'The government should raise the minimum wage'). Business economics draws heavily on positive analysis but also incorporates normative considerations when advising on policy.", 'The scope of business economics includes demand analysis (forecasting and estimating demand), cost and production analysis (determining optimal input combinations), pricing decisions under different market structures, profit management, and capital budgeting. It also touches upon macro-environmental factors — inflation, fiscal policy, monetary policy — that affect business planning.', 'Key assumptions underlying business economics include ceteris paribus (other things remaining constant) when analysing relationships between two variables, rational behaviour of economic agents (consumers maximise utility, firms maximise profit), and the availability of relevant information, though behavioural economics has relaxed the perfect-rationality assumption in advanced treatments.']

2. Theory of Demand

['Demand refers to the quantity of a commodity that a consumer is willing and able to purchase at various prices during a given period of time. It is not merely desire or need; it requires both willingness and purchasing power. The law of demand states that, ceteris paribus, there is an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded — as price falls, quantity demanded rises, and vice versa. The exceptions to the law of demand include Giffen goods (inferior goods where a price rise increases demand due to strong income effect), Veblen goods (status-symbol luxury items), and situations of sheer speculation.', 'Types of demand: (a) Individual demand vs market demand (the horizontal summation of all individual demands at each price). (b) Derived demand — demand for a factor of production arising from the demand for the final product (e.g., demand for steel is derived from demand for cars). (c) Joint/complementary demand — goods consumed together (cars and petrol). (d) Competitive/substitute demand — goods that can replace each other (tea and coffee). (e) Deferred demand — demand postponed expecting a future price decline.', 'The demand schedule is a table showing the relationship between price and quantity demanded. The demand curve is its graphical representation, typically downward sloping from left to right. A change in price causes a movement along the same demand curve (change in quantity demanded), while a change in any non-price determinant shifts the entire curve (change in demand). Shifts to the right indicate increased demand; shifts to the left indicate decreased demand.', 'Determinants of demand (non-price factors): consumer income (normal goods: demand rises with income; inferior goods: demand falls as income rises), prices of related goods (substitutes: price of coffee rises → demand for tea rises; complements: price of petrol rises → demand for cars falls), tastes and preferences, population size and demographics, expectations about future prices and income, and government policies (taxes, subsidies).', 'Elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in one of its determinants. Price elasticity of demand (Ed) = Percentage change in quantity demanded / Percentage change in price = (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P) = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q). It is normally negative, but we often refer to its absolute value. Types: perfectly inelastic (Ed = 0), inelastic (0 < Ed < 1), unit elastic (Ed = 1), elastic (Ed > 1), perfectly elastic (Ed = ∞).', 'Methods to measure price elasticity: (a) Total Outlay (Total Expenditure) Method — if price falls and total expenditure rises, demand is elastic; if total expenditure falls, demand is inelastic; if unchanged, unit elastic. (b) Point Method — elasticity at a specific point on the demand curve, useful for linear curves where Ed = lower segment / upper segment. (c) Arc (Average) Method — used when the change is large: Ed = [(Q₂ − Q₁)/(Q₂ + Q₁)/2] ÷ [(P₂ − P₁)/(P₂ + P₁)/2].', 'Income elasticity of demand (Ey) = %ΔQd / %ΔY. For normal goods Ey > 0; for inferior goods Ey < 0. Cross elasticity of demand (Exy) = %ΔQx / %ΔPy. For substitutes Exy > 0; for complements Exy < 0. These concepts are vital for business forecasting and strategic pricing decisions.']

3. Theory of Supply

['Supply refers to the quantity of a commodity that producers are willing and able to sell at various prices during a given period. The law of supply states that, ceteris paribus, there is a direct (positive) relationship between price and quantity supplied — as price rises, producers are willing to supply more, and as price falls, supply contracts. The supply curve slopes upward from left to right.', 'Exceptions to the law of supply: (a) Labour supply may backward-slope at high wages (leisure becomes preferred over extra income). (b) Rare/antique goods — fixed stock means supply cannot increase despite price rise. (c) Corner goods in a depressed market where sellers dispose of stock irrespective of price. (d) Expectation of further price rise — sellers may withhold supply.', "Determinants of supply (non-price factors): cost of production (input prices, technology), prices of related goods (joint products, competitive products), technology and productivity, prices of inputs (wages, rent, interest, raw material costs), taxes and subsidies, weather conditions (especially for agricultural goods), number of sellers in the market, and sellers' expectations of future prices.", 'Supply schedule is the tabular form; the supply curve is the graphical form. Movement along the curve results from a price change (change in quantity supplied). A shift of the entire curve results from changes in any non-price determinant — rightward shift = increase in supply, leftward shift = decrease in supply.', 'Elasticity of supply (Es) = Percentage change in quantity supplied / Percentage change in price = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q). Factors affecting elasticity of supply: nature of the commodity (agricultural goods are less elastic due to production time lags), time period (supply is more elastic in the long run), availability of raw materials, mobility of factors, and state of technology. Goods with long gestation periods have relatively inelastic supply in the short run.']

4. Equilibrium Determination of Price and Output

['Price and output equilibrium in a market is determined by the interaction of demand and supply. Equilibrium occurs at the point where the demand curve and supply curve intersect — the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied (Qd = Qs). At this price (equilibrium price or market-clearing price), there is no tendency for the price to change, and there is neither shortage nor surplus.', "If the market price is above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating a surplus (excess supply). Competitive pressure among sellers forces the price down toward equilibrium. If the market price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating a shortage (excess demand). Competitive pressure among buyers drives the price up toward equilibrium. This adjustment mechanism is known as the 'invisible hand' of the market.", 'Shifts in demand or supply change the equilibrium. An increase in demand (rightward shift) raises both equilibrium price and quantity. An increase in supply (rightward shift) lowers equilibrium price and raises equilibrium quantity. Simultaneous shifts in both curves produce ambiguous effects on either price or quantity depending on the relative magnitude of the shifts — e.g., if both demand and supply increase, quantity definitely rises but the price effect depends on which shift is larger.', 'Government intervention: Price ceiling (maximum legal price set below equilibrium, e.g., rent control) creates persistent shortage and black markets. Price floor (minimum legal price set above equilibrium, e.g., minimum support price for farmers) creates persistent surplus and requires government purchase or storage. Both distort the market outcome and create welfare losses.', 'Market structures affect how equilibrium is reached and who sets price. In perfect competition, individual firms are price-takers and equilibrium is purely market-determined. In monopoly, the single seller sets price. In imperfect competition, firms have some price-setting power. Understanding equilibrium is essential before analysing these differing structures.']

5. Theory of Consumer Behaviour

['Consumer behaviour theory explains how consumers allocate their limited income among various commodities to maximise total satisfaction (utility). Utility is the want-satisfying power of a commodity. Total utility (TU) is the sum of utility from consuming all units. Marginal utility (MU) is the additional utility from consuming one more unit: MU = ΔTU/ΔQ. The law of diminishing marginal utility states that as a consumer consumes more units of a commodity, the additional utility from each successive unit eventually declines. This law is fundamental to deriving the downward-sloping demand curve.', "Consumer's equilibrium under the cardinal approach (Marshallian): A consumer is in equilibrium when he allocates his income so that the marginal utility per rupee spent is equal across all commodities and total utility is maximised. Condition: MUx/Px = MUy/Py = MUm (where MUm is the marginal utility of money). If MUx/Px > MUy/Py, the consumer should buy more of X and less of Y until equality is restored. This implies the equi-marginal principle.", 'Under the ordinal approach (Indifference Curve Analysis), utility is ranked rather than measured numerically. An indifference curve (IC) shows all combinations of two goods that give the consumer equal satisfaction. Properties: (a) IC slopes downward to the right. (b) ICs never intersect. (c) ICs are convex to the origin (due to diminishing marginal rate of substitution). (d) A higher IC represents higher satisfaction. (e) An IC is thinner from above and thicker from below (they do not meet).\nMarginal Rate of Substitution (MRSxy) = ΔY/ΔX — the rate at which a consumer is willing to substitute good Y for good X while remaining on the same indifference curve. MRS diminishes as more of X is consumed, reflecting the convex shape.', 'A budget line (budget constraint) shows all combinations of two goods that a consumer can purchase given his income and the prices of the two goods. Equation: Px·X + Py·Y = M (where M is money income). The slope of the budget line = −Px/Py. When income changes, the budget line shifts parallelly. When the price of one good changes, the budget line rotates.\nConsumer equilibrium under ordinal analysis is achieved at the tangency point of the highest attainable indifference curve and the budget line, where MRSxy = Px/Py. At this point, the consumer maximises utility given the budget constraint.', 'Slutsky decomposition separates the price effect into substitution effect and income effect. The substitution effect (always negative — price fall leads to more consumption due to relative price change) and income effect (can be positive for normal goods or negative for inferior goods). For Giffen goods, the income effect outweighs the substitution effect in the opposite direction, producing an upward-sloping demand curve.']

6. Theory of Production and Costs

['Production is the process of creating utility (form, place, time, or possession utility) by transforming inputs (factors of production — land, labour, capital, organisation) into outputs. The production function expresses the technological relationship between inputs and output: Q = f(L, K, land, organisation). Time periods in production analysis: (a) Market period — supply is fixed; (b) Short run — at least one factor is fixed (usually capital); (c) Long run — all factors are variable.\nLaw of Variable Proportions (Short Run): When one input is varied while others remain fixed, output initially increases at an increasing rate (Stage I — increasing returns), then at a decreasing rate (Stage II — diminishing returns), and finally declines (Stage III — negative returns). Rational producers operate in Stage II only, where total product is still rising but at a diminishing rate. This stage is where marginal product is positive but falling and average product is also falling but remains above marginal product.', 'Law of Returns to Scale (Long Run): When all factors are increased proportionately, output may increase proportionately (constant returns to scale), more than proportionately (increasing returns to scale), or less than proportionately (diminishing returns to scale). Increasing returns arise from indivisibility of capital, specialization, bulk purchasing discounts, and technological advantages. Diminishing returns may result from management difficulties and coordination problems at very large scales.', 'Cost theory distinguishes between accounting costs (explicit, recorded expenses) and economic costs (explicit + implicit/opportunity costs). Total Fixed Cost (TFC) does not vary with output. Total Variable Cost (TVC) varies directly with output. Total Cost (TC) = TFC + TVC. Average Fixed Cost (AFC) = TFC/Q, always declining. Average Variable Cost (AVC) = TVC/Q. Average Cost (AC) = TC/Q = AFC + AVC. Marginal Cost (MC) = ΔTC/ΔQ = ΔTVC/ΔQ.\nThe shapes of cost curves: MC and AC are U-shaped. MC cuts AC and AVC at their respective minimum points. AFC continuously declines and never touches the axes. The vertical distance between AC and AVC equals AFC at every output level. The gap between AC and AVC narrows as output increases because AFC falls. MC intersects AC from below at the minimum of AC (the break-even point in perfect competition).']

7. Market Structures

['Perfect Competition: A market with many buyers and sellers, homogeneous products, free entry and exit, perfect knowledge, and no transportation cost. Firms are price-takers. The demand curve facing an individual firm is perfectly elastic (horizontal) at the market price. AR = MR = Price at all output levels. Profit maximisation occurs where MC = MR (= Price). In the short run, firms may earn super-normal profits, normal profits, or incur losses. In the long run, free entry and exit ensure that firms earn only normal profit (zero economic profit), and AC is minimised — indicating productive efficiency. Allocative efficiency also prevails since P = MC.', 'Monopoly: A single seller with no close substitutes, significant barriers to entry (legal, technical, natural), and the firm is a price-maker. The demand curve facing the monopolist is the market demand curve (downward sloping). Since the monopolist must lower price to sell more, MR < Price (AR) at every output level except the first unit. MR curve lies below the demand curve and is twice as steep for a linear demand curve. Profit maximisation: MC = MR. A monopolist can earn super-normal profits even in the long run due to barriers to entry. Monopoly leads to deadweight loss (allocative inefficiency) because P > MC. Price discrimination (charging different prices to different buyers for the same product) may occur — first-degree (perfect), second-degree (quantity-based), and third-degree (market-segment based).', 'Monopolistic Competition: Many sellers selling differentiated (not homogeneous) products, free entry and exit, and some degree of price-setting power due to product differentiation (branding, quality, location, advertising). In the short run, firms can earn super-normal profits. In the long run, free entry erodes super-normal profits, and firms earn only normal profit. Unlike perfect competition, monopolistic competitors do not produce at minimum AC (excess capacity) and P > MC (deadweight loss). Product differentiation is both a strength (brand loyalty) and a cost (advertising expenditure).', "Oligopoly: A market dominated by a few large firms selling homogeneous or differentiated products, with high barriers to entry. Firms are interdependent — each firm's decisions about price and output affect rivals. Key features: price rigidity (kinked demand curve model), collusion/cartels (explicit or tacit), non-price competition (advertising, branding), and strategic behaviour modelled by game theory. The kinked demand curve explains why prices tend to be sticky: rivals match price cuts but not price rises, creating a discontinuity (kink) in the MR curve and thus a range of MC over which profit-maximising output and price remain unchanged. Cartels like OPEC illustrate explicit collusion to restrict output and raise prices."]

8. National Income Accounting

['National income is the total money value of all final goods and services produced by a country during a given period (usually one year). Key aggregate measures: Gross Domestic Product at Market Price (GDPmp) — total value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during the year. Gross National Product at Market Price (GNPmp) = GDPmp + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA). Net National Product at Market Price (NNPmp) = GNPmp − Depreciation (Consumption of Fixed Capital). NNPmp is essentially National Income (NI) at market prices. To get National Income at Factor Cost: NNPfc = NNPmp − Net Indirect Taxes (Indirect Taxes − Subsidies).', 'Relationships summarised: GDPmp = C + I + G + (X − M) (expenditure approach, where C = consumption, I = gross investment, G = government spending, X = exports, M = imports). GNPmp = GDPmp + NFIA. NNPmp = GNPmp − Depreciation. NNPfc = NNPmp − Net Indirect Taxes. Nominal GDP uses current-year prices; Real GDP uses base-year prices to eliminate the effect of inflation. Real GDP = (Nominal GDP / GDP Deflator) × 100. The GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100.', "Methods of computing national income: (a) Product (Value Added) Method — sum of value added at each stage of production across all industries. Value Added = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption. To avoid double counting, only final goods' value is included, or alternatively, the sum of all value-added. (b) Income Method — sum of all factor incomes earned: Compensation of Employees + Operating Surplus (Rent + Interest + Profit) + Mixed Income of Self-Employed. (c) Expenditure Method — sum of all final expenditures: Private Final Consumption Expenditure + Government Final Consumption Expenditure + Gross Capital Formation (Gross Domestic Capital Formation) + Net Exports (Exports − Imports). All three methods must yield the same result in principle.", 'Important adjustments: Domestic Product at Factor Cost (NDPfc) = GDPmp − Depreciation − Net Indirect Taxes + NFIA = NNPfc. Gross Domestic Capital Formation = Net Addition to Fixed Assets + Change in Stock (inventory). Personal Income = National Income − Corporate Tax − Social Security Contributions − Undistributed Profits + Transfer Income. Personal Disposable Income = Personal Income − Direct Taxes. Per Capita Income = National Income / Total Population. It is a useful but imperfect indicator — it ignores income distribution, non-market transactions, environmental costs, and qualitative aspects of development.']

9. Money, Banking and Credit

['Money serves four primary functions: (a) Medium of Exchange — facilitates buying and selling, eliminating the need for barter. (b) Unit of Account — provides a common measure of value for pricing goods and services. (c) Store of Value — preserves purchasing power over time (though inflation erodes this). (d) Standard of Deferred Payment — enables borrowing and lending by providing a basis for future payments. Characteristics of good money: acceptability, durability, portability, divisibility, uniformity, and stability of value.', 'Types of money: Commodity money (gold, silver), Fiduciary money (paper currency, tokens), Bank money (demand deposits), and Digital/Electronic money. Modern money is predominantly fiat money — its value derives from government decree and public confidence, not intrinsic worth. M1 (narrow money) = Currency with public + Demand deposits with banks. M3 (broad money) = M1 + Net time deposits with banks. Money supply expansion is controlled by the central bank through monetary policy tools: Repo rate (rate at which central bank lends to commercial banks against securities), Reverse repo rate (rate at which central bank borrows from commercial banks), Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR — percentage of deposits banks must keep as reserves with central bank), Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR — percentage of deposits banks must maintain in liquid assets like gold/securities), and Open Market Operations (buying/selling government securities to absorb or inject liquidity).', 'Credit creation by commercial banks: Banks accept deposits and lend out a portion, creating new deposits in the process. The maximum credit created = Initial Deposit × (1 / CRR). This is the money multiplier effect. For example, if CRR = 10% and initial deposit = ₹1,00,000, maximum credit creation = ₹1,00,000 × (1/0.10) = ₹10,00,000. The banking system as a whole expands money supply, but not every bank can independently create the full multiplier because of leakages (cash withdrawals, reserve requirements).', "Functions of the central bank (Reserve Bank of India — RBI): (a) Issuing authority — sole right to issue currency notes (except one-rupee note and coins, issued by the Ministry of Finance). (b) Banker to the government — manages government accounts, advises on financial matters. (c) Banker's bank — holds reserves of commercial banks, acts as lender of last resort. (d) Controller of credit — uses monetary policy tools to regulate money supply. (e) Custodian of foreign exchange reserves. (f) Clearing house — facilitates interbank settlement."]

10. International Trade and Balance of Payments

['International trade arises from differences in resource endowment, climate, technology, and tastes among countries. Absolute advantage (Adam Smith) — a country has an absolute advantage if it can produce a good using fewer resources than another country. Comparative advantage (David Ricardo) — a country should specialise in and export the good for which it has the lower opportunity cost, even if it does not have an absolute advantage in anything. This is the foundation of mutually beneficial trade.', 'Factors influencing trade: relative factor endowments (Heckscher-Ohlin theory — countries export goods that use their abundant factors intensively), economies of scale, product differentiation, transport costs, tariffs and non-tariff barriers, and trading blocs (free trade areas, customs unions, common markets). Tariffs (import taxes) protect domestic industries but raise consumer prices and reduce welfare. Quotas physically limit import quantities. Export subsidies encourage domestic producers to sell abroad at lower prices.', 'Balance of Payments (BoP) is a systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world during a year. Current Account records: (a) invisible trade — exports and imports of services (tourism, banking, insurance, shipping). (b) unilateral transfers — remittances, gifts, donations. (c) trade balance — exports minus imports of visible goods. (d) income account — interest, dividends, profits earned abroad minus similar payments to foreigners. Capital Account records: (a) foreign direct investment (FDI), (b) portfolio investment, (c) external commercial borrowings, (d) reserve assets of the central bank. BoP must always balance: Current Account + Capital Account + Changes in Reserves = 0. A current account deficit must be financed by capital account surplus (borrowing or attracting investment). Devaluation (official lowering of exchange rate) improves competitiveness but raises import costs; revaluation has the opposite effect.']

Important facts

  • Law of demand: inverse relation between price and quantity demanded, ceteris paribus. Exceptions: Giffen goods, Veblen goods, speculation.
  • Price elasticity of demand = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q). Perfectly inelastic Ed = 0; perfectly elastic Ed = ∞.
  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: MU declines as consumption increases, explaining the downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Consumer equilibrium (ordinal): MRSxy = Px/Py at the tangency of the highest possible indifference curve and the budget line.
  • Law of variable proportions (short run): increasing → diminishing → negative returns. Rational producer operates in Stage II only.
  • Law of returns to scale (long run): all inputs changed proportionately — increasing, constant, or decreasing returns.
  • Cost relationships: MC cuts AC and AVC at their minimum points. TC = TFC + TVC. AC = AFC + AVC.
  • Perfect competition: many sellers, homogeneous product, price-takers, P = AR = MR, zero economic profit in long run.
  • Monopoly: single seller, price-maker, barrier to entry, MC = MR for profit maximisation, P > MC creates deadweight loss.
  • Monopolistic competition: differentiated products, free entry, excess capacity in long run, P > MC.
  • Oligopoly: few dominant firms, interdependence, kinked demand curve explains price rigidity, cartels possible.
  • GNPmp = GDPmp + NFIA. NNPfc = NNPmp − Net Indirect Taxes = National Income at factor cost.
  • Three methods of NI computation: Product (value added), Income (factor incomes), Expenditure (C+I+G+X−M).
  • Real GDP removes price effects: Real GDP = (Nominal GDP / GDP Deflator) × 100.
  • Max credit creation by banks = Initial Deposit × (1/CRR). Money multiplier = 1/CRR.
  • RBI monetary policy tools: Repo rate, reverse repo, CRR, SLR, Open Market Operations.
  • Comparative advantage (Ricardo): trade is beneficial even if one country is absolutely less efficient in everything.
  • BoP always balances: Current Account + Capital Account + Reserve Assets change = 0.
  • Price ceiling below equilibrium → shortage; price floor above equilibrium → surplus.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing movement along a demand curve (price change) with a shift of the demand curve (non-price determinant change). Movement = change in quantity demanded; Shift = change in demand.
  • Forgetting that elasticity of demand is negative in sign but usually expressed as absolute value. Noting the sign confusion leads to wrong conclusions about elastic/inelastic.
  • Applying the law of variable proportions (short-run, one variable input) to long-run decisions where all inputs are variable. Confusing returns to a variable factor with returns to scale.
  • Thinking that MC = AC at the minimum of MC. Actually, MC cuts AC at the minimum of AC (and similarly for AVC). MC is itself still rising or falling at that point.
  • Assuming a monopolist always makes profit. A monopolist maximises profit where MC = MR, but if ATC > Price at that output, the monopolist incurs a loss.
  • Treating GDP and GNP as identical. They differ by Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA). Indians earning abroad are included in GNP but not necessarily GDP.
  • Counting intermediate goods in national income. Only final goods' value is counted, or equivalently, only value-added at each stage. Double-counting inflates NI.
  • Believing that a current account deficit is always bad. It can be financed by healthy capital inflows (FDI) that boost productive capacity. Context matters.
  • Thinking that price ceiling always benefits consumers. While some can buy at lower prices, the resulting shortage may leave many consumers unable to buy at all, benefiting black marketers.
  • Assuming perfect competition firms can set price. They are price-takers; the market determines price. Only in monopoly and oligopoly do firms have price-making power.
  • Confusing nominal GDP with real GDP. Nominal GDP can rise simply due to price inflation even if physical output is unchanged. Always adjust for price changes to assess real growth.
  • Forgetting that the budget line slope equals −Px/Py, not the indifference curve slope. At equilibrium, |slope of IC| = |slope of budget line|, i.e., MRS = Px/Py.
