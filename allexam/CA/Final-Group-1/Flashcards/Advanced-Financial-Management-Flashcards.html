Card 1

What is Working Capital? How does it differ from Gross vs. Net Working Capital?

Working Capital refers to the capital used in day-to-day operations. • Gross Working Capital = Total Current Assets • Net Working Capital (NWC) = Current Assets – Current Liabilities For CA Final: NWC = Constant when CA – CL is constant; NWC varies inversely with sales only under % of sales method assumptions.

Card 2

Define Operating Cycle. What factors affect its length?

Operating Cycle = Time between acquisition of raw materials and realisation from debtors (cash conversion cycle). Operating Cycle = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Receivables Outstanding Factors affecting: • Nature of business (manufacturing > trading) • Production cycle length • Credit policy (lenient → longer) • Seasonal variations • Efficiency of collection/debt management

Card 3

What is the EOQ model? State formula and assumptions.

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) minimises total inventory cost (ordering + holding). Formula: EOQ = √(2 × D × S / C) Where: • D = Annual demand (units) • S = Ordering/setup cost per order • C = Carrying/holding cost per unit per year Assumptions: • Demand is known, constant throughout year • No shortages allowed • Lead time is constant • Price per unit is constant • Instantaneous replenishment

Card 4

How do you calculate Reorder Level (ROL), Safety Stock, and Maximum Stock Level?

• ROL = Maximum Usage × Maximum Lead Time • Safety Stock = ROL – (Normal Usage × Normal Lead Time) • Maximum Stock Level = ROL + EOQ – (Minimum Usage × Minimum Lead Time) • Minimum Stock Level = ROL – (Normal Usage × Normal Lead Time) • Average Stock = Minimum + (Maximum – Minimum)/2 • Or = (Min Stock + Max Stock)/2

Card 5

What is Miller-Orr Model for cash management? When is it used?

Used when cash inflows/outflows are uncertain. Model sets Upper Limit (U), Lower Limit (L), and Return Point (Z). Spread = U – L = 3 × ∛(3 × F × σ² / 4 × i) Z = L + Spread/3 U = L + Spread Where: • F = Fixed cost per transaction • σ² = Variance of cash flows • i = Opportunity cost of cash per period When cash hits U → invest (U-Z); when hits L → sell securities (Z-L).

Card 6

Explain Baumol's Model for cash management. Formula and when applicable?

Used when cash outflows are reasonably predictable. Optimal cash balance = √(2 × T × b / i) Where: • T = Total cash required over period • b = Fixed cost per transaction (selling securities) • i = Interest/opportunity cost per Rs. of cash held Total cost at optimum = √(2 × T × b × i) Similarity to EOQ: Baumol treats cash as inventory with ordering (transaction) cost and holding (opportunity) cost.

Card 7

What is the percentage of sales method vs. flexible budgeting approach for working capital estimation?

• % of Sales Method: Assumes CA and CL vary directly with sales. Simple but less accurate for stepped costs. • Flexible Budgeting Method: Estimates each CA/CL item individually based on activity levels. More accurate, accounts for fixed/semi-variable components. For CA Final: Flexible budgeting is preferred when there is a mismatch between variable and fixed components in current assets/liabilities.

Card 8

What is the Z-score model for working capital? When is it recommended?

Developed by Altman. Z = WC/TA + RE/TA + EBIT/TA + MVE/TL + S/TA Where: • WC = Working Capital • TA = Total Assets • RE = Retained Earnings • EBIT = Earnings Before Interest & Tax • MVE = Market Value of Equity • TL = Total Liabilities • S = Sales Interpretation: • Z > 2.99 → Safe zone • 1.81 < Z < 2.99 → Grey zone • Z < 1.81 → Distress zone Recommended for evaluating corporate financial health and liquidity risk.

Card 9

Explain the factors to consider while framing a Credit Policy.

• Credit Period: Length of credit granted (affects sales volume and bad debts) • Credit Standards: Creditworthiness criteria for customers (e.g., 5Cs: Character, Capacity, Capital, Collateral, Conditions) • Collection Policy: Aggressiveness of collection (soft vs. strict) • Discount Policy: Early payment discounts (e.g., 2/10 net 30) • Cost of Financing: Interest cost on receivables tied up • Bad Debt Probability: Expected default rate Trade-off: Liberal policy → higher sales but higher carrying cost; Tight policy → lower risk but lost sales.

Card 10

What is Leasing? Explain Financial Lease vs. Operating Lease.

Leasing = Agreement where owner (lessor) allows another (lessee) to use asset for consideration (rent). • Financial Lease: Covers entire economic life; non-cancellable; all risks/rewards transfer to lessee; resembles hire-purchase. • Operating Lease: Short-term, cancellable, lessor retains ownership risks; maintenance usually by lessor. Tax treatment in India: Lessor claims depreciation; lease payments are revenue expenditure for lessee.

Card 11

Compare Leasing vs. Borrowing for asset acquisition. When is leasing preferable?

| Factor | Leasing | Borrowing | |---|---|---| | Down payment | None (usually) | Required | | Ownership | Stays with lessor | Transfers to lessee | | Depreciation tax benefit | To lessor (reflected in lower lease rent) | To lessee | | Maintenance | In operating lease to lessor | By lessee | | Flexibility | Higher | Less flexible | Leasing preferable when: company has low tax liability (cannot utilise depreciation), needs flexibility, or wants to conserve borrowing capacity.

Card 12

How is the Net Advantage of Leasing (NAL) calculated?

NAL = Cost of Asset – PV of After-Tax Lease Payments – PV of Depreciation Tax Shield – PV of Salvage Value (after-tax) Or equivalently: NAL = V₀ – Σ[Lt × (1-t) × PVIFA(r×(1-t), n)] – [D × t × PVIFA] – S_n×(1-t)×PVIF Where: • V₀ = Cost of asset • Lt = Lease payment at time t • t = Tax rate • r = Post-tax cost of debt • D = Depreciation per year • S_n = Salvage value at end Decision: If NAL > 0 → Lease; if NAL < 0 → Borrow and buy.

Card 13

What are Venture Capital and Private Equity? Differentiate them.

Venture Capital (VC): Investment in early-stage, high-growth-potential startups. High risk, high return. Stage-specific (seed, start-up, expansion). Private Equity (PE): Investment in mature companies taking equity stakes, often with active involvement in management. May involve buyouts, restructuring. Key differences: | VC | PE | |---|---| | Early-stage companies | Mature companies | | Smaller ticket size | Larger ticket size | | Higher risk | Moderate risk | | Focus on growth potential | Focus on value creation/turnaround | | Active in mentoring | Active in strategic decisions | | Exit through IPO/acquisition | Exit through sale/IPO/refinancing

Card 14

What is a Real Option in financial management? List types.

Real options apply option pricing theory to capital budgeting — the right but not obligation to make investment decisions. Types: • Expansion Option: Ability to expand project scale if conditions favourable • Timing Option: Delay investment to gather more information • Abandonment Option: Right to exit/project abandon if unviable • Flexibility Option: Ability to switch inputs/outputs • Growth Option: Option to pursue follow-on investments Valuation uses Black-Scholes or Binomial models applied to real assets.

Card 15

Explain how Real Options change NPV evaluation.

Traditional NPV ignores managerial flexibility. Real-Option Value = NPV (conventional) + Value of Flexibility Options Example: A project with negative conventional NPV may have positive NPV + real option value because the firm can abandon, delay, or expand based on future events. This is critical in R&D, pharma, mining, and infrastructure projects where uncertainty is high.

Card 16

What are the key steps in a Merger valuation using DCF method?

Steps: 1. Forecast free cash flows of target company (5–10 years) 2. Estimate terminal value (perpetuity growth or exit multiple) 3. Determine appropriate discount rate (WACC of combined entity or target's cost of equity) 4. Discount cash flows to present value 5. Adjust for synergies (cost savings, revenue enhancement) 6. Subtract net debt to get equity value 7. Compare with offer price to assess deal fairness Synergy value = V(AB) – [V(A) + V(B)], where V(AB) is value of combined entity.

Card 17

What are the legal requirements for merger under Companies Act, 2013 (Sections 230-232)?

• Section 230: Compromise/Arrangement – notice to members/creditors, convening of class meetings • Section 231: Company amalgamation with related companies • Section 232: Merger/amalgamation in public interest (National Company Law Tribunal order) • Section 233: Fresh issue of shares/securities by transferee company • Approval required from: Shareholders (special resolution), Debenture holders, NCLT • Notice to Income Tax Authority (Section 230(5)) • SEBI regulations applicable if listed companies involved

Card 18

What is Consideration Transfer Value (CTV) for tax purposes in amalgamation?

CTV is the value of shares/securities/bonds debentures issued by the resulting company to shareholders of the target, in exchange for their shares. For tax exemption u/s 47 (viia)/(vii) of Income Tax Act: • Non-convertible preference shares not counted in CTV • Convertible preference shares: only non-convertible portion excluded • CTV determines whether shareholders get capital gains exemption If CTV ≤ book value of net assets transferred → exemption available under Section 47(viia).

Card 19

What is Securitisation? Explain the process and participants.

Securitisation = Converting illiquid assets (like loans, receivables) into marketable securities. Process: 1. Originator (bank/NBFC) identifies pool of assets 2. Transfers assets to Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) 3. SPV issues securities backed by those assets 4. Credit rating agencies rate the securities 5. Investors purchase the securities 6. Cash flows from underlying assets service the securities Participants: • Originator, SPV, Rating Agencies, Investors, Servicer, Trustee, Underwriter

Card 20

What is Net Asset Value (NAV) for an AIM (Alternative Investment Fund)?

NAV = (Total Assets – Total Liabilities) / Number of Units Outstanding For AIFs: • Calculated periodically as per SEBI (AIF) Regulations • Category I & II AIFs: NAV calculated weekly/monthly depending on category • Category III AIFs: NAV typically daily • Valuation principles: Fair value as per Accounting Standards • Independent valuer required for certain categories SEBI mandates: NAV shall be computed by the fund manager and verified by the custodian.

Card 21

What is Earnings Management? Name common techniques.

Earnings Management = Intentional intervention in financial reporting to alter earnings and mislead stakeholders or influence contractual outcomes. Common techniques: • Revenue recognition manipulation (channel stuffing, bill-and-hold) • Cookie jar reserves (over-reserving in good years, releasing in bad) • Channel stuffing at quarter-end • Changing depreciation methods • Recognising unrealised gains • Related-party transactions • Classification shifting (one-time items disguised as recurring) Regulatory response: Sarbanes-Oxley, tighter audit requirements, ICAI guidance.

Card 22

What is Transfer Pricing? Define Arm's Length Price (ALP).

Transfer Pricing = Pricing of goods/services/intangibles between associated enterprises (AEs) across borders. Arm's Length Price (ALP) = Price that would have been charged between independent enterprises under comparable circumstances. Methods to determine ALP (Income Tax Act, Section 92C): • CUP (Comparable Uncontrolled Price) • Resale Price Method (RPM) • Cost Plus Method (CPM) • Profit Split Method (PSM) • TNMM (Transactional Net Margin Method) Penalty for transfer pricing adjustment: 150% of tax sought to be evaded if ALP not reported correctly.

Card 23

How is Hedge Ratio and Cross Hedge calculated? When is cross hedge used?

Hedge Ratio (h*) = ρ × (σ_S / σ_F) Where: • ρ = correlation between spot and futures price changes • σ_S = standard deviation of spot price changes • σ_F = standard deviation of futures price changes Cross Hedge: Used when no direct futures contract exists for the underlying asset. Use a related/futures contract whose price movements are highly correlated. Example: Hedging EUR exposure using USD futures if EUR futures unavailable, due to high EUR-USD correlation.

Card 24

What is the MVA (Market Value Added) and EVA (Economic Value Added)?

MVA = Market Value of Firm – Book Value of Capital Invested Reflects market's assessment of value creation beyond accounting profit. EVA = NOPAT – (Capital Employed × WACC) Where: • NOPAT = Net Operating Profit After Tax • Capital Employed × WACC = Economic cost of capital EVA > 0 → Value creation; EVA < 0 → Value destruction. Both measures adjust for cost of equity (unlike accounting profit).

Card 25

What is the difference between Horizontal and Vertical Merger? Give examples.

• Horizontal Merger: Between firms in same industry at same stage of production. Example: Two banks merging. Effect: Increases market share, reduces competition. • Vertical Merger: Between firms at different stages of production chain. Example: Manufacturer merging with distributor. Effect: Improves supply chain efficiency, reduces transaction costs. • Conglomerate Merger: Between unrelated businesses. Further classified as pure or mixed.

Card 26

Explain SEBI's guidelines for Takeover Code (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers Regulations, 2011).

Key provisions: • Threshold: 25% voting rights triggers mandatory open offer • Open Offer: Acquirer must make public offer to public shareholders at ECPL (Equity Capital raised at pre-open offer price) • ECPL = highest price paid in last 26 weeks OR average weighted average price of shares bought in last 26 weeks • Squeeze-out: At 90%+ shareholding, acquirer can compulsorily acquire remaining • Slip-out: Minority shareholders can exit at open offer price • Exemptions available for certain acquisitions (qualified institutional buyers, etc.)

Card 27

What is the concept of 'Book Value' vs. 'Replacement Value' in merger valuation?

• Book Value: Based on historical cost as per financial statements. Easy to compute but may not reflect true economic value. • Replacement Value: Cost to replace assets at current prices. More realistic for asset-based valuation. In merger context: • Asset-based approach uses replacement value for better decision-making • For goodwill calculation: Goodwill = Purchase Consideration – Net Assets (at book/replacement value) • ICAI guidance note on amalgamations recommends considering fair values of identifiable assets

Card 28

What is the 'Going Concern' assumption in valuation and its exceptions?

Going Concern = Assumption that enterprise will continue operations indefinitely. When going concern is NOT appropriate: • Company is being liquidated • Management intends to cease operations • Legal/regulatory orders mandate closure • Severe financial distress with no recovery plan Impact on valuation: • Going concern → DCF/earnings-based valuation • Non-going concern → Liquidation value (net realisable value of assets minus liabilities) Significance for auditors and valuers: Disclosure requirement if material uncertainty exists about going concern.

Card 29

What are the conditions for tax-free amalgamation under Section 47(viia) of Income Tax Act?

Conditions for tax-free amalgamation: 1. Amalgamation of company with another company (not amalgamation in control) 2. All property and liabilities of transferor company become property/liabilities of transferee company 3. Shareholders receiving shares in transferee company hold at least 3/4th in value of shares of transferor company 4. The amalgamation is registered with Registrar of Companies 5. Transferee company is an Indian company Result: No capital gains tax on transfer of assets/shares by transferor company or its shareholders.

Card 30

Explain SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations regarding rights issue pricing.

• Rights issue price cannot be lower than average market price of last 10 working days preceding date of notification, or face value (whichever is higher) • Equity shares included in benchmark index on recording date eligible for rights issue at market-determined price • Pro-rata basis preferred; minimum 15% of expanded capital underwritten • Time limit: Issue must be completed within 30 days of finalization (extendable) • Preferential allotment and rights issue have separate pricing regimes

Card 31

What is the difference between Factoring and Forfeiting?

| Aspect | Factoring | Forfeiting | |---|---|---| | Type | Trade receivables financing | Export receivables financing | | Security | Current asset | Medium/long-term | | Recourse | Recourse or non-recourse | Non-recourse | | Term | Short-term (days/months) | Medium to long-term | | Focus | Domestic trade | International trade | | Discount | Small | Significant (covers risk premium) | • Factoring: Factor buys receivables, collects from debtors, charges commission • Forfeiting: Exporter sells receivables at discount, factor bears credit/political risk

Card 32

What is a Special Purpose Acquiring Vehicle (SPAV)? When is it used?

SPAV is a company created specifically to acquire controlling stake in another company. Used in: • Takeover bids to avoid direct disclosure requirements • Holding structure before amalgamation • Facilitating financing of acquisition Regulatory view: SEBI and Companies Act regulate sponsor disclosures and related party transactions through SPVs to prevent obscuring true ownership and control.

Card 33

What is Financial Restructuring? Differentiate internal vs. external restructuring.

Financial Restructuring = Reorganisation of capital structure, debt, assets, or operations to improve financial health. • Internal Restructuring: Within the company — debt conversion, equity infusion, asset rationalisation, share buyback, merger of divisions. • External Restructuring: Involves outside parties — acquisition, de-merger, joint venture, sale of business units, strategic disinvestment. Triggers: Financial distress, strategic shift, regulatory change, M&A opportunity, excess debt burden.

Card 34

How is Share Buyback valued and what are its implications for EPS and control?

Buyback = Company repurchases its own shares from shareholders. Valuation approaches: • Market price method: Buyback at premium to market price (regulated by SEBI) • DCF method: Intrinsic value of shares • PE multiple method Effects: • EPS: Increases (fewer shares outstanding) • Control: Concentrates ownership in remaining shareholders • Signalling: May signal undervaluation • Tax: Tax-efficient compared to dividend in some jurisdictions • SEBI limits: Buyback ≤ 25% of paid-up capital in a financial year

Card 35

What is the 'Control Premium' in takeovers? How is it estimated?

Control Premium = Extra amount paid over market price to acquire controlling stake. Estimation methods: • Precedent transactions: Compare acquirer's premium over target's pre-bid price • Block trade method: Compare control block price vs. open market price • DCF with control assumptions: Value under new management vs. status quo Typical range: 20–50% above market price in India. Relevance: Determines maximum bid price in takeover situations.

Card 36

What are the key features of a Public Financial Institution (PFIs) like SIDBI, EXIM Bank, NHB?

• SIDBI: Refinance to NBFCs, promote, finance and develop micro, small and medium enterprises • EXIM Bank: Finance, promote and facilitate India's international trade • NHB: Promote housing finance companies, refinance housing loans • IDBI: Earlier focused on industrial finance; now commercial bank Functions common to PFIs: • Long-term project financing • Refinancing institutions • Technical and financial assistance • Development of capital markets • Policy advisory roles

Card 37

What is the 'Weighted Average Cost of Capital' (WACC) and its role in project appraisal?

WACC = (E/V × Re) + (D/V × Rd × (1-t)) Where: • E = Market value of equity • D = Market value of debt • V = E + D • Re = Cost of equity • Rd = Cost of debt • t = Corporate tax rate Role in appraisal: • Discount rate for unlevered free cash flows • Hurdle rate for project acceptance (NPV > 0) • Basis for EVA calculation • Used in DCF valuation of firms/projects • Segment-specific WACC may differ from corporate WACC

Card 38

What is the 'Cash Conversion Cycle' (CCC)? Formula and significance?

CCC = Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO) + Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) – Days Payable Outstanding (DPO) DIO = Average Inventory / COGS × 365 DSO = Average Receivables / Credit Sales × 365 DPO = Average Payables / Purchases × 365 Significance: • Measures efficiency of working capital management • Shorter CCC = faster cash conversion = better liquidity • Negative CCC = company collects from customers before paying suppliers (e.g., retail giants) • Key metric for operational performance comparison

Card 39

Explain the 'Pecking Order Theory' of capital structure.

Theory by Myers and Majlof: Firms prefer financing in this order: 1. Internal funds (retained earnings) — no information asymmetry cost 2. Debt — cheaper than equity, signals confidence 3. Equity — last resort, signals management believes shares overvalued Reason: Information asymmetry between managers and investors makes equity issuance costly (adverse selection). Implication: Profitable firms use less debt (enough internal funds); distressed firms forced to issue equity (bad signal).

Card 40

What is 'Negative Goodwill' in a merger? When does it arise?

Negative Goodwill = Purchase Consideration < Fair Value of Net Identifiable Assets Acquired Also called 'Gain on Bargain Purchase'. Arises when: • Target company is distressed/selling urgently • Acquirer gets assets well below market value • Synergies expected are minimal • Regulatory/compliance issues depress target value Accounting treatment: Recognised immediately in profit and loss account (Ind AS 103). Not amortised.

Card 41

What is 'Share Swap Ratio' in a merger? How is it determined?

Share Swap Ratio = Number of shares issued by acquiring company for each share of target company. Determinants: • Relative valuation (P/E multiples, NAV multiples) • Negotiated premium/discount • Tax considerations for shareholders • Dilution effect on EPS • Market perception Formula: Swap Ratio = (Price per share of Target × Agreed Premium) / Price per share of Acquirer Example: If Target price ₹100 with 20% premium → ₹120; Acquirer price ₹200 → Swap ratio = 120/200 = 0.6
