1. Introduction to Auditing Standards and Quality Control

['The Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 and the Rules thereunder empower the Institute to prescribe professional standards for its members. Under Section 22B read with Section 44A of the Act, the Council may frame regulations specifying the conduct and practices to be followed by members in their professional work. The Standards on Auditing (SAs) are issued by the Auditing and Ethics Board (AEB) and adopted by the Council under Section 22B(1)(a). Every member must comply with these standards; departure is permitted only when a law or regulation specifically permits or requires it, and in such cases the member must disclose the departure in the auditor’s report.', 'SA 200, ‘Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing’, sets out the fundamental objectives: (i) to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework; and (ii) to report on the financial statements and communicate as required by the SAs. Reasonable assurance is a high level of assurance but is not absolute assurance because of inherent limitations such as the use of testing, the nature of evidence being largely persuasive rather than conclusive, the possibility of fraud involving collusion or concealment, and the exercise of auditor judgment.', 'Key principles underlying an audit include professional skepticism, professional judgment, integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, and professional behavior. Professional skepticism requires an attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to fraud or error, and a critical assessment of audit evidence. It applies throughout the audit, particularly in questioning contradictory evidence, evaluating the competence and credibility of sources, and considering the possibility of management override of controls.', 'Quality control is governed by SQC 1, ‘Quality Control for Firms that Perform Audits and Reviews of Financial Statements, and Other Assurance and Related Services Engagements’. The firm must establish a system of quality control including policies and procedures addressing seven elements: leadership responsibilities for quality within the firm, ethical requirements, client acceptance and continuance, human resources, engagement performance, monitoring, and resources. Engagement performance encompasses direction, supervision, and review. The engagement partner is responsible for the quality of the firm’s engagements in accordance with professional standards and applicable legal and regulatory requirements, and for concluding that appropriate consultation has taken place on difficult or contentious matters.', 'The difference between internal quality control of the firm and engagement-level quality control under SA 220 is critical. SQC 1 operates at the firm level, covering all engagements undertaken by the firm. SA 220, ‘Quality Control for an Audit of Financial Statements’, prescribes the engagement partner’s responsibilities regarding quality control procedures for the conduct of the engagement. The engagement quality control review (EQCR) is mandatory for listed entities and may be performed for other engagements if required by the firm’s policies. EQCR involves an objective evaluation of significant judgments made and conclusions reached by the engagement team, performed by a qualified reviewer who is not part of the engagement team.']

2. Risk Assessment and Internal Control (SA 315 & SA 330)

['SA 315, ‘Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement through Understanding the Entity and Its Environment’, requires the auditor to identify and assess risks of material misstatement at two levels: (a) the level of individual assertions for classes of transactions, account balances, and disclosures; and (b) the overall financial statement level. The understanding is developed in three domains: (i) relevant industry, regulatory, and other external factors; (ii) the nature of the entity, including its operations, ownership, governance, financing, and performance; and (iii) the entity’s selection and application of accounting policies, its objectives and strategies, and its internal control system.', 'Internal control is defined as a process designed, implemented, and maintained by those charged with governance, management, and other personnel to provide reasonable assurance about the achievement of objectives relating to the reliability of financial reporting, effectiveness and efficiency of operations, and compliance with applicable laws and regulations. The five components of internal control are: (1) Control environment — sets the tone of the organization, influencing the control consciousness of its people; (2) Risk assessment — the process identifying and assessing risks to achieving objectives; (3) Control activities — policies and procedures that help ensure management directives are carried out; (4) Information and communication — systems supporting the identification, capture, and exchange of information; (5) Monitoring activities — ongoing or periodic evaluations of the quality of internal control performance.', 'Under SA 330, ‘The Auditor’s Responses to Assessed Risks’, the auditor shall design and implement appropriate responses to address the assessed risks of material misstatement. For risks at the financial statement level, the auditor considers overall responses such as emphasizing the importance of professional skepticism, assigning more experienced staff or specialists, providing more supervision, and selecting procedures that incorporate more unpredictability. For risks at the assertion level, the auditor designs further audit procedures whose nature, timing, and extent are responsive to the assessed risks. Nature refers to the type of procedure (inspection, observation, inquiry, confirmation, recalculation, reperformance, or analytical procedure); timing refers to when the procedure is performed (period end or interim); and extent refers to the quantity of testing.', 'The concept of significant risk is central. A significant risk is an identified risk of material misstatement that, in the auditor’s judgment, requires special audit consideration. Factors indicating a significant risk include: degree of subjectivity, complexity, unusual transactions, significant related party transactions, large-volume transactions processed routinely by IT systems with general controls deficiencies, susceptibility to management override, and risks arising from significant events or transactions outside the normal course of business. For significant risks, the auditor shall obtain an understanding of the related controls, including those relevant to the risk, and test their operating effectiveness unless the auditor plans to rely on those controls or substantive procedures alone are insufficient.', 'Audit evidence obtained in previous audits may be used as audit evidence for the control operating effectively, but the auditor must determine its relevance. For significant risks, if the auditor relies on controls, they must be tested in the current period; reliance on prior evidence alone is not permitted. For other risks, the auditor may use evidence from prior periods if combined with tests of changes in controls and obtained at least once in every audit. The auditor must also consider the effect of IT general controls on the ability to rely on automated controls, especially when processing volume is high and manual intervention is minimal.']

3. Audit Evidence (SA 500)

['SA 500, ‘Audit Evidence’, establishes that audit evidence is necessary to support the auditor’s opinion and includes both information contained in the accounting records and other information used in the preparation of the financial statements. The sufficiency of audit evidence is the measure of quantity, and appropriateness is the measure of quality, comprising relevance and reliability. Both sufficiency and appropriateness are interrelated; more evidence may be needed if its quality is lower. The auditor should obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level, but sufficiency is not merely a cumulative function of quantity — poor quality evidence cannot be compensated by obtaining more of it.', 'Procedures for obtaining audit evidence include inspection (of records or assets), observation, inspection together with recalculation, external confirmation, recalculation, reperformance, analytical procedures, and inquiry. External confirmations are generally considered highly reliable because they come from independent third parties, though the auditor must evaluate the credibility and objectivity of the respondent. Confirmation requests should be under the auditor’s control to prevent tampering, and negative confirmations provide less persuasive evidence than positive confirmations and should only be used when individual account balances are small, the risk of material misstatement is assessed as low, and a large number of small balances are involved.', 'The reliability of audit evidence depends on several factors: source independence (external sources are more reliable than internal), nature (original documents more reliable than copies), effectiveness of internal control over its production, directness (auditor’s direct knowledge via observation is most reliable), and expertise (evidence from experts is more reliable when the expert is competent and objective). However, reliability is not absolute — even external confirmations can be unreliable if the respondent is biased, lacks knowledge, or is under the influence of the entity.', 'When audit evidence obtained from one source is inconsistent with evidence from another, or when evidence contradicts expectations, the auditor shall investigate the inconsistency and modify the planned audit procedures accordingly. In some cases, additional evidence may be insufficient to resolve the inconsistency. The auditor may also obtain corroborative evidence from other sources, use professional judgment regarding the reliability of specific items, or reconsider the reliability of management representations. If inconsistencies remain unresolved and may affect the opinion, the auditor should discuss with those charged with governance and consider the effect on the auditor’s report.', 'Specific application areas require particular attention: revenue recognition (assessing cut-off, existence, completeness, and accuracy, especially for complex or bundled contracts), estimates (evaluating assumptions, methods, and data, and reviewing subsequent events), provisions and contingencies (assessing adequacy of disclosure and measurement), impairment testing (validating models and assumptions), and tax provisions (reviewing positions taken, uncertain tax treatments, and disputes with tax authorities). In each area, the auditor must combine multiple procedures to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence.']

4. Use of Service Organizations (SA 402)

['SA 402, ‘Auditor’s Considerations and, when Applicable, Reports on Subservice Organizations’, applies when an entity uses service organizations to carry out certain transactions. The auditor must understand the nature and significance of the services provided by the service organization and their effect on internal control relevant to the audit. Service organizations may provide services such as payroll processing, accounts receivable management, data center services, and transaction processing. These services may be embedded in the entity’s accounting information system, making them integral to financial reporting.', 'The auditor obtains understanding through inquiry of management, inspection of documentation provided by the service organization, and review of service auditor’s reports. Two types of service auditor’s reports are commonly encountered: Type 1 and Type 2. Type 1 report describes the service organization’s system and states whether the description is fairly presented and whether controls were suitably designed to meet specified criteria as of a point in time. Type 2 report includes the same description and design assessment but also covers the operating effectiveness of the controls over a specified period. Type 2 provides more comprehensive evidence and is therefore more useful to the user auditor.', 'If the service auditor’s report is unavailable, the user auditor may request the service organization to perform specific procedures, visit the service organization to observe controls, or engage a service auditor on behalf of the user auditor. When relying on controls operated by the service organization, the user auditor must test the operating effectiveness of those controls or verify that the service auditor has tested them and obtained sufficient evidence of operating effectiveness. The user auditor’s report must not refer to the service auditor’s report unless the user auditor assumes responsibility for the service auditor’s work; if responsibility is assumed, a clear indication must be given in the report.', 'Cybersecurity and cloud computing present modern challenges under SA 402. Data hosted in the cloud introduces risks related to access controls, encryption, availability, and data integrity. The auditor should evaluate the service organization’s security certifications (e.g., ISO 27001, SOC 2), understand the contractual rights regarding audit access, and consider the need for additional procedures when data is transmitted across borders subject to different legal regimes. Penetration testing results, vulnerability assessments, and incident response plans may be relevant indicators of control effectiveness.']

5. Audit Sampling (SA 530)

['SA 530, ‘Audit Sampling’, defines sampling risk as the risk that the auditor’s conclusion based on a sample may be different from the conclusion if the entire population was subject to the same auditing procedure. Sampling risk can lead to two types of errors: (a) for tests of controls, either the auditor assesses control risk too high (inefficiency) or too low (ineffectiveness); (b) for substantive procedures, either the auditor concludes that a material misstatement exists when it does not (incorrect rejection) or that no material misstatement exists when it does (incorrect acceptance). Nonsampling risk arises from reasons unrelated to the size of the sample, such as inappropriate procedure selection, misinterpretation of evidence, or oversight by the auditor.', 'Sampling approaches are classified as non-statistical (judgmental) sampling and statistical sampling. Statistical sampling involves random selection and the quantitative measurement of sampling risk using probability theory. Non-statistical sampling relies on the auditor’s judgment for selection and evaluation. Both approaches can provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence if properly designed and evaluated. The choice depends on factors such as cost-effectiveness, the auditor’s experience, and the availability of statistical tools. Under the new ICAI curriculum, non-statistical sampling remains widely used in practice due to practical constraints, but familiarity with basic statistical concepts is expected at the final level.', 'For tests of controls, sample size is influenced by the expected population deviation rate, tolerable deviation rate, allowable risk of assessing control risk too low, and population size. In attributes sampling, the auditor evaluates the sample by comparing the observed deviation rate against the tolerable rate adjusted for the risk of assessing control risk too low. If the upper deviation limit exceeds the tolerable rate, the sample does not support the planned reliance on the control. For substantive testing of details, variables sampling techniques such as monetary unit sampling (MUS) and classical variables sampling are used. MUS is particularly suited for overstatement detection in populations where individual items vary widely in value.', 'Misstatements found in a sample must be projected to the population. The auditor distinguishes between factual misstatements (those with certainty, such as incorrect calculations), judgmental misstatements (differences in professional judgment), and projected misstatements (estimates of total misstatements based on sample results). If projected misstatements approach or exceed materiality, the auditor should request management to investigate and adjust the financial statements. If management refuses, the auditor should consider the effect on the opinion. Tolerable misstatement is set at or below materiality for the financial statements as a whole, often allocated to specific assertions or classes of transactions.']

6. Analytical Procedures (SA 520)

['SA 520, ‘Analytical Procedures’, requires the auditor to apply analytical procedures as part of the overall strategy, during the audit as substantive procedures, and at or near the end of the audit as a final review. Analytical procedures consist of evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data, and investigation of identified fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information or differ from expected values. They are used to assist in understanding the entity’s business, identifying risks of material misstatement, designing the nature, timing, and extent of other audit procedures, and providing overall reassurance at the conclusion stage.', 'The objectives of analytical procedures at different stages vary. During the planning stage, they help identify unusual transactions, events, or amounts that may indicate risks of material misstatement. At the substantive stage, they may provide evidence if the information is sufficiently reliable and precise expectations can be developed. At the review stage, they serve as a final examination to determine whether the financial statements are consistent with the auditor’s understanding of the entity. Analytical procedures are generally more effective when applied to aggregates of data representing relationships that are reasonably predictable, such as gross margin percentages, salary expense relative to headcount, or depreciation to fixed assets.', 'Factors affecting the reliability of an analytical procedure include the source and comparability of information, the nature and relevance of the information, and the level of precision achievable. The precision threshold determines whether the procedure can detect a material misstatement. Sources include internal data (auditor’s own expectation derived from prior periods or budgets) and external data (industry benchmarks, economic statistics). Comparability requires adjustment for changes in the entity’s operations, accounting policies, or the economy. If discrepancies between expected and actual values exceed performance materiality, the auditor must investigate by inquiry, inspection, or additional analytical procedures.', 'Common applications in CA Final include ratio analysis for going concern assessment, segment profitability analysis, revenue recognition trend analysis, inventory turnover comparisons, and overhead absorption rate verification. Analytical procedures are also used in forensic auditing contexts, such as Benford’s Law analysis for detecting manipulated figures, and in business valuation reviews. At the final review stage, the auditor assesses whether unusual relationships have been investigated and resolved, and whether any residual risks remain unaddressed.']

7. Audit Documentation (SA 230)

['SA 230, ‘Audit Documentation’, requires the auditor to prepare audit documentation that provides a sufficient and appropriate record of the basis for the auditor’s report and evidence that the audit was planned and performed in accordance with SAs and applicable legal and regulatory requirements. The form, content, and extent of documentation depend on professional judgment, the auditor’s experience, the nature of the audit procedures, the identified risks, and the significance of the evidence obtained. Documentation includes audit programs, analytical reviews, summaries of significant findings or issues, letters of representation, and memoranda explaining the resolution of significant matters.', 'Key documents required are: (a) the overall audit strategy and audit plan; (b) issued financial statements and management’s report thereon; (c) minutes of meetings of owners and those charged with governance; (d) correspondence with regulators and insurers; (e) internal auditor reports; (f) risk assessment documentation including flowcharts and narratives; (g) summary of audit adjustments; (h) management representation letter; and (i) engagement letter. The auditor should also document discussions of significant matters with management, those charged with governance, and internal auditors, along with the nature, timing, and extent of procedures performed, the results thereof, and conclusions reached.', 'Assembly of the final audit file is required within 60 days of the auditor’s report date. After this date, the auditor should not delete or discard audit documentation before the end of its retention period, which under the Companies Act, 2013 is 8 years from the date of conclusion of the audit for companies, and as prescribed under professional rules. Temporary file completion involves consolidating documentation, deleting superseded drafts, and ensuring completeness and consistency. Changes to documentation after the assembly date are permitted only for administrative reasons, must be recorded with the date, reason, and names of persons making and reviewing the changes, and must not be made to change substantive judgments.', 'Confidentiality and security of audit documentation are paramount. The auditor must take appropriate measures to protect the integrity and confidentiality of documentation, especially in electronic form. Access should be restricted to authorized personnel. While clients have rights to access audit documentation, the auditor may impose restrictions if disclosure could compromise the audit process or reveal proprietary information. Third-party requests for documentation should be handled carefully, typically requiring legal advice or client consent before release.']

8. Fraud, Laws and Non-Compliance (SA 240 & SA 250)

['SA 240, ‘The Auditor’s Responsibilities Relating to Fraud in an Audit of Financial Statements’, requires the auditor to identify and assess the risks of material misstatement due to fraud, obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding those assessed risks, and respond to the risks of fraud at the financial statement and assertion levels. Fraud is an intentional act by one or more individuals among management, those charged with governance, employees, or third parties, involving the use of deception to obtain an unjust or illegal advantage. Two types are distinguished: misappropriation of assets (theft of entity assets) and fraudulent financial reporting (intentional misrepresentation in financial statements). Fraud is inherently concealed and difficult to detect, especially when it involves collusion, forgery, deliberate omission, or management override of controls.', 'The fraud triangle comprises three factors: (1) Incentive/pressure to commit fraud — financial stability threatened, excess compensation targets, personal financial obligations; (2) Opportunity — weak internal control, complex organizational structure, significant related party transactions, management override ability; (3) Attitude/rationalization — ethical culture lacking, arrogance of management, disregard for existing controls. At the planning stage, the auditor holds required discussions among engagement team members about the susceptibility of the financial statements to material fraud, maintaining professional skepticism throughout. Brainstorming sessions should consider how and where the financial statements might be susceptible to material misstatement due to fraud.', 'SA 250, ‘Consideration of Laws and Regulations in an Audit of Financial Statements’, distinguishes between (a) provisions of a legal framework directly determining the recognized amount and disclosures in financial statements (such as tax laws, company law provisions on dividends and reserves), and (b) other laws and regulations that do not directly affect the financial statements but compliance with which is fundamental to the entity’s operations, license, or continuing existence (such as environmental laws, securities regulations, labor laws). The auditor is responsible for obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding compliance with the first category, but only for the second category, the auditor performs specific audit procedures limited to inspection of correspondence with authorities, reading minutes, and inquiries.', 'When the auditor becomes aware of information concerning an actual or suspected fraud or non-compliance, the auditor should communicate with those charged with governance promptly, unless the suspected fraud involves those charged with governance, in which case communication should be to an higher authority within the entity or externally if required by law. If the financial effects are material and not adequately reflected, the auditor should express a qualified or adverse opinion. If the scope of audit is restricted due to concealment by management, the auditor should disclaim an opinion. Under the Companies Act, 2013, Section 143(12) mandates reporting of frauds to the Central Government in Form ADT-4 within 60 days, along with notes on action taken or proposed to be taken.']

9. Going Concern (SA 570)

['SA 570, ‘Going Concern’, requires the auditor to evaluate management’s assessment of the entity’s ability to continue as a going concern. Management is responsible for preparing the financial statements on a going concern basis unless management intends to liquidate the entity or cease trading. The auditor shall obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding the appropriateness of management’s use of the going concern assumption. If events or conditions exist that may cast significant doubt on the entity’s ability to continue as a going concern, the auditor shall determine whether the financial statements adequately disclose these events or conditions and whether the going concern assumption is appropriate. The evaluation period extends at least 12 months from the reporting date, or longer if required by the applicable financial reporting framework.', 'Indicators of potential going concern problems include: recurring operating losses, negative cash flows from operations, adverse key financial ratios, defaults on loans or overdrafts, arrears of dividends, inability to pay creditors, denial of normal trade credit, non-compliance with statutory requirements, and dependence on successful completion of a major project or refinance. The auditor should perform inquiries of management regarding known events beyond the评估 period, assess future plans and mitigation strategies, consider the credibility of past projections, and review post-period events. If mitigation actions are significant, the auditor evaluates whether they are practicable and likely to be effective.', 'If the auditor concludes that material uncertainty exists, the auditor’s report should include an emphasis of matter paragraph drawing attention to the disclosures in the financial statements. The opinion remains unmodified if disclosures are adequate. If disclosures are inadequate, a qualified or adverse opinion is issued. If management refuses to make or extend its assessment, the auditor should request management to do so; if still refused, the auditor should express a disclaimer of opinion or qualify it, depending on circumstances. In cases where the entity is not a going concern but continues to prepare statements on that basis, the auditor should express an adverse opinion.', 'For listed entities and entities required to prepare Ind AS, specific disclosure requirements exist under Schedule III and Accounting Standard 30 (now AS 30 replaced by Ind AS 1 par. 125-141). The auditor must consider whether management’s assessment has considered all available information about the future, whether plans are realistic, and whether disclosure of uncertainties is sufficiently explicit. Additional considerations apply when the entity has recently emerged from financial difficulties, is subject to regulatory sanctions, or relies on government support.']

10. Related Parties (SA 550)

['SA 550, ‘Related Party Disclosures’, establishes standards and provides guidance on the auditor’s responsibilities relating to related parties in an audit of financial statements. A related party is a person or entity that is related to the reporting entity. Under Ind AS 24 / AS 18, related parties include parents, subsidiaries, fellow subsidiaries, associates, joint ventures, key management personnel, and their close family members, and entities controlled or significantly influenced by such persons. The definition extends to trusts, pension funds, and management companies for post-employment benefit plans, and entities for which investment manager control exists.', 'The auditor’s objectives are to (a) obtain an understanding of related party relationships and transactions sufficient to recognize fraud or error resulting from them; (b) respond to the risks of material misstatement associated with related party relationships and transactions; and (c) ensure adequate presentation and disclosure in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework. Identified risks of material misstatement from related parties often arise from the inability or unwillingness of management to identify or disclose all related party relationships and transactions, from complex structures designed to obscure related party arrangements, or from the commercial rationale behind transactions with related parties.', 'Required audit procedures include: (1) making inquiries of management and those charged with governance about the identity of related parties and the nature of relationships and transactions; (2) reviewing shareholder registers, register of directors and key managerial personnel, and minute books; (3) reviewing conflict of interest declarations; (4) inspecting significant contracts and agreements for indications of related party relationships; (5) performing analytical procedures on unusual transactions; and (6) reviewing banking and legal correspondence for guarantees or contingent liabilities. When related party transactions are conducted on terms equivalent to arm’s length, the auditor should verify the basis for such conclusion, typically requiring independent pricing evidence, board approvals, or market comparisons.', 'Disclosure requirements under Ind AS 24 include the name of the related party, description of the relationship, nature of transactions, amounts involved, outstanding balances and terms, provisions for doubtful debts, and commitments. If related party transactions are not conducted on arm’s length terms and are material, the auditor should consider whether the terms are fairly disclosed and whether additional provisions or reclassifications are required. Independence implications also arise: if the auditor’s firm has business dealings with related parties of the audit client, safeguards must be evaluated.']

11. Management Representations (SA 580)

['SA 580, ‘Written Representations’, requires the auditor to seek written representations from management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance. Written representations are a necessary supplement to other audit evidence but do not provide sufficient audit evidence on their own. They serve to confirm certain matters or to support other audit evidence. Management is required to acknowledge its responsibility for the fair preparation of financial statements, including the adequacy of disclosures, and for the completeness of information provided to the auditor. Oral representations are insufficient and must be corroborated.', 'Required written representations include: (a) management’s acknowledgment of its responsibility for the preparation of the financial statements in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework; (b) management’s belief that the financial statements fairly present, in all material respects, the required disclosures and are so prepared; (c) completeness of information provided to the auditor; (d) recognition, measurement, and disclosure of items requiring significant judgment; (e) identification of all related parties and related party transactions; (f) knowledge of fraud or suspected fraud affecting the entity; (g) disclosure of uncorrected misstatements; (h) legality and regularity of operations; (i) plans or intentions that may affect the carrying value of assets or settlement of liabilities; and (j) frequency of events occurring after the reporting period.', 'If management does not provide the requested written representations, the auditor should discuss the matter with management, determine the reasons, and take appropriate action. Failure to provide representations may indicate integrity issues or limitation on the scope of the audit. The auditor should consider the implications for the opinion and, if necessary, disclaim an opinion or withdraw from the engagement. If there is a limitation imposed by management on the auditor’s ability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence, the auditor should qualify the opinion or disclaim an opinion, depending on the pervasiveness of the possible effects.', 'Conflicts between management representations and other audit evidence require the auditor to investigate the inconsistency. If the conflict cannot be resolved, the auditor should reconsider the reliability of management representations in general and modify the opinion accordingly. Representations should be dated as of the date of the auditor’s report. Prior to issuing the report, the auditor should obtain updated representations reflecting any changes since the original date. The auditor’s opinion should not be dated earlier than the date on which the representations are signed.']

12. Subsequent Events and Comparative Information (SA 560 & SA 730)

['SA 560, ‘Subsequent Events’, requires the auditor to perform procedures to identify events occurring between the date of the financial statements and the date of the auditor’s report that may require adjustment or disclosure in the financial statements. Subsequent events are of two types: (a) adjusting events provide evidence of conditions that existed at the end of the reporting period, requiring adjustment (e.g., settlement of a litigation after the reporting date confirming the obligation existed at balance sheet date); and (b) non-adjusting events indicate conditions that arose after the reporting period, requiring disclosure only if material (e.g., a major fire destroying inventory after the reporting date). The auditor’s responsibility extends to the date of the auditor’s report.', 'Procedures include: (1) obtaining an understanding of any updates to management’s assessment of going concern; (2) inquiring of management and those charged with governance about events occurring after the reporting date; (3) reading minutes of meetings held after the reporting date; (4) examining subsequent issues of financial statements, bank statements, and cash receipts; (5) seeking updated legal correspondence; and (6) obtaining written representations regarding subsequent events. If a subsequent event comes to the auditor’s attention after the report date but before the financial statements are issued, the auditor should discuss the matter with management and determine whether the financial statements need amendment. If amended, the auditor should perform necessary procedures on the amended financial statements and extend the subsequent events review to the new report date.', 'SA 730, ‘Comparative Information’, deals with comparative financial statements presented alongside current period statements. The auditor’s opinion on the current period financial statements does not cover the comparative information unless specifically required by law or regulation. When comparative information consists of prior period financial statements audited by a predecessor auditor, the current auditor should not refer to the predecessor’s report unless required by law. If the prior period statements are unaudited, the auditor should not refer to them as audited. Material misstatements in comparative information identified during the current audit should be brought to management’s attention for correction; if uncorrected, the auditor should modify the opinion on the comparative information.', 'Modified opinions on comparative information may arise from: (a) improper application of accounting policies; (b) departures from SAs; (c) material uncertainties related to prior period events; or (d) inadequate disclosure of subsequent events affecting the prior period. The auditor’s report should clearly state that the opinion covers only the current period. If the prior period opinion was modified and the modification remains material in the current period, the current auditor should refer to the prior modification and its effect on comparatives.']

13. Group Audits (SA 600)

['SA 600, ‘Special Considerations — Audits of Group Financial Statements (Including the Work of Component Auditors)’, applies when the auditor audits group financial statements. A group consists of a parent, one or more subsidiaries, and other entities controlled by the parent. Components are entities included in the consolidation. The group engagement team is responsible for the group audit opinion and must obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding the financial information of components. Component materiality is set lower than group materiality to allocate for aggregation risk and undetected misstatements.', 'The group engagement team must: (1) obtain an understanding of the group, its components, and their environments, including internal control relevant to the group audit; (2) identify components that are significant due to size or specific risk; (3) determine the type and extent of audit procedures to be performed on component financial information; (4) evaluate the competence and independence of component auditors; and (5) review the audit work of component auditors. Significant components may require a full audit by the component auditor or specified audit procedures determined by the group engagement team. Non-significant components may be subject to analytical procedures at the group level.', 'When using the work of component auditors, the group engagement team should issue group audit instructions specifying the work to be performed, reporting requirements, and timelines. Instructions should include group materiality, component materiality, identified significant risks, and specific areas of focus. The group team must evaluate the adequacy of component auditors’ work by reviewing their audit documentation, discussing significant findings, and performing additional procedures if necessary. If the component auditor’s report is modified or if there are limitations on the group team’s access to component information, the group engagement partner should take appropriate action, which may include modifying the group opinion.', 'Consolidation adjustments and eliminations are critical areas. The group team must verify intercompany transactions, unrealized profits in inventory, dividend distributions, and foreign currency translation adjustments. For investments accounted for under the equity method, the group team should obtain evidence on the investee’s net assets and earnings. If a component is audited under a different financial reporting framework, the group team should ensure the component’s financial information is converted to the group framework with proper reconciliation and disclosure.']

14. Reporting under Standards on Auditing (SA 700–706)

['SA 700, ‘Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements’, specifies the form and content of the auditor’s report. The report includes: title, addressee, opinion section, basis for opinion section, key audit matters (for listed entities), other information (if applicable), responsibilities of those charged with governance, auditor’s responsibilities, and signature. The opinion section must clearly state whether the financial statements present fairly, in all material respects, the requisite information in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework. The basis for opinion section describes the basis for the auditor’s opinion and confirms compliance with SAs and independence requirements.', 'Modified opinions arise when: (a) material misstatements exist (qualified or adverse); (b) insufficient appropriate audit evidence has been obtained (qualified or disclaimer). Qualification wording differs depending on whether the matter is material but not pervasive or material and pervasive. Adverse opinions are expressed when misstatements are both material and pervasive. Disclaimers are issued when possible effects are both material and pervasive. Emphasis of Matter paragraphs draw attention to matters appropriately presented in the financial statements (e.g., significant uncertainty, exceptional events) without modifying the opinion. Other Matter paragraphs refer to matters other than those presented in the financial statements that are relevant to users’ understanding of the audit, opinion, or auditor’s responsibilities.', 'Key Audit Matters (KAMs) under SA 701 are communicated for listed entities and are those matters that, in the auditor’s professional judgment, were of most significance in the audit. Examples include significant risk assessments, complex估值, litigation outcomes, related party transactions, and going concern uncertainties. Each KAM is described with reference to relevant disclosures and the auditor’s response. Other Information (SA 720) requires the auditor to read other information included in annual reports and report if material misstatements are identified; failure to do so may require an emphasis of matter or other matter paragraph.', 'Special reports under SA 705 amendments cover situations where the auditor reports on a single financial statement rather than the complete set. The report should specify the frame of reference and refer to the complete set if applicable. Reports on compliance with aspects of contractual requirements or regulatory obligations (SA 805) require separate consideration. For reasonable assurance engagements other than audits (SA 800 series), the report format mirrors SA 700 but addresses the subject matter agreed upon with the client.']

15. Other Assurance and Related Services (SA 800 Series)

['SA 800, ‘Special Considerations — Fair Value Measurements and Disclosure’, addresses situations where the auditor is engaged to report on fair value measurements and disclosures in financial statements. The standard emphasizes the auditor’s responsibility to evaluate the methodology, assumptions, and data used in determining fair value, and to assess compliance with the applicable financial reporting framework. For Level 3 fair values (unobservable inputs), greater scrutiny is required, including the use of actuaries or valuation specialists when appropriate. Important facts

  • Standards on Auditing (SAs) are issued by the Auditing and Ethics Board and adopted by ICAI Council under Section 22B of the CA Act, 1949.
  • SA 200 sets overall objectives: reasonable assurance on material misstatement due to fraud or error, and reporting per SAs.
  • Reasonable assurance is high but not absolute, due to inherent limitations like sampling, evidence persuasiveness, and fraud concealment.
  • SQC 1 covers firm-level quality control; SA 220 covers engagement-level quality control. Engagement quality control review is mandatory for listed entities.
  • SA 315 requires understanding entity and environment including internal control; SA 330 requires responding to assessed risks with nature, timing, and extent of procedures.
  • Significant risks require special audit consideration; controls over significant risks must be tested in current period if relied upon.
  • SA 500 defines audit evidence; sufficiency = quantity, appropriateness = quality (relevance + reliability). External confirmations are generally highly reliable.
  • SA 402 covers service organizations; Type 2 report includes operating effectiveness and is more useful than Type 1 (design only).
  • SA 530 covers sampling; sampling risk differs from nonsampling risk. Misstatemens are projected to population; tolerable misstatement ≤ materiality.
  • SA 520 requires analytical procedures at planning, substantive, and final review stages; reliability depends on source, comparability, and precision.
  • SA 230 requires audit documentation forming sufficient record of basis for opinion; final file assembled within 60 days of report date, retention 8 years under Companies Act.
  • SA 240 covers fraud; fraud triangle = incentive/pressure, opportunity, rationalization. SA 250 covers laws and regulations — direct vs indirect compliance.
  • SA 570 covers going concern; material uncertainty requires emphasis of matter paragraph; inadequate disclosure leads to qualified/adverse opinion.
  • SA 550 covers related parties; identification via registers, minutes, conflict declarations, and contract inspection. Arm’s length terms must be verified.
  • SA 580 requires written representations on completeness, fraud knowledge, estimates, and subsequent events. Oral representations are insufficient.
  • SA 560 covers subsequent events; adjusting events (conditions existed at balance sheet date) vs non-adjusting (arose after). Report date responsibility.
  • SA 730 covers comparative information; opinion on current period only unless law requires otherwise. Prior period modifications must be addressed.
  • SA 600 covers group audits; component materiality < group materiality; group team evaluates component auditor competence and independence.
  • SA 700-706 govern reporting; opinion section, basis for opinion, KAMs for listed entities, Emphasis of Matter and Other Matter paragraphs.
  • Threats to ethics: self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation. Safeguards must eliminate or reduce threats to acceptable level.
  • Listed audit client prohibited non-assurance services: bookkeeping, valuation, internal audit outsourcing, corporate finance advisory, litigation support.
  • Partner rotation for listed entities: maximum 5 years service, 2-year cooling-off period, as per ICAI independence regulations.

Formulas and relationships

Sampling Risk for tests of controls: Risk of Assessing Control Risk Too Low = function of sample size, tolerable deviation rate, and expected population deviation rate (attributes sampling tables or statistical formulas).
Projected Misstatement (statistical sampling) = (Sample Misstatement / Sample Tolerable Misstatement) × Population Tolerable Misstatement, or more precisely: Projected Error = (Number of Misstatements in Sample / Sample Size) × Population Size × Average Misstatement Amount.
Monetary Unit Sampling (MUS) — Upper Misstatement Limit = Sample Interval × Factor (from tables based on expected misstatements and risk of incorrect acceptance).
Tolerable Misstatement = Materiality for Financial Statements As a Whole (FSW) or Performance Materiality (PM) allocated to specific assertions, typically PM = 50%-75% of FSW.
Sample Size for Variables Sampling (Classical): n = (Z × σ / E)² where Z = confidence level factor, σ = estimated population standard deviation, E = tolerable misstatement.
Reliability Hierarchy Score (conceptual): Original Document > Copy (if certified) > Oral Representation; External Source > Internal Source; Direct Knowledge > Indirect Knowledge.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing reasonable assurance with absolute assurance — candidates often write that audit provides 100% certainty, which is fundamentally wrong.
  • Mixing up SA 315 (risk assessment) with SA 330 (responses to risks) — SA 315 is about understanding and identifying, SA 330 is about designing and implementing procedures.
  • Failing to distinguish between adjusting and non-adjusting subsequent events — candidates often treat all post-balance sheet events as requiring adjustment.
  • Not recognizing that Type 2 service auditor report includes operating effectiveness while Type 1 does not — frequent exam trap.
  • Assuming written representations provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on their own — they are corroborative, not substitutes for other evidence.
  • Incorrectly applying materiality — using performance materiality as if it were overall materiality, or failing to allocate materiality to components in group audits.
  • Overlooking the requirement to test controls over significant risks in the current period even if tested in prior years — prior period evidence alone is insufficient.
  • Failing to distinguish between fraud and error in SA 240 — fraud requires intentional deception; error is unintentional. Both cause misstatements but intent differs.
  • Incorrect opinion modification — confusing when to use qualification versus adverse opinion (material but not pervasive vs material and pervasive), or disclaimer (scope limitation pervasive) versus adverse (misstatement pervasive).
  • Neglecting to address going concern disclosures in the audit report — if material uncertainty exists but is adequately disclosed, opinion is unmodified with Emphasis of Matter paragraph; failure to disclose leads to qualified/adverse opinion.
  • Treating analytical procedures as optional at substantive stage — SA 520 makes them mandatory when they provide relevant evidence and are effective; only when ineffective must other procedures dominate.
  • Not updating written representations to the report date — representations must be dated as of the auditor’s report date, not earlier.
  • Ignoring the 60-day assembly deadline for audit documentation under SA 230 — missing this procedural requirement affects compliance.
  • Confusing self-review threat with advocacy threat — self-review is evaluating one’s own work; advocacy is promoting client’s position to the point of losing objectivity.
  • Forgetting that partner rotation applies specifically to listed audit clients and key audit partners, not all audit engagements.
