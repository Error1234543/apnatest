Indirect Tax Laws — Complete CA Final Group 2 Notes

1. Structure and Framework of GST in India

The Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime in India was constitutionally introduced through the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, which inserted Article 246A, granting concurrent power to both Parliament and State Legislatures to make laws on GST. The dual-GST model comprises CGST (Central GST), SGST (State GST), UTGST (Union Territory GST), and IGST (Integrated GST). The governing statutory framework consists of four enactments: the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (CGST Act); the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (IGST Act); the Union Territory GST Acts applicable to each UT; and state-specific GST Acts such as the Maharashtra Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The GST Council, constituted under Article 279A, is the apex decision-making body comprising the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson), the Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance, and the Ministers in charge of Finance/Taxation from every State and UT. It determines the threshold limits, exemptions, rate structures, and principles for place of supply. Every provision must be read harmoniously with the CGST/IGST Acts and the respective State GST Act, since the substantive law is identical across the Centre and States — only the nomenclature and collecting authority differ.

Key constitutional provisions that shape indirect tax litigation and advisory work include Article 246A (legislative competence), Article 246B (parliamentary power to provide for GST on goods transported in the course of inter-State trade or import), Article 246C (residual taxation powers), Article 246D (levy and collection of GST in the manner prescribed by Parliament), Article 269A (goods and services supplied in the course of inter-State trade or commerce — IGST), Article 269B (Goods and Services Tax levied and collected by the Union but assigned to the States — current goods tax), and Articles 269-270 (taxes on inter-State sale/purchase and devolution of shares in central taxes). The transition from the pre-GST regime to GST involved extensive legislative bridging mechanisms, and understanding these articles is essential because ICAI examination questions frequently test the constitutional basis for GST jurisdictional disputes.

2. Levy, Charge, and Basic Definitions (CGST Act, Sections 2–7)

Section 9 of the CGST Act charges central GST on every taxable supply of goods or services made within a State, while Section 5 of the IGST Act charges integrated GST on every taxable supply made in the course of inter-State trade or commerce or on imports. A supply attracts GST only when it satisfies four cumulative conditions: (a) it is a supply of goods and/or services, (b) it is made for consideration, (c) it is made in the course or furtherance of business, and (d) it is a taxable supply — i.e., not wholly exempt and not made by an unregistered person below threshold (unless compulsory registration applies).

Critical definitional provisions under Section 2 of the CGST Act include:
- "Supply" [Section 2(94)] encompasses all forms of disposition, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease, or assignment of goods or services, including import of services for consideration. The definition is deliberately wide and captures constructive transfers.
- "Person" [Section 2(84)] includes an individual, HUF, company, firm, LLP, association, body of individuals, local authority, artificial juridical person, Government, etc.
- "Taxable person" [Section 2(108)] means a person registered under the Act or liable to register.
- "Input" [Section 2(59)] means any goods or services used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business, supplied by a supplier to a registered person.
- "Input Service Distributor (ISD)" [Section 2(61)] means a registered person who receives input service invoices and distributes the input tax credit to its branches.
- "Place of supply" [Section 2(96)], "time of supply" [Section 2(100)], "value of supply" [Section 2(112)] are defined by reference to their respective rules in the Acts.
- "Supply of goods" [Section 2(97)] and "supply of services" [Section 2(102)] carry extended meanings that determine whether a transaction falls under goods or services classification.

Section 4 makes GST payable on the notified date at such rates as the Council recommends, subject to the provisions of the Chapter. The tax becomes chargeable only upon completion of the supply — hence understanding time of supply is prerequisite. The phrase "taxable supply" under Section 2(105) means any supply that is not wholly exempt from tax under the Act and on which tax is chargeable.

3. Value of Supply and Input Tax Credit Mechanism

The value of supply under Section 15 of the CGST Act is the transaction value — the price actually paid or payable where the supplier and recipient are not related and consideration is the sole consideration. When parties are related [Section 15(1)], or when consideration includes money and other things, or when the aggregate consideration cannot be ascertained, the value is determined by the GST Valuation Rules, 2017 framed under Section 15(2).

Exclusions from the value of supply under Section 15(2) include: discounts (before or after supply, if recorded in invoice), subsidies limited to those by Government entities, and interest/late fee for delayed payment if charged separately. Inclusions under Section 15(3) cover: commission, packing, freight, forwarder charges (if billed separately), incidental expenses charged by the supplier, and interest/penalty for delayed payment when included in the price. The transaction value method prevails unless a specific valuation rule applies — for instance, where no consideration is paid (Section 16, Schedule I), where supply is between related persons (Section 17), or where goods are supplied by a principal to its job-worker (Section 18).

The Input Tax Credit (ITC) mechanism under Sections 16–19 of the CGST Act is the core anti-cascading provision. Eligibility for ITC [Section 16(1)] requires: (a) the person is registered, (b) he has received the goods/services, (c) a tax invoice or debit note has been issued, (d) the supplier has actually paid the tax to Government, (e) the return has been filed by the recipient (now governed by Rule 36C of CGST Rules post-valuation correction orders), and (f) the goods/services are used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business. Section 17 specifies blocked credits under Section 17(5) — motor vehicles (except those used for specified purposes), food/beverages, outdoor catering, beauty treatment, health/sports services, membership of club/health centre, personal use items, construction of immovable property for own use, goods lost/stolen/abandoned, and works contract services when used for construction of immovable property. Section 17(2) mandates proportionate reversal when ITC is used partly for business and partly for non-business purposes.

Input Service Distributor [Section 2(61) read with Rule 39] distributes credit based on the turnover of each recipient unit. Section 16(4) provides a time-bar of one year from the date of issue of invoice for claiming ITC. Section 17(1) deals with ineligible ITC, and the amendment introduction via the Finance Act 2021 brought significant changes to Section 17(5), particularly narrowing exceptions for blocked credits — a critical area for current examinations.

4. Time of Supply and Place of Supply Rules

Time of supply [Sections 12 and 13 of the CGST Act, Sections 7 and 8 of the IGST Act] determines the point at which tax liability crystallises. For goods [Section 12], the time of supply is the earlier of: (a) the date of issue of invoice by the supplier (or the last date by which the invoice should have been issued per Section 31), or (b) the date of receipt of payment, whichever is earlier, with a consequential adjustment if the invoice is issued after the payment receipt date. For services [Section 13], the general rule is the earlier of: (a) date of issue of invoice or last date for issuing invoice, or (b) date of receipt of payment, subject to the proviso for advance receipt (Section 31(3)(b)) where the time of supply of advance is the date of supply of service when the advance is adjusted against the final invoice.

The reverse charge mechanism (RCM) has distinct time-of-supply rules: for goods [Section 12(3)] and services [Section 13(3)] supplied under RCM, the time of supply is the earlier of the date of payment (when entered in books) or the day following 60 days from the date of receipt of goods/services. Where the supplier fails to issue an invoice, the time of supply is the date on which the recipient becomes aware of the supply.

Place of supply rules are equally critical. For goods [Section 10 of the CGST Act / Section 10 of IGST Act], the general rule is the location of the goods at the time movement commences; where goods are delivered by carrier, it is the destination; where goods are installed and commissioned, it is the place of installation. For services [Section 12 of CGST Act / Section 12 of IGST Act], the general rule is the location of the recipient where registered; if unregistered, the recipient's location (as per Aadhaar/pan); special rules apply for hospitality services, restaurant services, hotel accommodation, transport services, and event management. For immovable property services [Rule 3 of POS Rules], the location of the property governs. Understanding the distinction between intra-State supply (CGST+SGST/UTGST) and inter-State supply (IGST) hinges entirely on correctly applying the place of supply rules.

5. Supply, Composite and Mixed Supplies; Special Valuation Provisions

Under Section 7 of the CGST Act, "supply" includes all forms of supply as listed in Schedule I through IV. Schedule I lists supplies even without consideration: permanent exchange, transfer/destruction of goods by employer to employee, supply of goods between related persons where business is affected, provision of insurance by body corporate to members, supply of sample gifts, and such other supplies as notified. Schedule II deals with activities treated as supply of goods or services — for example, transfer of right to use goods is a supply of services [Section 7(1)(c)]; construction of immovable property for sale to a buyer (except where stamp duty indicates land sale) is a supply of services [Section 7(5A)]; and goods supplied by a principal to a job-worker are neither supply of goods nor services [Section 7(2)]. Schedule III contains supplies that are neither goods nor services — for instance, services by an electoral college, land/building where full beneficial interest is not transferred, and services relating to functions before a judge or arbitrator.

Composite supply [Section 8] means a supply comprising two or more taxable supplies naturally bundled, supplied in conjunction by a taxable person in the ordinary course of business, where one supply (the principal supply) constitutes the principal element. Example: a restaurant meal with dessert is a composite supply of food service with sweets as ancillary. Mixed supply [Section 9] means any combination of taxable supplies not naturally bundled, offered together for a single price, where the supply with the highest rate of tax governs the entire bundle. Example: a gift hamper containing biscuits, chocolate, and a calendar — the highest-rate item determines the tax rate for the entire mixed supply.

Special valuation provisions include Section 16 (supply without consideration), Section 17 (related person supply), Section 18 (supply from principal to job-worker), and Section 19 (agency provisions). The GST Valuation Rules further prescribe methods for determining value when transaction value is not applicable, requiring careful assessment especially in related-party transactions where arm's length pricing must be established.

6. Registration, E-Way Bill, Invoicing, and Returns — Procedure and Compliance

Registration [Section 22–24 of CGST Act, Rule 4–13 of CGST Rules] is required for any person whose aggregate turnover exceeds the threshold limit — ₹40 lakhs for goods (₹20 lakhs for special category states) for services, and lower thresholds for suppliers of inter-State taxable goods/services. Compulsory registration [Section 24] applies to casual taxable persons, persons making inter-State taxable supplies, persons requiring reverse charge, non-resident taxable persons, persons supplying online information/database access services from outside India to unregistered persons in India, e-commerce operators requiring deduction at source (TCS under Section 52), persons supplying goods through e-commerce operators who are required to collect tax at source (TCS under Section 52A), and any person paying tax under composition scheme opting for regular registration.

E-way bill [Rule 13 of CGST Rules] is required for movement of goods exceeding ₹50,000 in value, whether inter-State or intra-State. It must be generated on the common portal before the consignment movement, containing details of supplier, recipient, conveyance, and HSN code. Validity periods depend on distance: up to 100 km — 1 day; every additional 100 km or part thereof — 1 additional day. E-Way bills are generated electronically via the GSTN portal and are now fully integrated with e-invoicing.

E-invoicing [Rule 4 of CGST Rules and IRP notifications] mandates generating invoices through an Invoice Registration Portal (IRP) for businesses exceeding the prescribed turnover threshold (currently ₹5 crore as of recent notifications). The IRP assigns an Invoice Reference Number (IRN) and QR code. Mandatory fields for e-invoice include supplier GSTIN, consignee GSTIN, invoice number, date, HSN/SAC code, taxable value, IGST/CGST/SGST amount, and place of supply.

Return filing under Section 39 requires monthly returns: GSTR-1 (outward supplies summary), GSTR-3B (monthly summary return with self-declared tax liability), and GSTR-9/GSTR-9C (annual return and reconciliation). Composition dealers file GSTR-4 annually. Late fee provisions under Section 51 impose ₹50/day (₹20 CGST + ₹20 SGST, or ₹50 IGST) late fee, with a cap of ₹5,000 or 10% of turnover, whichever is less. Interest under Section 50 is levied at 18% per annum on delayed tax payment.

7. Customs Law — Basic Customs Act, 1962 and Customs Tariff Act, 1975

The Customs Act, 1962 governs the import and export of goods and the levy of customs duties. It has 30 Chapters covering port provisions, ships and aircraft, entry of vessels, goods, baggage, clearance, warehousing, smuggling prevention, duties, penalties, and appeals. Key concepts include:

- Import [Section 2(26)] means bringing goods into India from a place outside India; Export [Section 2(19)] means taking goods out of India to such a place.
- Customs territory [Section 2(8)] includes land, territorial waters, and airspace over which India exercises sovereignty but excludes Special Economic Zones (SEZs).
- Appropriate currency [Section 2(4)] is the Indian Rupee; customs duty is calculated using the exchange rate notified by CBIC.
- Free ship [Section 2(17)] is the line beyond which shipping is free of customs duty, used as the basis for assessing ad valorem duties.
- Delivery [Section 46] requires filing a bill of entry for imported goods or shipping bill for exported goods; goods are deemed imported/exported only upon delivery.

Section 12 imposes basic customs duty (BCD) on imported goods and excise duty equivalent on exported goods. Section 3(1) empowers levy of additional customs duty (counter-vailing duty under Section 3(2)) equal to GST/State VAT that would be payable if the goods were sold in the course of inter-State trade, and Section 3(3) levies special additional duty equal to the state VAT on inter-State sales. Section 3(4) provides for safeguard duties. Section 25 governs warehousing — goods may be stored in bonded warehouses without payment of duty until removal.

The Customs Tariff Act, 1975 contains the harmonised system nomenclature (HSN) — a six-digit classification code, with India extending it to eight digits for national tariff lines. Section 12A imposes anti-dumping duty, Section 12B counters subsidisation/dumping, and Section 17 governs drawback (refund of duties on re-export). Sections 28–28F deal with penalty provisions for fraudulent evasion, misdeclaration, concealment, and improper claim of drawback. Valuation [Sections 14–19] follows the WTO Customs Valuation Agreement, with sequential application of transaction value, identical goods value, like goods value, deductive value, computed value, and reasonable fallback methods.

8. Customs Valuation, Drawback, SEZ/Warehousing, and Penalty Regime

Customs valuation [Section 14 read with Customs Valuation (Determination of Price of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007] follows a hierarchical sequence. Method I: Transaction value of imported goods — the price actually paid or payable, adjusted for certain elements including commissions, packing, assists, royalties, and proceeds flowing back to the seller. Method II: Transaction value of identical goods. Method III: Transaction value of like goods. Method IV: Deductive value based on selling price in India, with deductions for profit, general expenses, transport, and insurance. Method V: Computed value based on cost of production plus profit and general expenses. Method VI: Reasonable means consistent with Principles of WTO Valuation Agreement.

Important adjustments to transaction value [Rule 4 of the Valuation Rules]: add commission except buying commission, cost of containers, packing, assists provided by buyer free or reduced cost, royalties and license fees, proceeds to seller, and freight/insurance up to Indian port or place of importation. Exclusions: interest under deferred payment arrangements, post-importation construction/assembly costs, domestic selling commission, carriage after importation, and duties/taxes paid in India.

Drawback [Section 16–17 of Customs Act, drawn under Drawback of Duties and Taxes on Exported Goods Regulations, 2019] allows refund of import duties and central taxes paid on inputs consumed in producing export goods. Two schemes exist: Special Drawback Rate (SDR) and General Drawback Rate (GDR). The current framework operates on a self-declaration basis with random verification.

Warehousing [Section 59–64] permits temporary storage without immediate duty payment. Bonded warehouses operate under security deposit or bank guarantee. SEZ units enjoy duty-free import of capital goods, plant, machinery, and raw materials; exports from SEZ are zero-rated under Section 16 of IGST Act read with SEZ Act, 2005. Duty exemption is contingent on realization of export proceeds within the prescribed period.

Penalty regime [Sections 112–119, 117, Chapter VII] imposes penalties ranging from ₹1,000 to ten times the duty evaded or value of goods, depending on severity. Sections 112–115 cover contraventions relating to import/export, smuggling, misuse of bonds, short-payment, and false declarations. Section 117 provides penalties for abetment and conspiracies. Section 120 empowers the Principal Commissioner to compound offences. The statute of limitation under Section 28I is three years from the relevant date (date of determination of duty, date of release from warehouse, or date of commission of offence).

9. Anti-Profiteering, GST Council Functions, and Miscellaneous Provisions

Anti-profiteering [Section 171 of CGST Act, Rules 127–131 of CGST Rules, and the National Authority for Anti-Profiteering (now subsumed under the GST Council structure)] mandates that benefits of reduction in rate of tax or increase in input tax credit shall be duly passed on to the recipient in the form of corresponding reduction in price. Any person availting ITC or reducing prices due to GST implementation must maintain records for five years. The Authority can order refund to beneficiaries with interest and impose penalty up to ₹50,000.

The GST Council under Article 279A performs the following key functions [Article 279A(4)]: recommends principles for levying GST on goods/services supplied in the course of inter-State trade or commerce; principles for input tax credit; goods/services that may be subjected to GST; threshold limits for levying GST; special provisions for NE states; incidence of tax in inter-State trade; determination of rate for ratifiable purposes; and any other matter relating to GST.

Other critical miscellaneous provisions include: Section 31 (invoice rules — time, contents, and e-invoicing requirements); Section 32 (delivery notes and bills of supply); Section 33 (delivery of goods and clearance); Section 34 (credit/debit notes and their modification rules); Section 37 (adjustment of excess/short payment after return filing); Section 38 (transfer of input tax credit to government account); Section 40 (unclaimed tax payable); Section 41 (interest on delayed refund); Section 52 (TDS — tax deducted at source by withholding agents); Section 52A (TCS — tax collected at source by e-commerce operators); Sections 67–73 cover inspection, search, seizure, and arrest provisions; Sections 74–79 deal with penalties for offenses involving fraud and misdeclaration; and Sections 107–133 contain appellate and revision mechanisms through GCIDA, SAT, and the High Courts.

For CA Final examination purposes, candidates must also be familiar with important circulars and notifications concerning ITC reversals, composition scheme amendments, e-way bill updates, place of supply clarifications for specified services, rate notifications, and the harmonised system code changes — as these represent the rapidly evolving face of indirect tax law in India.

Important facts

  • GST was constitutionally introduced via the 101st Amendment Act, 2016, inserting Articles 246A, 246B, 246C, 246D, 269, 269A, and 269B.
  • The GST Council (Article 279A) has 31 members — the Union Finance Minister chairs it; each State and UT has one vote weighted 1/3rd for Centre and 2/3rds for States.
  • Threshold for GST registration: ₹40 lakhs for normal states (goods and services), ₹20 lakhs for special category states; composition scheme threshold: ₹1.5 crore (₹75 lakh for special category states).
  • A composite supply has a principal supply; a mixed supply is taxed at the highest rate among the constituents.
  • Section 17(5) blocks ITC on motor vehicles (except those used for transport business, driving schools, or resale), food/beverages, outdoor catering, beauty treatment, health/sports services, membership of clubs/health centres, personal use items, construction of immovable property for own use, and work contract services for construction.
  • Time of supply for goods is the earlier of invoice date (or last date to issue) and payment receipt date. For services, the same rule applies with the advance provision under Section 31(3)(b). Under RCM, it is the earlier of payment date or the day following 60 days from receipt.
  • E-way bill is mandatory for movement of goods valued above ₹50,000, generated on the GSTN portal with validity dependent on distance travelled.
  • Customs valuation uses six hierarchical methods under the Customs Valuation Rules, 2007, with transaction value being the primary method.
  • Anti-profiteering (Section 171) requires passing on GST benefit to consumers; non-compliance attracts refund with interest and penalty up to ₹50,000.
  • Appeal hierarchy: Tribunal (GCISA) → High Court; revision lies before the Commissioner; first appeal is to the Appellate Tribunal.
  • Section 74 applies to fraud/intentional evasion with higher penalties (100% of tax evaded); Section 75 applies to cases without fraud with penalties capped at 25% of tax evaded.
  • Drawback of duties is available on export of goods under the Drawback Regulations, 2019, on either SDR or GDR basis.

Formulas and relationships

Late Fee = ₹50 per day (₹25 CGST + ₹25 SGST) for delayed GSTR-3B filing; cap = lower of ₹5,000 or 10% of turnover.
Interest on delayed tax payment (Section 50) = Outstanding tax × 18% per annum ÷ 365 × number of days of delay.
Penalty under Section 122 (without fraud) = 100% of tax evaded for certain specified contraventions; under Section 125 (no fraud) = 25% of tax evaded or ₹25,000, whichever is higher.
Penalty under Section 122 (with fraud, Section 74) = 100% of tax evaded plus interest at 18% per annum from the date tax became payable to the date of order.
Penalty under Section 125 (no fraud, Section 75) = 25% of tax evaded or ₹25,000, whichever is higher; interest under Section 50 applies at 18% p.a.
Customs duty payable = Assessable value × Basic Customs Duty rate + Customs Handling Fee (0.01% rounded off) + Social Welfare Surcharge (10% of total duty).
Additional Customs Duty (SAD/CVD) = Assessed value × applicable rate (varies by item, typically aligned with GST rate).
E-way bill validity: up to 100 km = 1 day; every additional 100 km or part = 1 additional day; beyond 100 km = base 1 day + (distance-100)/100 rounded up days.
Refund of ITC where export is under bond/LUT without payment of IGST = IGST payable on exports minus ITC availed on inputs/input services — per Section 16 of IGST Act.
Place of supply for services to registered person = location of recipient (State GST + CGST if intra-State; IGST if inter-State); for unregistered = location of recipient as per Aadhaar/PAN.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing composite supply with mixed supply — the former has a natural bundling with a principal supply; the latter lacks natural bundling and is taxed at the highest rate.
  • Incorrectly applying time-of-supply rules for advances under RCM — the 60-day rule under RCM differs from the general provision, and the earlier of payment date or 60th day after receipt governs, not the invoice date.
  • Assuming blocked credits under Section 17(5) can be claimed by reversing and re-claiming — once blocked, they remain blocked; however, amendments (Finance Act 2021) have narrowed some exclusions, so candidates must verify the latest position.
  • Using wrong place-of-supply rule for services — the default is recipient's location for registered persons, but specific services (hospitality, restaurant, transport, immovable property, events) have bespoke rules under Section 12.
  • Miscalculating e-way bill validity — it extends per 100 km slab, not per exact km; rounding-up applies to every partial 100 km.
  • Treating SEZ supplies as zero-rated without verifying the condition of realization of export proceeds within the prescribed time — failure to realize within the timeframe leads to duty demand with interest.
  • Applying Section 74 penalties (fraud-based) in cases where only misdeclaration exists without intent to evade — Section 75 penalties apply in the absence of fraud.
  • Overlooking the requirement under Section 16(2)(b) that the tax invoice must be issued by the supplier — without a valid tax invoice, ITC cannot be claimed regardless of actual tax payment by the supplier.
  • Not distinguishing between IGST (inter-State supply/import) and CGST+SGST (intra-State supply) in questions on value of supply — miscalculating GST composition is a frequent error in computation problems.
  • Ignoring the amendment introduced via the Finance Act 2021 widening the scope of Section 17(5) to include personal use, construction services, and certain other categories — updated knowledge of Section 17(5) is essential for current exams.
