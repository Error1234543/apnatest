CA Final Group 2 — Integrated Business Solutions: Flashcards

CAEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

What is Integrated Business Solutions (IBS) at CA Final level, and what distinguishes it from single-subject papers?

Integrated Business Solutions is a problem-based, case-integrative paper in CA Final Group 2 where students must apply knowledge across accounting, direct tax, indirect tax, auditing, laws, and financial management within real-world business scenarios. It differs from single-subject papers by testing cross-functional linkage — e.g., how a transfer-pricing adjustment affects tax liability, contingent liability disclosure, audit risk, and stakeholder communication simultaneously. The paper assesses professional judgment, not merely procedural recall.

Card 2

Name the key standard-setter and framework governing Integrated Reporting globally, and state its core purpose.

The International Integrated Reporting Council (IIRC), now part of the IFRS Foundation, issued the <IR> Framework. Its core purpose is to promote a cohesive understanding of how an organisation creates value over time through integration of financial and non-financial information, focusing on six capitals (financial, manufactured, intellectual, human, social and relationship, natural) and the connectivity of information between them. For Indian ICAI's IBS context, it serves as a reference point for evaluating multi-stakeholder value creation cases.

Card 3

What are the six elements of a high-quality report or presentation required under the <IR> Framework principles?

The <IR> Framework specifies nine guiding principles (not six elements); however, if referring to the six capitals that feed into those principles, they are: (1) Financial capital, (2) Manufactured capital, (3) Intellectual capital, (4) Human capital, (5) Social and relationship capital, (6) Natural capital. Each capital represents a stock of value that an organisation uses in the course of creating value over time. Under the framework principles, a good integrated report demonstrates: strategic focus, connectivity of information, stakeholders, value creation/erosion, conciseness, reliability, materiality, and consistency/transparency.

Card 4

In an IBS case involving a holding-subsidiary group with related-party transactions, identify three distinct professional issues that must be addressed and cite the relevant standards or provisions for each.

Three typical issues: (1) Related-party disclosures — Ind AS 24 / AS 18 requires identification of related parties, disclosure of transaction nature, volume, and amounts; ICAI's Guidance Note on Related Party Disclosures may be relevant. (2) Transfer pricing — Section 92–92F of the Income-tax Act, 1961 and Rules 10A–10D govern ALP determination and documentation; international or domestic related-party transactions must comply. (3) Audit and professional ethics — SA 550 governs auditor responsibilities regarding related parties; Code of Ethics (IESBA / ICAI) requires threat assessment (self-interest, familiarity, advocacy) and safeguards for the group audit.

Card 5

A company proposes to merge with a listed entity through a scheme under Section 230-232 of the Companies Act, 2013. Identify four critical areas of professional scrutiny an ICS aspirant must address.

Four areas of scrutiny: (1) Valuation fairness — valuation methodology (DNV/NPV/market multiples) and reasonableness of share-exchange ratio; potential dissenting shareholder protection. (2) Statutory compliances — board and NCLT approval requirements, SEBI (SAST) and SEBI (LODR) implications if listed, ROC filing timelines. (3) Tax consequences — Section 47(vi)/(via)/vb carry-forward of losses and unabsorbed depreciation may be allowed subject to conditions in Section 72A and Section 47; GST treatment on amalgamation. (4) Stakeholder impact — creditor consent mechanism, employee rights, continuity of contracts, disclosure adequacy under draft scheme.

Card 6

Differentiate the legal mechanism and creditor-protection emphasis between Scheme of Arrangement under Sections 230-232 and Compromise and Arrangement under the IBC, 2016.

Scheme under Sections 230-232 (Companies Act, 2013) is a civil court/NGD process initiated by the company (or creditors/members) for reconstruction or amalgamation; NCLT sanction is required, and there is no automatic moratorium unless invoked separately. The IBC, 2016 provides a time-bound insolvency resolution process with a statutory moratorium under Section 14 upon NCLT admission, protecting the corporate debtor's assets from litigation and recovery actions; a resolution plan is approved by 66% of voting share of financial creditors and binds all stakeholders including secured creditors. IBC takes precedence over Companies Act under Section 238.

Card 7

In a cross-border group restructuring where an Indian subsidiary sells IP to a foreign parent at an arm's length price, but the parent then licenses it back at a royalty exceeding the ALP, identify the transfer-pricing risk and the corrective mechanism available.

Risk: The royalty paid back to the parent constitutes an international transaction (Section 92B) at a price potentially exceeding ALP (Section 92C); this can lead to addition under Section 92CE and disallowance under Section 40(a)(ia) if TDS provisions are also breached (Section 195). Corrective mechanism: the assessee may seek advance pricing agreement (APA) under Section 92CC(3) prospectively; retroactively, remedial action is through revision u/s 263 by the PCAR (if order is prejudicial to revenue) or mutual agreement procedure (MAP) under the relevant DTAA if double taxation arises. Cost accounting records u/s 286 and Rule 8D may also require adjustment.

Card 8

An auditor discovers that management has classified a probable contingent liability as a provision under Ind AS 37. Critically evaluate whether the classification is correct and state the disclosure requirement.

Under Ind AS 37, a 'provision' is recognised only when there is a present obligation (legal or constructive), probable outflow, and reliable estimate. A 'contingent liability' exists when either the probability is not probable (possible or remote) or the amount cannot be measured reliably; it is NOT recognised but disclosed (unless remote). If the outflow is 'possible' but not 'probable', it must remain disclosed as a contingent liability, not recognised as a provision. Disclosure includes nature, estimated financial effect (or statement that it cannot be estimated), and possibility of reimbursement.

Card 9

A listed Indian company enters into a joint venture with a foreign entity. As the professional advisor, identify the specific regulatory requirements you must address beyond general company law.

Regulatory requirements include: (1) RBI — FDI route and sectoral caps (Automatic vs Government) under FEMA; Form FC-GPR for issue of shares to foreign JV partner. (2) SEBI — SEBI (LODR) Regulation disclosure onJV materiality, related-party transaction compliance, and possibly Scheme of Arrangement if restructuring involved. (3) Competition Commission — Section 5/6 of Competition Act, 2002 if combination thresholds are crossed. (4) Tax — DTAA benefits consideration, Transfer Pricing documentation, TDS u/s 195 on any remittances, and CTH (Controlled Foreign Company) rules under Chapter X-D if applicable. (5) External Commercial Borrowing (ECB) or Guarantee regulations if JV is funded externally.

Card 10

Explain how the concept of 'Going Concern' intersects with 'Fraud Risk' in an IBS case where a company faces severe liquidity stress but continues trading without disclosure.

Going concern assessment (Ind AS 1 / SA 570) requires management to evaluate availability of resources for at least 12 months from reporting date. Continued trading despite severe liquidity stress without adequate disclosure is a red flag for fraud risk (SA 240), as it may indicate intentional understatement of liabilities or overstatement of assets to conceal inability to meet obligations. Auditor must: (a) challenge management's going-concern assumptions, (b) assess incentive/pressure rationalisation triangle (fraud risk factors), (c) consider legal/regulatory implications — violation of Companies Act disclosure norms and possible Section 447 proceedings, and (d) evaluate impact on audit opinion (qualification/emphasis of matter/adverse/reporting under Companies Act u/s 143(12)).

Card 11

Identify four principal sources of professional guidance a CA must consult when drafting an Integrated Business Solutions answer, regardless of the specific case.

Four principal sources: (1) Statutory provisions — Companies Act, 2013; Income-tax Act, 1961; FEMA, 1999; IBC, 2016; GST Act where applicable. (2) Accounting Standards — Ind AS / AS notified by MCA, as interpreted by ICAI's guidance notes. (3) Auditing Standards — SAs issued by ICAI, plus SA's on related matters (fraud, going concern, related parties). (4) Professional standards — ICAI's Code of Ethics, ICAI's Guidance Notes, ICAI's Examination Scheme/Study Material, and relevant judicial precedents (Supreme Court/Tribunal) cited in ICAI study material.

Card 12

A corporate group wishes to demerge a profitable division while keeping the loss-making division. Analyse the tax, corporate, and audit implications to be addressed in your recommendation.

Tax: Section 2(19A) defines demerger; Section 72A read with Section 47(vib) permits carry-forward of losses/unabsorbed depreciation of the losing company ONLY IF the losses are attributable to the demerged undertaking and conditions (public shareholding, minimum 3/5th shareholders) are satisfied; otherwise losses lapse. Ind AS 52 / AS 64 may apply. Corporate: Sections 230-232 NCLT procedure; creditor protection via Tribunal sanction; proportionate asset/liability transfer terms. Audit: auditor's report on demerged company's separate financials, continuity of engagement, related-party implications post-demerge, and compliance with SA 710 on comparative figures. Also address GST continuity u/s 18(3) and employees' PF/ESOP vesting.

Card 13

State the two key criteria that differentiate a Finance Lease from an Operating Lease under Ind AS 116, and explain one financial-statement impact of this distinction.

Under Ind AS 116, a lease is classified as a Finance Lease if it transfers substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership; indicators include: (1) Transfer of ownership by end of lease term, (2) Bargain purchase option, (3) Lease term covers major part of asset's economic life, (4) Present value of lease payments amounts to substantially all of fair value, (5) Asset is of specialised nature. Operating leases are those not meeting these criteria. Impact: Finance lease recognises both Right-of-Use asset and lease liability on balance sheet; operating lease also now generally requires recognition u/s 116 (except short-term/low-value exemptions), but earlier AS 19 operated differently — distinction critically affects leverage ratios, EBITDA, and covenants.

Card 14

In a case where an Indian company receives technology know-how from its foreign associate, what dual regulatory regimes apply and what is the compliance overlap?

Dual regimes: (1) Transfer Pricing — Section 92B defines 'intangible property'; know-how/know-how licensing is an international transaction subject to ALP assessment under Section 92C; OLC/Global NOLC u/s 92CD documentation is required. (2) FEMA/RBI — if consideration involves royalty/remittance, Form 15CA/CB, and possibly approval u/s 6(5) if not on Automatic Route depending on sectoral cap and DPR requirements; royalty rate subject to Schedule III of FEMA and DTAA ceiling. Overlap: ALP determination affects allowable deduction u/s 37/40; excess royalty paid at non-ALP may be disallowed under Section 40(a)(ia) for TDS default; both regimes converge on documentation and substance-over-form assessment.

Card 15

What is the 'principle of substance over form' in IBS contexts, and illustrate with one scenario involving a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV).

Substance over form requires that transactions be accounted for based on their economic reality rather than merely their legal structure. Scenario: A company establishes an SPV and transfers receivables to it purportedly as a 'sale' to clean up its balance sheet, but retains de facto control over the SPV (e.g., through recourse clauses, repurchase agreements, or majority board representation). Ind AS 112 / AS 32 requires consolidation of the SPV and derecognition of receivables only if substantially all risks and rewards are transferred. Treating it as a sale purely on legal title would violate substance-over-form, resulting in misstatement of liabilities and off-balance-sheet financing.

Card 16

A manufacturing company faces a environmental remediation liability after discovering soil contamination. Identify the accounting treatment under Ind AS 37, the tax deductibility position, and the audit implication.

Accounting: Ind AS 37 requires provision for environmental remediation when (a) present obligation exists (legal or constructive), (b) outflow is probable, (c) reliable estimate can be made; discounting is applied using pre-tax rate reflecting time value. Tax: Deductibility depends on actual expenditure being incurred and claimed u/s 37(1); provisions alone are generally NOT deductible (Section 43B — if it qualifies as liability incurred by payment u/s 43B, timing matters). Audit: SA 540 (estimates) and SA 550 (related party if obligation stems from associate) may apply; also assess whether environmental non-compliance indicates going-concern risk and whether additional disclosures under environmental laws are required.

Card 17

In a complex group reconstruction involving cross-border entities, explain the role of the Principal Officer (Section 163) and how it interacts with GST and direct-tax obligations.

Under GST, Section 163 of the CGST Act appoints a Principal Officer for a foreign corporate body or unincorporated body in India, responsible for compliance, filing returns, and receiving notices. This designation creates dual accountability: for GST, the PO is liable for penalties and defaults; for direct tax, if the entity has a Permanent Establishment (PE) or is required to furnish returns under Section 142/143, the PO may also serve as the authorised representative under Section 167 of the IT Act. A CA advising must ensure consistent PO appointment across regimes, avoid conflicting designations, and verify that the PO has requisite authorisation powers and tax compliance standing.

Card 18

When analysing a case involving related-party guarantees, identify the four distinct legal/tax/accounting issues that arise simultaneously and briefly state each.

Four issues: (1) Corporate guarantee — Companies Act Section 185/186 prohibits/directs guarantees to related parties without member approval and within limits; violation attracts penalty and invalidity concerns. (2) Transfer pricing — guarantee may constitute an internationally related-party transaction (Section 92B(1)(d)) requiring ALP assessment (Rule 10B) even if no explicit fee is charged; benchmarking may imply arm's length guarantee commission. (3) Contingent liability disclosure — AS 29 / Ind AS 37 may require disclosure of exposure; if guarantee is called, it becomes a provision. (4) Audit risk — related-party guarantee increases self-interest/familiarity threats under Code of Ethics and requires specific SA 550 procedures.

Card 19

Define 'Business Restructuring' in the IBS context and list four restructuring options commonly tested, with one advantage and one risk per option.

Restructuring refers to fundamental changes in a company's organisational structure, capital, operations, or legal form to improve viability, tax efficiency, or strategic positioning. Four options: (1) Amalgamation/Merger — Advantage: operational synergies; Risk: tax conditions strict, dissenting shareholder objections. (2) Demerger — Advantage: unlock value of divisions; Risk: loss carry-forward may be forfeited if conditions not met. (3) Internal reorganisation/slice-and-dice — Advantage: flexibility; Risk: scrutiny under thin-capitalisation or substance doctrine. (4) Scheme of Compromisement under IBC — Advantage: moratorium protects assets; Risk: resolution plan may dilute original equity, creditor approval threshold complexity.

Card 20

An IBS case presents a company that has recognised deferred tax assets (DTAs) amounting to ₹500 crore against future taxable profits, but the company's recent performance shows consistent losses. As an advisor, critique the DTA recognition and propose corrective actions.

Under Ind AS 12 / AS 22, DTAs are recognised only to the extent that probability of sufficient future taxable profit exists to utilise the DTA. Persistent losses create significant doubt about that probability. The DTA of ₹500 crore likely violates prudence and constitutes material misstatement. Corrective actions: (1) Reduce DTA to the recoverable portion based on credible cash-flow projections and tax-planning strategies. (2) Disclose the basis of estimation and sensitivity in notes (AS 12 paragraph 24/Ind AS 12 para 46). (3) Consider whether temporary differences giving rise to the DTA are themselves recoverable. (4) Evaluate going-concern and fraud-risk implications if overstatement was deliberate.

Card 21

Explain the interaction between Chapter X-D (GAAR) of the Income-tax Act and the arm's-length principle (Chapter X-B) in a case where an arrangement is structured to secure a treaty benefit.

Chapter X-B (Sections 92–92F) applies the ALP principle to international and specified domestic transactions, requiring benchmarking of prices. Chapter X-D (Sections 144–147) is GAAR — a general anti-abuse rule that can disregard or reconstruct an impermissible avoidance arrangement even if individual transactions appear to comply with ALP. If an arrangement's sole or main purpose is obtaining a treaty benefit (e.g., reduced withholding tax via a conduit company in a low-tax jurisdiction), GAAR may be invoked if the arrangement lacks commercial substance, involves mismatched deductions/credits, or uses an intermediary contrary to the object of the treaty. GAAR overrides specific provisions and requires Principal Adjudicating Authority approval.

Card 22

What are the auditor's obligations when a case reveals a 'related-party relationship of dominance' under AS 18 / Ind AS 24, and how does this differ from ordinary related-party disclosure?

Under AS 18 (and converged Ind AS 24), a related-party relationship of dominance exists when one party has the ability to control or significantly influence the other's financial and operating decisions. Disclosure requirements are more extensive: (a) nature of relationship, (b) description of transactions, (c) amounts involved, (d) terms and conditions (including Whether guaranteed), (e) amounts due/receivable, and (f) provisions for doubtful debts. In audit, SA 550 requires special procedures: confirm the existence of the dominant relationship independently, assess whether financial statements adequately disclose the dominance itself (not just transactions), evaluate whether the dominance indicates potential management override, and consider communicating with those charged with governance about the significance of the relationship.

Card 23

A listed company's board proposes issuing sweat-equity shares to key managerial personnel. List the statutory conditions that must be mandatorily complied with under the Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI regulations.

Statutory conditions under Companies Act, 2013 (Section 54): (1) Issue must be authorised by special resolution; (2) Maximum 25% of issued share capital in a financial year (can be increased to 30% with further special resolution); (3) Must be for consideration other than cash (intellectual property, know-how, grants); (4) One-year lock-in on sweat-equity shares; (5) Valuation by registered valuer as prescribed. SEBI (SSRs) and SEBI (LODR) conditions: (a) disclosure of particulars in annual return, (b) compliance with corporate governance code, (c) prior approval of board and shareholders, (d) maximum price not less than average of volumetric weighted average of closing prices of preceding week; additional SEBI SAST/LODR takeover and continuous disclosure norms may apply.

Card 24

In an IBS advisory involving a distressed company, distinguish between a 'Resolution Plan' under IBC and a 'Compromise Scheme' under Sections 230-232, highlighting two key procedural differences.

Two key procedural differences: (1) Decision-making body — Resolution Plan is approved by 66% of financial creditors (vote-share), whereas a Scheme under Sections 230-232 requires 3/4th value of creditors and 3/4th majority in number of members/classes. (2) Timeframe and binding effect — IBC imposes a strict 180-day (extendable to 270 days) timeline, and upon NCLT approval the plan binds all stakeholders including dissentient creditors, secured creditors, and guarantors (subject to IRAC norms); a scheme under Companies Act has no hard statutory timeline and requires individual class-wise consent with potential for prolonged appeals. Additionally, IBC resolution professional takes over management, while a Companies Act scheme typically leaves management in place during the process.

Card 25

A case study describes a company that has capitalised interest during construction of a qualifying asset but has failed to disclose the capitalisation rate applied. Identify the applicable standard, the specific disclosure gap, and the material misstatement risk.

Applicable standard: Ind AS 23 / AS 16 'Borrowing Costs'. Required: capitalise borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition/construction of a qualifying asset using the capitalisation rate determined for general borrowings or specific borrowings. Disclosure gap: failure to disclose the capitalisation rate, the amount capitalised, and the methodology — mandated under paragraphs 67–68 of Ind AS 23. Risk: misstatement of asset carrying value (overstated) and profit (understated expense recognition); also breaches transparency requirements. Further, if the capitalisation period extends beyond the asset's ready-for-use date, interest capitalisation must cease — another area of potential misstatement to check.

Card 26

As a professional advisor, you are asked to evaluate whether a cross-border service charge paid to a parent entity qualifies for deduction under Section 37(1) and is at ALP. Outline your step-by-step analytical approach.

Stepwise approach: (1) Characterise the transaction — determine whether the service constitutes 'specified international transaction' u/s 92B(1)(a) and whether it is an eligible expense u/s 37(1) (ordinary, necessary, not capital or personal). (2) Verify benefits test — ensure services actually benefited the Indian entity; self-serving charges denied (TN-1 precedent). (3) Apply transfer pricing methodology — select appropriate CUP/ TNMM/PSM under Rule 10A/10B; benchmark the service charge against comparable uncontrolled transactions. (4) Document — prepare OLC and Form 3CEB (Rule 10DA/10DD). (5) Assess TDS — withholding u/s 195 on service fee/remittance; check DTAA benefit. (6) Evaluate reasonableness — ensure allocation basis is fair; challenge excessive overhead allocations.

Card 27

Discuss the concept of 'Controlled Foreign Company' (CFC) rules under Indian tax law and identify two situations where they would be triggered for an Indian resident holding a stake in a low-tax jurisdiction offshore company.

CFC rules (Chapter X-D, Sections 94–94F, inserted via Finance Act 2012) tax certain income of foreign companies in the hands of Indian controlling residents even if not distributed. Trigger situations: (1) An Indian resident holds >50% voting power or value in an offshore company situated in a jurisdiction with an effective tax rate below 50% of the Indian corporate tax rate, and the offshore company's income is primarily passive (dividends, interest, royalty, capital gains). (2) An Indian resident controls an offshore entity primarily configured to defer or avoid Indian tax — e.g., accumulating profits that are unrealized or not distributing dividends to the Indian holder. Consequence: the proportionate share of undistributed CFC income is included in the Indian resident's total income u/s 94(3).

Card 28

An auditor is engaged to audit a group where the parent uses a different accounting policy for inventory (FIFO vs Weighted Average) compared with the consolidated financial statements (Weighted Average). How should this be handled professionally?

Under Ind AS 2 / AS 2, inventory should be valued using FIFO or Weighted Average consistently for each category of inventory; mixing methods within the same group is generally non-compliant unless justified. At consolidation, uniform accounting policies must be adopted (Ind AS 110 para 19/AS 21 para 17); if the parent's standalone uses FIFO but consolidation uses WA, the consolidation adjustments must reconcile and disclose the variance clearly. Auditor should: (1) request management to align the policy across standalone and consolidated, or ensure proper reconciliation. (2) If not aligned, qualify opinion for non-compliance with Ind AS 110/AS 21 uniformity requirement. (3) Consider whether this signals deeper control weaknesses over group reporting.

Card 29

In the context of IBS, what is 'Enterprise Risk Management' (ERM) and why is it critical to advise a client embarking on a large-scale digital transformation project?

ERM is a structured approach to identifying, assessing, prioritising, and managing risks across the entire enterprise (strategic, operational, financial, compliance) rather than in silos. For a digital transformation project, ERM is critical because: (1) Technology adoption introduces new risks — data privacy (DPDP Act / GDPR), cyber-security, vendor lock-in, legacy system compatibility. (2) Strategic risk — misalignment with business objectives, change-management resistance. (3) Financial risk — cost overruns, ROI uncertainty, impairment of existing IT assets. (4) Compliance risk — regulatory reporting changes, tax tech compliance. A CA must evaluate whether the transformation plan incorporates risk mitigation, insurance coverage, third-party assurance, and measurable milestones to avoid value destruction.

Card 30

A company issues convertible debentures that are classified as debt but contain a mandatory conversion feature. Critique the classification under Ind AS 32 / AS 30 and state the correct treatment.

Under Ind AS 32 / AS 32 (Financial Instruments), an instrument is classified as liability or equity based on the substantive contractual obligation. If conversion is mandatory at a predetermined date irrespective of the issuer's ability to deliver shares, the instrument may still contain a liability component. However, if the conversion is solely into a fixed number of equity shares (fixed-for-fixed), the entire instrument could be equity. A mandatory conversion feature that does not give the holder a choice usually means the instrument is a liability until conversion. Misclassification as pure debt ignores the equity component; the correct treatment may require split accounting — recognising the liability component at fair value and the residual equity component, with subsequent measurement of the liability component using the effective-interest method.

Card 31

Outline the essential elements of a valid Advance Pricing Agreement (APA) under Indian tax law and name the three types available.

Essential elements of a valid APA: (1) Application by an assessee engaged in an international transaction (Section 92CC(3)); (2) Declaration of proposed ALP and chosen method; (3) Assessment by Transfer Pricing Officer (TPO) and approval by Principal Commissioner; (4) Mutual Agreement Procedure (MAP) route for unilateral/bilateral APAs; (5) Renewal/revision mechanism; (6) Binding on concerned units across jurisdictions for the APA term. Three types: (a) Unilateral APA (between Indian assessee and Indian tax authority only), (b) Bilateral APA (under the relevant DTAA's MAP article, involving two tax administrations), (c) Multilateral APA (involving three or more jurisdictions, permitted under BEPS Action 14 framework).

Card 32

Explain how the 'arm's-length principle' applies to intra-group service charges and what the 'benefit test' requires in practice.

The arm's-length principle for intra-group services (Rule 10B(2)(b)) requires that a charge be made only if the service would have been provided by an independent enterprise under comparable circumstances and on comparable terms. The 'benefit test' mandates that: (1) the service must provide economic or commercial value to the recipient (enhance value, reduce cost, or manage risk), (2) the recipient would have performed the same service in-house or obtained it from an unrelated party if available at arm's length, (3) duplicative charges (where group overhead is already reflected in the functional profile of the recipient) must be eliminated. Pure holding-company overheads not conferring direct benefit to subsidiaries are typically disallowed.

Card 33

In a liquidation scenario under the IBC, rank the following claims in order of priority according to Section 53(1) of the IBC: (a) Secured creditors, (b) Wages due to workers (last 24 months), (c) Government dues, (d) Resolution plan costs and liquidation expenses, (e) Preferential creditors.

Order of priority under Section 53(1): (1) Liquidation expenses and resolution costs — pari passu (highest). (2) Workmen's dues for last 24 months — pari passu with securing creditors on secured assets (but secured creditors rank equally with workmen on security). (3) Financial creditors (unsecured) — pari passu. (4) Government dues (central/state) for last 2 years — pari passu. (5) Remaining government dues and others. (6) Preferential creditors — pari passu with other creditors. The exact order within each tier is pari passu, but overall the waterfall is: (d) first, then (b) alongside secured creditors on their collateral, then (a) and (b) together on security value, then (e), then (c), and lastly remaining unsecured creditors.

Card 34

What is the 'materiality concept' in IBS case analysis, and how should a CA determine what is material when advising on a possible acquisition?

Materiality refers to the magnitude of an omission or misstatement that, in the surrounding circumstances, makes it probable the judgment of a reasonable person would have been changed or influenced. In IBS acquisition cases, a CA determines materiality by: (1) quantitative thresholds — percentage of assets, revenue, profit, or market cap; (2) qualitative considerations — whether the item affects compliance status, tax position, regulatory standing, or stakeholder decisions; (3) contextual relevance — even a small amount may be material if it involves fraud, related-party manipulation, or breach of covenant. ICAI's Guidance on Materiality and ICAI's Statement on Materiality in Auditing provide benchmarks; in advisory work, the CA should document the basis of conclusion and adjust for industry norms and investor expectations.

Card 35

Describe the audit evidence requirements under SA 500 when auditors are challenged on the recoverability of a large intangible asset acquired in a business combination.

SA 500 (Audit Evidence) requires auditors to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence regarding the impairment or recoverability of intangible assets acquired in a business combination. Procedures include: (1) Understanding management's impairment model (Ind AS 36 / AS 28) — CGU identification, cash-flow projections, discount rates, terminal values. (2) Evaluating the reasonableness of assumptions against external data (market multiples, industry reports, macro assumptions). (3) Testing the mathematical accuracy of the DCF model. (4) Considering whether events or changes indicate possible impairment (negative goodwill, market decline, technological obsolescence). (5) Reviewing subsequent events for corroboration. (6) Assessing disclosure adequacy (sensitivity analysis, key assumptions). If evidence is insufficient, modify opinion or add emphasis of matter.

Card 36

When a case requires evaluation of whether a joint venture should be consolidated or accounted for using the equity method, which standard governs and what criterion decides the outcome?

Ind AS 28 / AS 23 'Investments in Associates and Joint Ventures' governs. The deciding criterion is 'significant influence' (associate, typically ≥20% voting power) versus 'control' (subsidiary, consolidation). For a joint venture specifically, if the venturers share joint control and have rights to the net assets (not necessarily the assets/liabilities themselves), equity method is used. If joint control exists but the arrangement is structured as a jointly-controlled operation where the venturer has rights to assets and obligations for liabilities, proportionate consolidation may have been permitted under older standards; under Ind AS 11, joint arrangements are classified as joint operations or joint ventures — only joint ventures use equity method; joint operations require proportionate recognition of assets/liabilities/income/expenses.

Card 37

A parent company provides a guarantee to a bank for a loan taken by its subsidiary. The subsidiary defaults. As the parent's advisor, list the cascade of legal, tax, and accounting consequences.

Cascade consequences: (1) Corporate guarantee enforcement — bank calls the guarantee; parent is legally obligated to repay principal + interest per guarantee deed. (2) Companies Act compliance — Section 185/186 scrutiny: was guarantee approved by board and members? If not, director may be personally liable and guarantee may be voidable. (3) Tax — repayment treated as business expenditure u/s 37(1) if for business purpose; however, if the guarantee was given as accommodation rather than commercial benefit, deduction may be denied. TDS implications on any recovery. (4) Transfer pricing — if between related parties, TP documentation required; whether arm's length commission for guarantee is imputed. (5) Accounting — contingent liability converted to provision/loss under Ind AS 37; possible impairment of investment in subsidiary. (6) Audit — related-party audit procedures, going-concern review for the subsidiary.

Card 38

Explain the 'look-through approach' in consolidated financial statements and state one situation where it is mandatory under Ind AS 110.

The look-through approach (sometimes termed 'transparency approach') is used when a parent invests through an intermediate vehicle (e.g., a fund or SPV). Instead of applying a blanket equity method or consolidation, the parent looks through the intermediate entity to recognise its proportionate share of the underlying entity's assets, liabilities, income, and expenses — effectively consolidating line-by-line. Under Ind AS 110, a look-through approach is mandatory when the intermediate entity is a 'qualified investment entity' (QIE) — the parent must measure its investment in the QIE at fair value through profit or loss (Ind AS 110 paras 31–33) rather than consolidate, and the QIE in turn applies the look-through to its own investments (measuring at FVTPL) instead of consolidating its subsidiaries.

Card 39

A company has entered into a swap agreement to hedge its exposure to interest-rate fluctuations. Identify the applicable accounting standard, the hedge-types permitted, and the disclosure burden.

Applicable standard: Ind AS 109 / AS 32 'Financial Instruments: Recognition and Measurement' — hedging accounted for under the hedge accounting model. Permitted hedge types: (1) Fair value hedge (hedging exposure to changes in fair value of a recognised asset/liability), (2) Cash flow hedge (hedging exposure to variability in cash flows of a recognised asset/liability or forecast transaction), (3) Hedge of a net investment in a foreign operation. Disclosure burden (Ind AS 109 paras 26A–26C): nature and extent of hedging relationships, risk management objectives and strategy, qualification of hedging instruments, effectiveness measurement methodology, gain/loss recognised in P&L and OCI, and sensitivity/disclosure of hedge ineffectiveness.

Card 40

In advising a PSU on privatisation via strategic disinvestment, what four distinct professional domains must a CA cover, and give one key consideration under each domain.

Four domains and key considerations: (1) Legal/Corporate — compliance with Companies Act (share transfer restrictions, minority shareholder rights), SEBI take-over code, and parliamentary/ Cabinet approval thresholds for disinvestment. (2) Tax — stamp duty implications, capital-gains tax on transfer, withholding obligations, and availability of indexation or section 54 relief where applicable. (3) Accounting/Valuation — fair value determination per Ind AS 113, identification of hidden liabilities (PF/leave encashment, pension), and restatement of financials to reflect disposal group held for sale (Ind AS 105). (4) Labour/Social — employee retrenchment compensation (Chapter V-B of Industrial Disputes Act requires 300+ workers for prior govt permission), rehabilitation packages, and CSR continuity commitments.

Card 41

What is 'transfer-pricing documentation' and what are the three categories of information a non-resident assessee must maintain under Rule 10D/10DD?

Transfer-pricing documentation is the contemporaneous record maintained by the assessee to substantiate that international/specified domestic transactions were conducted at arm's length. Three broad categories: (1) Overall description of the enterprise — organizational structure, business description, intangible ownership, funding arrangements, and global business context. (2) Details of international/specified domestic transactions — parties, nature, terms, amount, currency, ALP study/methodology, comparables analysed, and adjustments made. (3) Information pertaining to Comparables — search criteria, primary/secondary databases used, listing of comparables with selection rationale, and financial details. Additionally, Form 3CEB must be furnished by the chartered accountant as a certifying report.

Card 42

A company is in advanced stages of insolvency and a resolution professional proposes to continue operations. Identify the protections available and the fiduciary duties that constrain the RP's actions.

Protections under IBC: (1) Moratorium u/s 14 prevents institution/pendency of suits, continuation of proceedings, and recovery of properties. (2) Security interests cannot be enforced during moratorium. (3) RP may continue business u/s 25(2)(e) and incur debts/administrative expenses. Fiduciary duties constraining the RP: (a) Act in good faith and in the best interest of all stakeholders (creditors, employees, members). (b) Exercise duties of reasonable diligence and competence (Section 25). (c) Obtain NCLT approval for material decisions (sale of undertaking, borrowing). (d) Avoid conflicts of interest and self-dealing. Violation attracts removal by NCLT,个人 liability, and possible criminal prosecution.

Card 43

Critically evaluate whether a 'Management Fee' paid to a foreign parent by an Indian subsidiary, calculated as a flat 5% of turnover, is likely to satisfy the arm's-length principle. State the reasoning and possible alternative benchmark.

A flat 5% of turnover is unlikely to satisfy the arm's-length principle because it bears no demonstrable relationship to actual services rendered — it is an arbitrary allocation that fails the 'benefit test' inherent in Rule 10B(2)(b). Arm's-length pricing requires cost-plus or comparable uncontrolled price methodology tied to verifiable services. Possible alternatives: (1) Cost-plus methodology — reimburse actual direct/indirect costs plus a reasonable markup (typically 5-10%) based on TNMM comparison with independent service providers. (2) CUP if comparable uncontrolled service fees are available. (3) Benchmark using comparable uncontrolled transactions (CUT) or profit-split where services are integral to value creation. The flat % approach may be rejected as not reflective of economic substance.

Card 44

In an IBS examination context, why is it considered inadequate to answer a case solely by citing provisions without applying them to the facts, and what structure should a good answer follow?

Citing provisions without application is inadequate because IBS tests professional judgement — knowing that a section exists is not sufficient; the examiner expects the candidate to diagnose the issue, identify the relevant law/standard, apply it to the specific facts, and arrive at a reasoned conclusion. A good answer structure: (1) Issue identification — what is the core problem? (2) Legal/factual matrix — what provisions, standards, and facts are relevant? (3) Analysis — apply law to facts, discuss alternatives, weigh evidence. (4) Conclusion — clear, reasoned recommendation or opinion with caveats. (5) Action steps — concrete next steps (disclosure, compliance, valuation, approval) for the professional to undertake.

Card 45

A listed company issues preferential shares to a promoter's entity at a price significantly lower than the prevailing market price. Enumerate the multiple regulatory violations this triggers, citing the relevant framework for each.

Regulatory violations: (1) Companies Act — Section 62(1)(c) requires preferential allotment to be by special resolution; if promoter is a related party, Section 42/188 may apply and prior board/shareholder approval is mandatory. (2) SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations — pricing must comply with prescribed methodology (market-based pricing using weighted average closing prices); deep discount attracts SEBI scrutiny. (3) SEBI (LODR) — related-party transaction disclosure, prior committee approval, and public announcement obligations. (4) SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations — if threshold crossed, open offer may be triggered. (5) Transfer pricing — may constitute an international transaction if priced below ALP, triggering TP documentation. (6) Insider trading — if information was material non-public, trading before disclosure violates SEBI (PID) Regulations.

Card 46

Explain the difference between 'joint operation' and 'joint venture' under Ind AS 11 and how this distinction affects the accounting treatment of a co-owned oil-and-gas pipeline.

Under Ind AS 11, a joint arrangement is classified based on the rights and obligations of the parties: a 'joint operation' gives each venturer rights to the assets and obligations for the liabilities relating to the arrangement; a 'joint venture' gives each venturer rights to the net assets (equity interest). For a co-owned pipeline: if the parties have direct rights to specific parts of the pipeline and obligations for their share of costs/liabilities (typical of cost-sharing/parallel production), it is a joint operation — each venturer recognises its share of assets, liabilities, revenue, and expenses directly in its financial statements. If instead the parties contribute to a separate vehicle and share profits/losses according to ownership %, it is a joint venture — equity-method accounting is applied rather than line-by-line recognition.

Card 47

In the context of an IBS case involving ESOPs, identify the accounting standard applicable, the measurement basis at grant date, and the disclosure requirement upon exercise.

Applicable standard: Ind AS 102 / AS 24 'Share-based Payments'. Measurement basis: at grant date, equity-settled awards are measured at fair value of the equity instruments granted (using option-pricing models such as Black-Scholes or binomial lattice); the fair value is recognised as an expense over the vesting period with a corresponding credit to equity (reserve). Upon exercise: (1) recognise share capital and securities premium at the exercise price received, (2) transfer the reserve accumulated under the ESOP scheme to the appropriate equity component, (3) disclose the number of options exercised, fair value of shares issued, and any modifications/cancellations during the period, as required by Ind AS 102 disclosure requirements.

Card 48

An Indian company is accused by its overseas parent of underperforming and requests a 'management audit'. Differentiate a management audit from a statutory audit, and state three value-added outputs a CA can deliver.

Difference: A statutory audit is a mandatory, standards-based examination of financial statements to express an opinion on true and fair view (SA 200/210), focusing on compliance and accuracy. A management audit is a voluntary, advisory exercise examining the efficiency and effectiveness of management policies, practices, and processes, going beyond financial compliance to operational and strategic performance. Three value-added outputs: (1) Gap analysis report comparing actual vs budgeted/peer performance with root-cause diagnostics. (2) Process-reengineering recommendations with quantified cost-savings or revenue-enhancement opportunities. (3) Governance improvement blueprint — strengthening internal controls, risk-register updates, and escalation protocols to restore stakeholder confidence.

Card 49

Discuss the consequences under the Companies Act, 2013 if a board approves a related-party transaction without board approval at a general meeting, and outline the corrective measures available.

Consequences: (1) Section 188 — related-party transactions (sale/purchase, leasing, consulting, etc.) require prior board AND shareholder approval (except in ordinary course at arm's length). Non-compliance renders the transaction voidable; directors are liable for losses arising from the contravention (Section 184/197). (2) Section 196 — remuneration to related-party directors also requires shareholder approval. (3) Potential penal provisions u/s 197(12) and contempt of NCLT directions. Corrective measures: (a) Ratify the transaction retrospectively by passing a special resolution at a general meeting (if shareholders agree). (b) Seek NCLT condonation of delay under Section 466 for procedural lapses. (c) Compensate the company for losses caused. (d) Ensure future transactions strictly comply with Sections 177(4)(d), 188, and disclosure norms u/s 134(3)(q).

Card 50

What are the essential features of a 'Resolution Plan' under Section 31 of the IBC, and what happens to security interests on approval of a resolution plan?

Essential features of a Resolution Plan under Section 31: (1) Approved by 66% of voting share of financial creditors and implemented by the successful resolution applicant. (2) Must provide for management and control of the corporate debtor by persons who are not promoters or defaulting related parties (Section 29A). (3) Must make provisions for implementation, supervision, and closure. On approval: (a) The resolution plan binds all stakeholders including secured creditors (Section 31(1)). (b) Security interests in respect of collateral security are extinguished upon approval — meaning secured creditors cannot enforce security post-approval; their claims are crystallised and payable according to the plan (Section 31(3)), and any appeal must be filed within 30 days (Section 61).

Card 51

A CA is engaged to review whether an entity qualifies as a 'qualified investment entity' (QIE) under Ind AS 110. State the three definitional criteria and explain why this classification matters for group reporting.

Three QIE criteria (Ind AS 110 paras B99-B100): (1) The entity obtains funds from one or more investors for the purpose of providing investment management services. (2) The entity commits to its investors that its business purpose is to invest funds solely for returns from capital appreciation, investment income, or both. (3) The entity measures and evaluates performance of substantially all of its investments on a fair-value basis. Why it matters: If parent qualifies as QIE, it is exempt from consolidating its subsidiaries and instead measures investments at fair value through profit or loss (FVTPL). This affects consolidated net assets, volatility of P&L, and disclosure. Downstream, the QIE must still apply the look-through approach — i.e., not consolidate its own subsidiaries but measure them at FVTPL — creating a cascade of non-consolidation through the chain.

Card 52

Identify five risk factors that could cause an auditor to modify an unqualified opinion on the financial statements of a going concern viable entity.

Five risk factors: (1) Negative operating and net current assets indicating inability to meet obligations as they fall due. (2) Key customer or supplier loss that materially impacts revenue/ supply chain with no viable replacement. (3) Non-compliance with loan covenants (e.g., debt-service coverage ratio breach) leading to acceleration of repayments. (4) Pending litigations or regulatory actions with reasonably possible material adverse outcome. (5) Dependency on government subsidies or bail-outs that are uncertain or withdrawn. Each factor requires evaluation under SA 570 — if substantial doubt remains unmitigated by management's plans, the auditor must add an Emphasis of Matter paragraph or qualify/disclaim, and under Companies Act Section 143(12) report the matter to the Central Government if severe.

Card 53

A corporate group wishes to shift its centre of主要 management and control to a low-tax jurisdiction to reduce its tax residence liability in India. Assess the validity of this strategy under Indian domestic law and international tax norms.

Under Indian law, a company is resident in India if its Place of Important Management and Control (PIC) is in India (Section 6(3)). Mere physical shift of board meetings to a low-tax jurisdiction is insufficient if real and substantial management continues in India. The India-UAE, India-Singapore, and other DTAAs include 'mutual agreement' and 'tie-breaker' clauses based on PIC tests. Moreover, GAAR (Chapter X-D) can disregard such arrangements if the main purpose is tax avoidance and the arrangement is 'impermissible'. India also introduced Controlled Foreign Company (CFC) rules and has adopted BEPS-aligned measures. Therefore, shifting residence without substantive change in management and operations is likely to be challenged; only a genuine relocation of key managerial personnel and decision-making substantiated by fact will hold.

Card 54

Describe the process and evidentiary burden of the 'General Anti-Avoidance Rule' (GAAR) investigation under Indian tax law, including who must approve invoking it.

Process: (1) TPO or AO suspects an 'impermissible avoidance arrangement' (IAA) as defined in Section 144. (2) The case is referred to the Approving Panel consisting of the Principal Commissioner (three members). (3) The Approving Panel must be satisfied, after hearing the assessee, that the arrangement is an IAA (lacks commercial substance, generates tax benefit as main purpose, involves abusive steps). (4) If satisfied, the panel issues approval; only then can the AO make adjustments under Section 144. Evidentiary burden: initially on the AO to demonstrate IAA characteristics; once prima facie established, burden shifts to the assessee to rebut. The taxpayer has rights of notice and hearing before the panel. Post-GAAR adjustment, the assessee may appeal to the APR (Appeals Range) and subsequently to the ITAT, but GAAR approval itself is a precondition.

Card 55

An IBS scenario involves a company that has recognised revenue from a long-term construction contract before obtaining the client's acceptance certificate. Which standard applies, and what should the professional conclude?

Applicable standard: Ind AS 115 / AS 7 'Revenue from Construction Contracts'. Under Ind AS 115, revenue is recognised over time if the customer simultaneously receives and consumes benefits, or the entity's performance creates/enhances an asset controlled by the customer, or the output has no alternative use and the entity has an enforceable right to payment. For construction contracts where completion-stage measurement is used, revenue recognition depends on stage of completion verified by independent engineer's certificates. Without client acceptance or certified stage-of-completion, recognising revenue prematurely violates the 'probable collection' and 'reliable measurement' criteria. The professional should conclude: revenue should be deferred to the extent not supported by verifiable progress; the misstatement must be corrected, and controls around revenue recognition need strengthening.

Card 56

In a corporate turnaround situation, why might a CA recommend a voluntary liquidation under Section 59 of the IBC rather than a resolution plan under Part II?

Voluntary liquidation (Section 59) may be recommended when: (1) No resolution plan is achievable within the statutory timeline (270 days max) and extending the moratorium is unlikely to succeed. (2) The asset value is maximised through orderly wind-down rather than distress sale. (3) There is no willing resolution applicant or the existing financial creditors prefer realisation over continuance. (4) The corporate debtor wishes to liquidate to discharge obligations transparently under supervisory NCLT oversight. Advantages: predictable waterfall under Section 53, finality of dissolution, and avoidance of protracted resolution uncertainty. Drawbacks: lower realisation values and potential stigma, hence recommendation is contingent on exhaustive prior attempt at resolution.

A multinational group runs a shared-services centre in India. Outline the four key transfer-pricing documentation requirements it must satisfy, and name the penalty for non-maintenance.

Four key documentation requirements (Rule 10D): (1) Overall description of the enterprise — organisational structure, business overview, intangibles, funding, and global context. (2) Description of international/specified domestic transactions — nature, terms, amounts, currency, ALP benchmarking methodology, and comparables. (3) Information pertaining to comparables — database sources, selection criteria, listing, adjustments. Card 58

Explain the impact of 'significant financing component' in a revenue contract under Ind AS 115, and describe how a CA should adjust the transaction price if such a component exists.

Ind AS 115 requires adjusting the transaction price for the time value of money when the contract contains a significant financing component — i.e., when payment is deferred significantly beyond performance transfer (or prepaid with significant delay). Impact: revenue recognised is the present value of consideration, with interest revenue accrued over the financing period (separately disclosed). Adjustment method: (1) Determine the discount rate — usually the rate the entity would borrow at (or use observable rate). (2) Discount the promised consideration to its present value at contract inception. (3) Recognise the difference between consideration and PV as interest income over the financing period. The standard presumes a financing component exists if payment term exceeds one year, but the practical expedient permits ignoring it if the period is ≤ 1 year.

Card 59

A company's management is considering a reverse merger to achieve a listed status faster than an IPO. Analyse the four principal regulatory and market risks associated with this route.

Four risks: (1) SEBI LODR compliance — the listed entity (acquirer) must meet continued listing requirements; if the merged entity's financials or governance were weak, the listed shell may face delisting or suspension. (2) Valuation opacity — determining the share-exchange ratio fairly is contentious; minority shareholders may challenge undervaluation of the listed entity or overvaluation of the unlisted target, inviting NCLT rejection. (3) Regulatory scrutiny — SEBI and stock exchanges closely examine such 'shell' listings for circular trading, manipulation, or circumvention of IPO norms; enhanced disclosure and lock-in commitments are imposed. (4) Integration risk — cultural, operational, and system incompatibilities often erode the anticipated synergy, damaging market perception and stock price post-merger.

Card 60

Describe the interaction between Section 44AD (presumptive taxation) and Section 44AF/AB where a CA is advising a small business with both speculative and non-speculative activities.

Section 44AD allows presumptive taxation for eligible assessees (turnover up to ₹2 crore) on business income — presumed at 6% (digital) / 8% (offline) of turnover. However, Section 44AD is not available for speculative business (Section 43(5)) or specified professions covered u/s 44AA(1). If the assessee has both speculative (e.g., commodity futures) and non-speculative income, Section 44AD can apply only to the non-speculative business; speculative income must be computed on actual books. Section 44AF applies to money-lending business (presumed 8% of gross receipts). Section 44AB mandates audit if turnover/profit exceeds specified limits regardless of presumptive scheme. A CA must ensure that the assessee maintains books u/s 44AA and gets audited u/s 44AB if the income shown under 44AD exceeds the upper limit of presumptive disclosure or if total income exceeds basic exemption limit.

Card 61

A listed company discloses an 'Operating Lease Obligation' in its notes despite having adopted Ind AS 116. What is the error, and what corrective entry is required?

Error: Under Ind AS 116, lessees recognise a Right-of-Use (ROU) asset and a lease liability for almost all leases; operating lease off-balance-sheet treatment is abolished (with limited exemptions for short-term ≤12 months and low-value assets). The company should have capitalised its operating leases on the balance sheet. Corrective entry: debit ROU Asset (at PV of remaining lease payments discounted using the incremental borrowing rate), credit Lease Liability. Subsequently, recognise depreciation of the ROU asset and interest expense on the lease liability (front-loaded expense pattern replacing straight-line rent). Also, restate comparative figures if the adoption is a change in accounting policy (Ind AS 116 transition provisions: modified retrospective or full retrospective). Adjust disclosures accordingly.
