c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}}

Symbols: \mu_0 = \text{permeability of free space} (4\pi \times 10^{-7}\;\text{T·m/A}), \quad \varepsilon_0 = \text{permittivity of free space} (8.854 \times 10^{-12}\;\text{C}^2/\text{N·m}^2), \quad c = \text{speed of light in vacuum}

Unit: m/s (dimensionally L T⁻¹)

When to use: Calculate the speed of any electromagnetic wave in vacuum from fundamental constants. Also used to find \mu_0 or \varepsilon_0 if one is known.

Conditions: Valid only in vacuum/free space. In a medium, replace \mu_0 → \mu and \varepsilon_0 → \varepsilon to get v = 1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}.

Common mistake: Using \mu_0 and \varepsilon_0 values in a medium instead of vacuum values. Remember n = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r \mu_r} ≈ \sqrt{\varepsilon_r} for non-magnetic media.

E_0 = c\,B_0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad E = c\,B

Symbols: E_0, B_0 = \text{amplitudes of electric and magnetic fields}; E, B = \text{instantaneous magnitudes}

Unit: E in V/m or N/C; B in Tesla (T); c in m/s

When to use: Convert between electric-field amplitude and magnetic-field amplitude of an EM wave. Directly links the two field strengths at every point.

Conditions: Holds at every instant and every point in a plane EM wave in vacuum (and non-conducting media with \mu_r ≈ 1). Both fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of propagation.

Common mistake: Writing E = B/c or swapping the relation. Always remember the larger quantity (E in V/m) equals c times the smaller one (B in T).

u_E = \frac{1}{2}\,\varepsilon_0\,E^2

Symbols: u_E = \text{energy density due to electric field}, \quad \varepsilon_0 = \text{vacuum permittivity}, \quad E = \text{electric field magnitude}

Unit: J/m³

When to use: Find the electric-field contribution to the total energy stored in an EM wave at any point.

Conditions: Instantaneous value; for sinusoidal waves use RMS or amplitude as specified. Holds in vacuum and linear isotropic media.

Common mistake: Forgetting the factor 1/2. Also confusing E (instantaneous) with E_0 (amplitude). In time-averaged form: u_{E,avg} = \tfrac{1}{4}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2.

u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\,\mu_0}

Symbols: u_B = \text{energy density due to magnetic field}, \quad B = \text{magnetic field magnitude}, \quad \mu_0 = \text{vacuum permeability}

Unit: J/m³

When to use: Find the magnetic-field contribution to the total energy stored in an EM wave.

Conditions: Same as u_E. For a plane EM wave in vacuum, u_E = u_B at every instant, so total u = 2u_E = 2u_B.

Common mistake: Writing u_B = \mu_0 B^2/2 (wrong placement of \mu_0). The correct form has \mu_0 in the denominator.

u = u_E + u_B = \varepsilon_0 E^2 = \frac{B^2}{\mu_0} \qquad (\text{instantaneous})

Symbols: u = \text{total EM energy density}

Unit: J/m³

When to use: Total energy density of an EM wave. Since u_E = u_B for a plane wave, either term doubled gives the total.

Conditions: Plane EM wave in vacuum. For time-averaged (sinusoidal): u_{avg} = \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{B_0^2}{\mu_0} = \varepsilon_0 E_{rms}^2.

Common mistake: Adding u_E and u_B but then forgetting they are equal, leading to u = \varepsilon_0 E^2 + B^2/\mu_0 = 2\varepsilon_0 E^2 incorrectly. They are equal, so the sum is just twice either one.

I = \frac{P}{A} = \frac{1}{2}\,c\,\varepsilon_0\,E_0^2 = c\,\varepsilon_0\,E_{rms}^2 = \frac{E_0 B_0}{2\,\mu_0}

Symbols: I = \text{intensity (average power per unit area)}, \quad P = \text{power}, \quad A = \text{area}, \quad E_0 = \text{field amplitude}, \quad E_{rms} = E_0/\sqrt{2}

Unit: W/m²

When to use: Relate intensity to field amplitudes; find E_0 or B_0 from a known intensity (e.g., sunlight ≈ 1.36 kW/m² at Earth).

Conditions: Time-averaged intensity for a sinusoidal plane wave. For non-sinusoidal or peak intensity, adjust factors of 1/2 accordingly.

Common mistake: Using E instead of E_0 (amplitude) with the 1/2 factor. If E given is RMS, drop the 1/2 and use I = c\varepsilon_0 E_{rms}^2.

S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}(\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{B}) \qquad (\text{Poynting vector})

Symbols: \mathbf{S} = \text{Poynting vector (direction of energy flow, magnitude = instantaneous intensity)}

Unit: W/m²

When to use: Determine the direction and magnitude of EM energy flow. Average Poynting vector gives intensity: I = S_{avg} = \tfrac{E_0 B_0}{2\mu_0}.

Conditions: Always true for EM waves. Direction is along the direction of wave propagation (\mathbf{E} × \mathbf{B}).

Common mistake: Writing S = \mu_0(\mathbf{E} × \mathbf{B}) or confusing with magnetic force formula. Note the 1/\mu_0 factor.

p_{rad} = \frac{I}{c} \quad (\text{absorbing}), \qquad p_{rad} = \frac{2I}{c} \quad (\text{perfectly reflecting})

Symbols: p_{rad} = \text{radiation pressure}, \quad I = \text{intensity}, \quad c = \text{speed of light}

Unit: Pa (N/m²)

When to use: Calculate the pressure exerted by an EM wave on a surface. Absorbing surface takes all momentum; perfectly reflecting surface reverses it, giving double the impulse.

Conditions: Normal incidence on a flat surface. For oblique incidence at angle θ, multiply by cos²θ for absorption and by appropriate geometric factor for reflection.

Common mistake: Always using p = I/c even for reflective surfaces. Reflecting surfaces experience twice the radiation pressure.

F = \frac{IA}{c} \quad (\text{absorbing}), \qquad F = \frac{2IA}{c} \quad (\text{reflecting})

Symbols: F = \text{force exerted by EM radiation}, \quad I = \text{intensity}, \quad A = \text{area of surface}

Unit: Newton (N)

When to use: Find the total force on an object of area A due to EM radiation. Used in problems involving solar sails, radiation force on small particles, etc.

Conditions: Surface normal to the direction of propagation. Completely absorbs → factor 1/c; completely reflects → factor 2/c.

Common mistake: Forgetting to multiply by area A. Force depends on both intensity and the area intercepting the wave.

I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2} \qquad (\text{isotropic point source})

Symbols: P = \text{power of source}, \quad r = \text{distance from source}

Unit: W/m²

When to use: Intensity at distance r from a point source radiating uniformly in all directions. Combined with I = \tfrac{1}{2}c\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 to find field amplitude at distance r.

Conditions: Source radiates equally in all directions (spherical waves). For directional sources, replace 4πr² with the effective beam area.

Common mistake: Using πr² (disk area) instead of 4πr² (sphere area). The wave spreads over the surface of a sphere.

\lambda_{min} = \frac{hc}{eV_0} \quad (\text{cut-off wavelength in X-ray tube})

Symbols: h = \text{Planck constant} (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s), \quad c = \text{speed of light}, \quad e = \text{electron charge}, \quad V_0 = \text{accelerating voltage}

Unit: m (wavelength)

When to use: Find the minimum wavelength (maximum energy) of X-rays produced when electrons are accelerated through potential V_0 and strike a target.

Conditions: All kinetic energy of electron converts into a single photon (best case). Real spectra have continuous Bremsstrahlung plus characteristic lines.

Common mistake: Using eV without converting volts to joules if mixing units, or writing \lambda_{min} = hc/(mv²) instead of hc/(eV). The energy comes from the electric potential: K = eV.

\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J} + \mu_0 \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} \qquad (\text{Maxwell–Ampère law})

Symbols: \mathbf{J} = \text{conduction current density}, \quad \tfrac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} = \text{time-varying electric field (displacement current term)}

Unit: SI consistent (A/m² for J, T·m/A for μ₀)

When to use: The complete Ampère law including displacement current. Explains how a changing electric field produces a magnetic field — the key mechanism enabling EM waves.

Conditions: Always valid. In regions with no conduction current (\mathbf{J} = 0), the displacement current term alone sustains the wave.

Common mistake: Omitting the displacement current term (\mu_0\varepsilon_0 \partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t) and using only the conduction-current form outside capacitors. Inside a charging capacitor, only displacement current exists.

I_d = \varepsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} \qquad (\text{displacement current})

Symbols: I_d = \text{displacement current}, \quad \Phi_E = \text{electric flux} = \int \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{A}

Unit: Ampere (A)

When to use: Calculate the effective 'current' between capacitor plates where no real charge flows but the electric field changes with time. Crucial for symmetry in Ampère's law.

Conditions: Valid anywhere there is a time-varying electric flux. In a parallel-plate capacitor with plate area A and changing voltage V: I_d = \varepsilon_0 A \, dE/dt.

Common mistake: Confusing displacement current with conduction current. I_d does not involve movement of charges; it is a mathematical construct that completes the circuit in Ampère's law.

E = E_0 \cos(kx - \omega t), \qquad B = B_0 \cos(kx - \omega t), \qquad \frac{E_0}{B_0} = c = \frac{\omega}{k}

Symbols: k = \text{wave number} = 2\pi/\lambda, \quad \omega = \text{angular frequency} = 2\pi f, \quad \lambda f = c

Unit: E in V/m, B in T, k in rad/m, ω in rad/s

When to use: Write the standard plane-wave expressions. Key relations: c = ω/k = λf, and E and B are in phase and perpendicular to each other and to propagation direction.

Conditions: Plane EM wave propagating in +x direction. For -x direction, change (kx - ωt) to (kx + ωt). Fields always perpendicular to propagation.

Common mistake: Writing E and B with different phases (e.g., sin for E and cos for B). In a plane EM wave, E and B are in phase. Also, don't write E = c/B — it's E_0 = cB_0 for amplitudes.
