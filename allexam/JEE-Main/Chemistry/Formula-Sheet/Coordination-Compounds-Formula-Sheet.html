[M(L)_n]^{charge}

Symbols: M = central metal ion; L = ligand; n = coordination number; charge = overall charge on complex

Unit: N/A

When to use: General representation of a coordination compound

Common mistake: Forgetting to include the charge on the complex or confusing oxidation state with charge

Oxidation State of Metal = Overall Charge − Sum of Charges of Ligands

Symbols: Overall Charge = charge on entire complex; Charges of Ligands = known charges of each ligand (e.g., Cl⁻ = −1, NH₃ = 0, CN⁻ = −1, en = 0)

Unit: N/A

When to use: Finding the oxidation state of the central metal in any coordination compound

Conditions: Ligand charges must be correctly known

Common mistake: Taking neutral ligands like NH₃, H₂O, en as having charge; forgetting that X⁻ type ligands contribute negative charge

Effective Atomic Number (EAN) = Z − OS + 2 × CN

Symbols: Z = atomic number of metal; OS = oxidation state of metal; CN = coordination number

Unit: N/A

When to use: Applying Sidgwick's EAN rule — complexes with EAN equal to nearest noble gas are generally more stable

Conditions: Valid for most octahedral and square planar complexes; not a strict law

Common mistake: Using wrong CN or forgetting to multiply by 2 (each ligand donates 2 electrons)

Crystal Field Splitting Energy (Octahedral): Δ₀

Symbols: Δ₀ = energy difference between t₂g and e_g sets in octahedral field

Unit: cm⁻¹ or kJ/mol

When to use: Predicting electronic configuration, magnetic properties, colour in octahedral complexes

Conditions: Applies to Oh symmetry; depends on ligand strength, metal charge, and 3d/4d/5d series

Common mistake: Confusing Δ₀ with Δ_t (tetrahedral splitting); Δ_t ≈ 4/9 Δ₀

Tetrahedral Splitting: Δ_t ≈ (4/9) Δ₀

Symbols: Δ_t = crystal field splitting in tetrahedral geometry; Δ₀ = splitting in octahedral geometry for same metal-ligand pair

Unit: cm⁻¹ or kJ/mol

When to use: Estimating tetrahedral splitting from known octahedral values

Conditions: Approximate relation; no pairing in tetrahedral due to small Δ_t

Common mistake: Assuming Δ_t > Δ₀ — it is always significantly smaller

Spectrochemical Series (weak → strong field): I⁻ < Br⁻ < S²⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < NO₃⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < CH₃CN < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < PPh₃ < CN⁻ < CO

Symbols: Ordered list of common ligands by field strength

Unit: N/A

When to use: Predicting whether a complex will be high-spin or low-spin, magnitude of Δ, colour

Conditions: Approximate order; some variation exists depending on metal and oxidation state

Common mistake: Memorising wrong order; treating it as absolute rather than approximate

Spin-Only Magnetic Moment: μ = √[n(n+2)] BM

Symbols: μ = magnetic moment in Bohr Magnetons (BM); n = number of unpaired electrons

Unit: BM (Bohr Magneton)

When to use: Calculating magnetic moment from unpaired electron count; identifying spin state

Conditions: Neglects orbital contribution — accurate for 3d metals in first approximation

Common mistake: Using total electrons instead of unpaired electrons; confusing BM with other units

Crystal Field Stabilisation Energy (CFSE) for Octahedral: CFSE = [−0.4x·n(t₂g) + 0.6x·n(e_g)] Δ₀ + P·p

Symbols: n(t₂g) = electrons in t₂g; n(e_g) = electrons in e_g; x = 1 for weak field, different for strong; P = pairing energy; p = number of pairs formed

Unit: Δ₀ or cm⁻¹

When to use: Quantifying stabilisation due to d-orbital splitting; comparing relative stability of configurations

Conditions: P is added only when pairing occurs beyond free-ion configuration

Common mistake: Ignoring pairing energy term when comparing high-spin vs low-spin; sign errors (t₂g is stabilising = negative)

CFSE for Tetrahedral: CFSE = [−0.4x·n(e) + 0.6x·n(t₂)] Δ_t + P·p

Symbols: n(e) = electrons in lower e set; n(t₂) = electrons in higher t₂ set

Unit: Δ_t or cm⁻¹

When to use: CFSE calculation for tetrahedral complexes

Conditions: Δ_t is small so tetrahedral complexes are usually high-spin

Common mistake: Swapping the coefficients 0.4 and 0.6 from octahedral case

Hybridization (VBT): CN=4 → sp³ (tetrahedral) or dsp² (square planar); CN=6 → sp³d² (outer orbital/high-spin) or d²sp³ (inner orbital/low-spin)

Symbols: sp³/dsp²/sp³d²/d²sp³ denote the orbitals混合 used by central metal

Unit: N/A

When to use: Predicting geometry and magnetic behaviour from VBT

Conditions: Strong field ligands favour inner orbital (d²sp³); weak field ligands favour outer orbital (sp³d²)

Common mistake: Assuming all CN=4 complexes are tetrahedral — square planar (dsp²) is common for d⁸ metals with strong ligands

Jahn-Teller Distortion: Occurs in octahedral complexes with uneven e_g occupancy (e.g., d⁹, high-spin d⁴, low-spin d⁷)

Symbols: e_g electrons = those in dz² and dx²−y² orbitals

Unit: N/A

When to use: Explaining distortion and elongation/compression in certain complexes

Conditions: More pronounced when e_g orbitals are asymmetrically filled; t₂g asymmetry causes weaker effect

Common mistake: Applying Jahn-Teller to all uneven configurations — only e_g asymmetry gives strong distortion

IUPAC Nomenclature Rules Summary: (1) Cation before anion; (2) Ligands named alphabetically before metal; (3) Anionic ligands end in 'o' (Cl→chlorido, OH→hydroxido, CN→cyanido); (4) Neutral ligands: NH₃→ammine, H₂O→aqua, CO→carbonyl, en→ethane-1,2-diamine; (5) Greek prefixes (di, tri, tetra…) for simple ligands; bis, tris, tetrakis for polydentate; (6) Metal name: English for cationic/neutral, Latin for anionic (Fe→ferrate, Cu→cuprate, Au→aurate, Ag→argentate); (7) Oxidation state in Roman numerals after metal name

Symbols: Applies to all coordination compounds

Unit: N/A

When to use: Writing correct IUPAC names and vice versa

Conditions: Must know ligand names and Latin metal names by heart

Common mistake: Using old 'n' spelling (nitro/nitrite) instead of new IUPAC (nitrito-N/nitrito-O); using Greek prefixes with polydentate ligands instead of bis/tris

Geometrical Isomerism — Octahedral [MA₆]: None; [MA₅B]: None; [MA₄B₂]: cis/trans; [M(AA)₂B₂]: cis/trans (AA = bidentate); [M(AA)₃]: No geom. isomerism but optical possible; [MABCD(EF)]: multiple isomers

Symbols: M = metal; A, B = monodentate ligands; AA = symmetrical bidentate; EF = unsymmetrical bidentate

Unit: N/A

When to use: Determining number of geometrical isomers

Conditions: cis = adjacent ligands; trans = opposite ligands

Common mistake: Forgetting that [M(AA)₃] shows optical isomerism but not geometrical; missing cis form of [M(AA)₂B₂]

Optical Isomerism — Octahedral: [M(AA)₃]ⁿ⁺ and cis-[M(AA)₂B₂]ⁿ⁺ show d/l (Δ/Λ) optical isomerism; trans-[M(AA)₂B₂] is optically inactive; [M(en)₃]³⁺ is dextrorotatory/levorotatory pair

Symbols: AA/en = bidentate symmetric ligand; Δ = right-handed propeller; Λ = left-handed propeller

Unit: N/A

When to use: Identifying chiral coordination complexes

Conditions: Complex must lack plane of symmetry and center of symmetry

Common mistake: Claiming trans-[M(AA)₂B₂] is optically active — it has a plane of symmetry

Linkage Isomerism: Occurs with ambidentate ligands (SCN⁻/NCS⁻, NO₂⁻/ONO⁻, CN⁻/NC⁻). M−SCN (thiocyanato-S) vs M−NCS (thiocyanato-N); M−NO₂ (nitro) vs M−ONO (nitrito)

Symbols: Ambidentate ligands bind through different donor atoms

Unit: N/A

When to use: Identifying linkage isomerism in complexes

Conditions: Ligand must have at least two possible donor atoms

Common mistake: Confusing linkage isomerism with geometrical isomerism

Ionisation Isomerism: Exchange of ligand between coordination sphere and counter ion. E.g., [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br ↔ [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄

Symbols: Requires both a dissociable counter ion and a ligand that can also act as counter ion

Unit: N/A

When to use: Identifying ionisation isomers and their different chemical tests

Conditions: Both isomers must have different ions in solution

Common mistake: Not checking that the exchanged species can actually exist as counter ion

Hydrate/Solvate Isomerism: Water molecules may be inside or outside coordination sphere. E.g., CrCl₃·6H₂O: [Cr(H₂O)₆]Cl₃ (violet), [Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O (green), [Cr(H₂O)₄Cl₂]Cl·2H₂O (dark green)

Symbols: Water of crystallisation vs coordinated water

Unit: N/A

When to use: Distinguishing hydrate isomers by conductivity and AgNO₃ test

Conditions: Only applicable when water is present in formula

Common mistake: Forgetting that water outside the sphere is written separately in formula

Coordination Isomerism: Both cation and anion are complex entities; ligands exchange between them. E.g., [Co(NH₃)₆][Cr(CN)₆] ↔ [Cr(NH₃)₆][Co(CN)₆]

Symbols: Requires both cationic and anionic complex ions

Unit: N/A

When to use: Identifying coordination isomers in salts of two complexes

Conditions: Both metal centres must be capable of forming complexes

Common mistake: Confusing with ionisation isomerism

18-Electron Rule: Total valence electrons = metal valence electrons + electrons donated by ligands + charge adjustment. Stable complexes often follow 18e⁻ rule

Symbols: Metal valence e⁻ = group number (for transition metals); each σ-donor ligand donates 2e⁻; each π-acceptor like CO donates 2e⁻ (σ) + accepts π-backbond

Unit: N/A

When to use: Predicting stability of organometallic and coordination complexes

Conditions: Most reliable for low-oxidation-state metal carbonyls and organometallics

Common mistake: Counting ligand donation wrong; not adjusting for ionic vs neutral ligand formalism

Colour in Coordination Compounds: λ_max = hc/ΔE where ΔE = Δ₀ (octahedral) or Δ_t (tetrahedral). Complementary colour observed = colour opposite on colour wheel to wavelength absorbed

Symbols: λ = wavelength of absorbed light; ΔE = energy gap between split d-orbitals

Unit: nm for λ; cm⁻¹ for wavenumber

When to use: Relating observed colour to crystal field splitting and ligand strength

Conditions: d-d transitions require unpaired d-electrons; d⁰ and d¹⁰ complexes are colourless (unless charge transfer)

Common mistake: Assuming all d-d transitions are allowed — they are Laporte-forbidden, giving weak colours; forgetting charge-transfer bands can be intense

Chelate Effect: Multidentate ligands form more stable complexes than equivalent monodentate ligands due to entropy increase (more particles released on chelation)

Symbols: Stability constant K_chelate > K_monodentate for comparable ligands

Unit: N/A

When to use: Explaining why EDTA, en form more stable complexes than NH₃

Conditions: Ring size 5 or 6 atoms most stable; larger or smaller rings less stable

Common mistake: Attributing chelate effect only to enthalpy — entropy is the dominant factor

STABILITY CONSTANTS: Stepwise: M + L ⇌ ML, K₁ = [ML]/([M][L]); M + L ⇌ ML₂, K₂ = [ML₂]/([ML][L]); Cumulative: β_n = K₁·K₂·…·K_n = [ML_n]/([M][L]ⁿ)

Symbols: K₁, K₂, … = stepwise formation constants; β_n = cumulative (overall) formation constant

Unit: N/A (dimensionless in thermodynamic convention)

When to use: Quantifying complex stability; predicting extent of complex formation

Conditions: Usually K₁ > K₂ > K₃… due to statistical and electrostatic factors

Common mistake: Confusing β_n with K_n; using wrong power of [L] in cumulative expression

Magnetic Behaviour Classification: Diamagnetic = all electrons paired (μ = 0); Paramagnetic = unpaired electrons present (μ > 0); Ferromagnetic = parallel alignment of moments (rare in simple complexes)

Symbols: μ = spin-only magnetic moment; n = unpaired electrons

Unit: BM

When to use: Classifying complexes based on magnetism

Conditions: Temperature affects paramagnetism (Curie's Law: χ ∝ 1/T)

Common mistake: Assuming all complexes with unpaired electrons are strongly paramagnetic — transition metal paramagnetism is usually weak

d-Orbital Splitting Diagrams — Quick Reference: Octahedral: t₂g (lower, 3 orbitals: dxy, dyz, dzx); e_g (upper, 2 orbitals: dz², dx²−y²). Tetrahedral: e (lower, 2: dz², dx²−y²); t₂ (upper, 3: dxy, dyz, dzx). Square Planar: dx²−y² (highest) > dxy > dz² > dxz, dyz (lowest)

Symbols: Orbital labels refer to angular dependence of d-orbitals

Unit: N/A

When to use: Drawing and interpreting crystal field splitting diagrams

Conditions: Square planar typically for d⁸ metals with strong field ligands (e.g., Pt²⁺, Pd²⁺, Ni²⁺ with CN⁻)

Common mistake: Drawing tetrahedral splitting with same label order as octahedral — note the reversal of e and t₂ labels

Common Coordination Numbers and Geometries: CN=2 → linear (e.g., [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺); CN=4 → tetrahedral (sp³) or square planar (dsp²); CN=5 → trigonal bipyramidal or square pyramidal; CN=6 → octahedral (most common)

Symbols: CN = coordination number

Unit: N/A

When to use: Predicting geometry from coordination number

Conditions: CN=4 has two possible geometries — determined by metal d-electron count and ligand field strength

Common mistake: Assuming all CN=4 are tetrahedral — d⁸ strong-field complexes are square planar

Common Ligand Denticity: Monodentate: Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O, OH⁻, CO, NO; Bidentate: en (ethane-1,2-diamine), ox²⁻ (oxalate), gly⁻ (glycinate), acac⁻ (acetylacetone); Tridentate: dien, TREN; Tetradentate: Trien, porphine; Hexadentate: EDTA⁴⁻

Symbols: Number of donor atoms per ligand molecule

Unit: N/A

When to use: Determining coordination number from ligand types

Conditions: EDTA can act as hexadentate but sometimes binds in lower denticity

Common mistake: Counting en as monodentate — it is bidentate (2 donor N atoms)

Werner's Postulates: (1) Metals show two types of valencies: primary (ionisable, = oxidation state) and secondary (non-ionisable, = coordination number); (2) Secondary valencies have fixed directional character → definite geometry; (3) Primary valencies are satisfied by anions, secondary by neutral molecules or anions within coordination sphere

Symbols: Primary valency = oxidation state; Secondary valency = coordination number

Unit: N/A

When to use: Foundational theory for understanding coordination compounds

Conditions: Historical theory — superseded by VBT/CFT but conceptually important

Common mistake: Confusing primary and secondary valencies
