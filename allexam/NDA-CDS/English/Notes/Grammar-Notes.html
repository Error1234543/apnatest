NDA & CDS English Grammar – Complete Study Notes

NDA & CDSEnglishNotes

1. Spotting Errors (Error Detection)

Error spotting is one of the most important grammar question types in the NDA & CDS English paper. Each question presents a sentence divided into three parts (Part A, Part B, Part C) with an optional fourth part (Part D = 'No error'). The candidate must identify which part contains the grammatical error, if any.

Common error categories tested:
- Subject-Verb Agreement errors (subject and verb not agreeing in number or person)
- Tense errors (incorrect tense usage or inconsistency)
- Preposition errors (wrong preposition or omitted preposition)
- Article errors (missing or incorrect article)
- Concord errors (pronoun-antecedent disagreement)
- Parallelism errors (lack of parallel structure in coordinate items)
- Redundancy errors (double negatives or unnecessary words)
- Wrong word usage (confusion between similar words like affect/effect, lie/lay, etc.)

Key rules:
- A compound subject joined by 'and' generally takes a plural verb. Example: 'Rahul and Arjun are coming.' However, when the two nouns refer to a single entity or idea, use singular: 'Bread and butter is my breakfast.'
- Subjects joined by 'or', 'nor', 'either...or', 'neither...nor' take a verb that agrees with the nearest subject. Example: 'Neither the boys nor the girl is here.'
- Indefinite pronouns like everyone, someone, nobody, each, either, neither are singular. Example: 'Everyone has submitted his/her report.'
- 'One of the + plural noun + relative clause' takes a singular verb because the head is 'one'. Example: 'One of the students who are present has won the prize.'
- Collective nouns (team, committee, jury, government) take singular verbs when acting as a unit and plural when members act individually.
- Expressions like 'along with', 'together with', 'as well as', 'in addition to' do not change the number of the subject. Example: 'The captain, along with his players, is going.'
- Numbers: amounts of money, distances, time periods treated as singular when considered as a unit. Example: 'Ten rupees is too little.' 'Five kilometers is a long distance.'
- Inverted sentences: locate the true subject after the verb. Example: 'On the table lies a book.' (singular 'lies' agrees with 'a book')

Strategy: Read the entire sentence, identify the subject first, check verb agreement, then scan for prepositions, articles, tense consistency, and parallel structure.

2. Subject-Verb Agreement

Subject-Verb Agreement is the cornerstone of English grammar and a frequent testing point in NDA & CDS.

Core Principles:
- A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb.
- The verb must agree with the SUBJECT, not with any object or phrase between them.

Specific Rules with Examples:
1. Each, every, either, neither, anyone, everybody, somebody — always singular: 'Each of the candidates has given his details.'
2. Many a + singular noun takes singular verb: 'Many a soldier has died for the country.'
3. A number vs. The number: 'A number of students are waiting' (plural sense) vs. 'The number of students is increasing' (specific quantity).
4. Both, several, few, many — take plural verbs: 'Both of them are correct.'
5. None can be singular or plural depending on context: 'None of the water was spilled' (uncountable → singular) vs. 'None of the boys were present' (countable → plural).
6. All, some, most, half — agree with their object: 'All of the fruit is ripe' / 'All of the fruits are ripe.'
7. Coincidental subjects (joined by 'and' but referring to one person/thing): 'The poet and philosopher is here.' (one person) vs. 'The poet and the philosopher are here.' (two people)
8. Relative pronouns (who, which, that): verb agrees with the antecedent. 'He is one of those students who always study hard.' (plural 'study' agrees with 'students') vs. 'He is the only one of those students who always studies hard.' (singular 'studies' agrees with 'one')
9. Interrogative subjects (who, what, which): usually singular unless context demands plural. 'Who is calling?' 'What makes you think so?'
10. Expressions of measurement, sum, time, distance as a whole take singular: 'Ten miles is a long trek.' 'Half a dozen eggs is enough.'
11. Plural-looking nouns that are singular: news, economics, physics, mathematics, politics, gossips, billiards — all take singular verbs. 'News is not always true.'
12. Singular-looking nouns that are plural: people, police, cattle, poultry, clergy — take plural verbs. 'The police are investigating.'
13. The + adj./participle denoting a class takes plural: 'The rich are privileged.' 'The retired are being pensioned.'
14. Noun phrases with 'a couple of', 'a lot of', 'lots of' — treat the object's countability. 'A couple of days were wasted.' 'A lot of work is pending.'

3. Tenses

Tense refers to the form of a verb that indicates the time of an action or state. NDA & CDS focus on correct usage rather than rote naming of all twelve tenses.

Present Tenses:
- Simple Present: habits, general truths, scheduled events. 'The sun rises in the east.' 'The train leaves at 6 p.m.'
- Present Continuous: actions happening now, temporary situations, future arrangements. 'She is writing a letter.' 'I am meeting him tomorrow.'
- Present Perfect: completed action with present relevance, life experiences, actions just finished. 'I have finished my work.' 'Has she visited Delhi?' Note: 'have/has + been to' means visited and returned; 'have/has + gone to' means still there.
- Present Perfect Continuous: action started in past and continues to present. 'I have been waiting for two hours.' Use duration expressions (for, since). Do not use with stative verbs (know, believe, belong).

Past Tenses:
- Simple Past: completed action at definite past time. 'He went yesterday.' Time markers: yesterday, last week, in 1947, ago.
- Past Continuous: ongoing past action, interrupted by simple past. 'I was reading when he called.' Also used for simultaneous actions: 'While I was cooking, she was cleaning.'
- Past Perfect: action completed before another past action. 'I had finished the work before he arrived.' Key: past perfect comes FIRST in sequence.
- Past Perfect Continuous: ongoing action up to a point in the past. 'They had been traveling since morning.'

Future Tenses:
- Simple Future: decisions, predictions, promises. 'I will help you.' 'It will rain tomorrow.'
- Future Continuous: ongoing action at a future time. 'At 8 p.m., I will be watching the match.'
- Future Perfect: action completed before a future time. 'By next year, I will have graduated.'
- Future Perfect Continuous: duration up to a future point. 'By December, she will have been working here for five years.'

Important Notes:
- Stative verbs (know, believe, understand, belong, possess, seem, contain) are generally NOT used in continuous forms.
- Sequence of tenses: main clause past → subordinate clause also past. 'He said that he was ill.'
- Historical present: simple present used for vivid narration of past events. 'Columbus discovers America in 1492.'
- Future in the past (would): 'He said he would come.' Used in indirect speech.
- Had better, would rather: 'You had better go now.' 'I would rather stay home.' These are followed by base form.

4. Voice (Active and Passive)

Voice shows whether the subject performs the action (Active) or receives it (Passive).

Rules for Conversion:
1. The object of active becomes the subject of passive.
2. The main verb changes to appropriate form of 'be' + past participle (V3).
3. The subject of active becomes the agent introduced by 'by' (optional in passive).
4. Tense remains the same; only the 'be' auxiliary changes.

Conjugation Pattern:
- Simple Present: He writes a letter. → A letter is written by him.
- Present Continuous: He is writing a letter. → A letter is being written by him.
- Present Perfect: He has written a letter. → A letter has been written by him.
- Simple Past: He wrote a letter. → A letter was written by him.
- Past Continuous: He was writing a letter. → A letter was being written by him.
- Past Perfect: He had written a letter. → A letter had been written by him.
- Simple Future: He will write a letter. → A letter will be written by him.
- Future Perfect: He will have written a letter. → A letter will have been written by him.
- Modal: He can write a letter. → A letter can be written by him.

Cases Where Passive Is NOT Possible:
- Intransitive verbs without an object: 'He sleeps.' 'She arrives.' However, some intransitive verbs taking an internal object (accomplish, die, happen, break out, take place) CAN form passive: 'The work was accomplished.' 'An accident happened.' → This is idiomatic and limited.
- Verbs of perception, possession, opinion: 'I see him.' 'She has beauty.' 'I know the answer.' These generally resist passive transformation.
- When active has two objects (ditransitive): both can become passive subject. 'He gave me a pen.' → 'I was given a pen by him' OR 'A pen was given to me by him.' The version with the person as subject is preferred.
- Impersonal constructions with reporting verbs (say, believe, know, consider, report): 'People say that he is honest.' → 'He is said to be honest.' Pattern: Subject + be + past participle + to-infinitive. Tense of infinitive matches original: present perfect active → perfect infinitive passive ('He is believed to have stolen the money.')
- Headlines and informal writing often omit the agent.

Exceptions to Remember:
- 'Let' imperative: 'Let the door be closed.' (Passive with 'let' uses bare infinitive after 'be')
- Causative: 'Have/Get something done.' 'I had my hair cut.'
- Passive with modal + perfect: 'The work should have been done.' (implies criticism)

5. Narrative / Reported Speech (Direct and Indirect)

Reported speech reports what someone said without quoting exact words. It requires systematic changes in tense, pronouns, and time/place expressions.

Tense Shift Rules (Backshifting):
- Simple Present → Simple Past: 'I am happy.' → He said he was happy.
- Present Continuous → Past Continuous: 'I am working.' → She said she was working.
- Present Perfect → Past Perfect: 'I have finished.' → He said he had finished.
- Present Perfect Continuous → Past Perfect Continuous: 'I have been waiting.' → She said she had been waiting.
- Simple Past → Past Perfect: 'I went there.' → He said he had gone there.
- Past Continuous → Past Perfect Continuous: 'I was sleeping.' → She said she had been sleeping.
- Past Perfect and Past Perfect Continuous → Remain unchanged.
- Simple Future (will) → Conditional (would): 'I will come.' → He said he would come.
- Would, could, might, should, ought to, used to → Remain unchanged.
- Must → Had to (when expressing obligation in past); must (when expressing strong deduction/present certainty).

Important Exceptions (No Backshift):
- Universal truths and scientific facts: 'The teacher said, "The Earth revolves around the Sun."' → He said the Earth revolves around the Sun.
- When the reporting verb is in present/future: 'She says, "I am busy."' → She says she is busy.
- When the reported statement refers to a future event from a past viewpoint: use 'would' for future-in-the-past.

Pronoun Changes:
- First person pronouns (I, me, my, mine, we, us, our) change according to the subject of the reporting clause.
- Second person pronouns (you, your, yours) change according to the object of the reporting clause.
- Third person pronouns remain unchanged.

Time and Place Expression Changes:
- Today → that day; Tomorrow → the next day / the following day; Yesterday → the previous day / the day before
- Here → there; Now → then; This → that; These → those
- Ago → before; Last week → the previous week; Next year → the following year

Types of Reporting:
- Statements: Use 'that' (optional). Affirmative: He said that he was tired.
- Questions:
- Yes/No questions: Use 'if' or 'whether'. He asked if I was coming.
- Wh- questions: Retain the question word. He asked where I was going.
- In reported questions, the word order is affirmative (subject before verb), not interrogative.
- Commands and Requests: Use infinitive. He told me to sit down. She requested him to help.
- Exclamations and Advice: Use 'that' or rephrase. 'What a beautiful flower!' → He exclaimed that it was a beautiful flower.
- Suggestions: 'Let us go.' → He suggested that they should go.

6. Modals

Modal auxiliaries express necessity, possibility, permission, ability, and advice. They are followed by the base form of the main verb (without 'to', except for 'ought to' and 'have to').

Primary Modals and Their Uses:
- Can: ability, informal permission, possibility. 'I can swim.' 'Can I come in?' Note: 'can' for permission is informal; in formal writing, prefer 'may'.
- Could: past ability, polite request, possibility. 'I could run fast when young.' 'Could you help me?' 'It could rain today.'
- May: formal permission, possibility (more certain than 'might'). 'May I come in?' 'It may rain.'
- Might: remote possibility, hesitant permission. 'It might rain tomorrow.' 'You might be right.'
- Must: strong obligation, logical deduction (present), necessity. 'You must wear a uniform.' 'He must be tired.' Negative 'must not' = prohibition; 'need not' = absence of obligation.
- Shall: formal future (with I/we), suggestions/offers (with you). 'Shall we go?' 'I shall return.' Modern usage increasingly replaces 'shall' with 'will'.
- Should: advice, mild obligation, expectation. 'You should study harder.' 'He should be here by now.'
- Will: future certainty, willingness, promise, habit. 'I will call you.' 'Water will freeze at 0°C.'
- Would: past habit, conditional, polite request, reported speech. 'He would walk every day.' 'Would you open the door?' 'She said she would come.'
- Ought to: moral duty, advisability (similar to 'should'). 'You ought to respect your elders.'
- Need: semi-modal (used without 'to' in questions/negatives). 'Need I come?' 'You need not worry.'
- Dare: semi-modal (rare). 'Dare he ask?' 'She dared not speak.'

Modal Perfect (have + past participle) — for past speculation/regret:
- Must have + V3: strong deduction about the past. 'He must have forgotten.'
- Should have + V3: expectation not fulfilled. 'You should have studied.'
- Could have + V3: past ability/possibility not realized. 'I could have won.'
- Might have + V3: uncertain past possibility. 'She might have missed the bus.'
- Would have + V3: conditional past. 'I would have helped.'
- Need not have + V3: unnecessary past action done anyway. 'You need not have cooked.'

Common Errors:
- 'Can' vs. 'Could': 'Can' for present ability; 'could' for past ability.
- 'Must' vs. 'Have to': 'must' = internal obligation; 'have to' = external obligation.
- Double modals are incorrect in standard English: never say 'I will can go' or 'I must should do it.'

7. Prepositions

Prepositions show relationships between a noun/pronoun and other words. NDA & CDS frequently test fixed prepositional combinations.

Important Fixed Prepositions with Verbs:
- Accuse of, Agree with/to/upon, Apologize for, Belong to, Complain of/against, Consist of, Depend on/upon, Die of/from, Expect from/of, Fear for, Hope for, Insist on/upon, Laugh at, Look after/at/for, Listen to, Object to, Rely on, Remind of/about, Resist doing, Succeed in, Suspect of, Thank for, Wait for, Wish for

Important Fixed Prepositions with Adjectives:
- Afraid of, Aware of, Best known for, Capable of, Content with, Different from/than, Famous for, Good at/bad at, Illiterate in, Interested in, Jealous of, Proud of, Responsible for, Similar to, Sure of, Tired of, Worried about

Important Fixed Prepositions with Nouns:
- Ability to, Advantage over, Advice on/about, Affection for, Alternative to, Attachment to, Cause of, Cause for, Chance of, Difference between/among, Effect of, Face of, Faith in, Fondness for, Idea of, Knowledge of, Love for, Means of, Need for, Occupation with, Opinion about/of, Possession of, Preference for, Reason for, Result of, Root cause, Success in/ at

Commonly Confused Prepositions:
- Among vs. Between: 'among' for three or more; 'between' for two (also for distinct items even if more than two)
- In vs. Into vs. Upon: 'in' = position; 'into' = movement toward inside; 'upon' = on top of, or immediately after
- On vs. Upon: largely interchangeable; 'upon' is more formal; 'upon' used in certain idioms (upon reflection)
- To vs. For: 'give to' (receiver) vs. 'buy for' (beneficiary)
- Since vs. For: 'since' + point in time; 'for' + duration
- Among vs. Amid: 'amid' used with uncountable nouns or abstract situations (amid chaos, amid difficulties)

Errors to Avoid:
- Preposition before relative pronoun: 'to whom', 'for which' (formal); avoid ending sentences with prepositions in formal writing, though acceptable in informal usage.
- Incorrect collocation: 'discuss about' is wrong; 'discuss' takes direct object. 'Entrance into' is correct; 'entrance to' also used. 'Married with' is wrong; use 'married to'. 'Consult about' is wrong; 'consult' takes direct object.

8. Conjunctions and Correlative Constructions

Conjunctions join words, phrases, or clauses. Understanding their usage is critical for error spotting and sentence improvement.

Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS):
- For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So
- Used to join equal elements: words, phrases, or independent clauses.
- When joining two independent clauses, a comma precedes the conjunction.

Subordinating Conjunctions:
- Time: when, while, as, before, after, until, since, as soon as, wherever, whenever
- Cause/Reason: because, since, as, so that, in order that
- Condition: if, unless, provided that, providing, as long as, on condition that
- Contrast/Concession: although, though, even though, whereas, while, despite the fact that
- Purpose: that, so that, in order that
- Result: so...that, such...that
- Comparison: as...as, so...as, than

Correlative Conjunctions (must be used in pairs):
- Either...or: 'Either you come or I go.' Verb agrees with the nearer subject.
- Neither...nor: 'Neither Rahul nor Priya is here.' Verb agrees with the nearer subject.
- Not only...but also: 'Not only did he study, but he also scored well.' Inversion after 'not only' when it begins the clause.
- Both...and: 'Both Ram and Shyam are here.' Takes plural verb.
- Whether...or: 'Whether you come or not, I will go.'
- Scarcely/Hardly...when: 'Scarcely had he reached home when it began to rain.' Inversion required; 'when' follows, not 'than'.
- No sooner...than: 'No sooner had the train left than it started raining.' Inversion required; 'than' follows, not 'when'.

Common Errors:
- Using 'despite of' — 'despite' never takes 'of'; use 'in spite of'.
- 'Although' and 'but' together: Do NOT use both in the same sentence. 'Although he is poor, he is happy.' (NOT: Although he is poor, but he is happy.)
- 'Because' and 'so' together: 'Because he was ill, he did not come.' (NOT: Because he was ill, so he did not come.)
- 'Since' implying both time and cause can create ambiguity; prefer 'because' for causation when clarity matters.
- 'As' can mean 'because' or 'while'; context determines meaning.

9. Articles

Articles (a, an, the) are determiners placed before nouns. Error-spotting and sentence improvement frequently test article usage.

Indefinite Articles (a, an):
- 'A' before consonant sounds; 'an' before vowel sounds.
- Critical point: it is SOUND, not spelling, that matters.
- 'A university' (sound /j/), 'an uncle' (sound /ʌ/)
- 'An hour' (silent h, sound /aʊ/), 'a honest man' is WRONG → 'an honest man'
- 'A one-time event' (sound /w/), 'an X-ray' (sound /eks/)
- Use 'a/an' with singular countable nouns mentioned for the first time, or to indicate one among many.
- Use 'a' with titles/units when used appositively: 'He is a Dr. Sharma.' 'He works as a Mr.' (old-fashioned)
- Do not use article before names of meals unless qualified: 'I had lunch.' But 'I had a good lunch.'
- Do not use article before languages, academic subjects, abstract nouns, or uncountable nouns in general sense: 'English is easy.' 'Honesty is the best policy.'

Definite Article (the):
- Use 'the' with things mentioned before, or understood from context: 'I saw a dog. The dog was black.'
- With superlatives and ordinals: 'the best', 'the first', 'the only'
- With unique things: 'the sun', 'the moon', 'the earth', 'the Himalayas', 'the Ganges', 'the Pacific Ocean'
- With plural nouns referring to a specific group: 'the rich', 'the poor', 'the unemployed'
- With musical instruments: 'She plays the piano.'
- With rivers, seas, mountain ranges, deserts, gulfs, plural islands: 'the Nile', 'the Sahara', 'the Alps', 'the Bahamas'
- With countries having 'Republic', 'States', 'Kingdom', 'Sultanate': 'the USA', 'the UK', 'the UAE'
- Do NOT use 'the' with most country names, city names, lake names, single peaks: 'India', 'Mumbai', 'Lake Superior', 'Mount Everest'
- With adjectives denoting a class: 'the dead', 'the blind', 'the French'
- 'The' with adjective to mean a class of people: 'the poor need help.'
- 'A the' before adjectives with superlative sense used figuratively: 'a the greatest pleasure.' (rare/archaic)

Zero Article (no article):
- Meals, languages, subjects, sports, languages, abstract concepts in general sense
- Names of people, planets, most lakes, single mountains, towns, streets, parks
- Before 'next', 'last', 'every' + time noun: 'next week', 'last year', 'every day'
- After 'so' with adjective: 'so good', 'so beautiful'

10. Clauses (Relative, Noun, and Adverbial)

A clause is a group of words containing a subject and a verb. Clauses are essential for understanding complex sentences tested in NDA & CDS.

Relative (Adjective) Clauses:
- Introduce relative pronouns: who, whom, whose, which, that. Also relative adverbs: when, where, why.
- Defining (restrictive) clauses: essential to meaning; no commas. 'The man who called yesterday is my uncle.' Use 'that' for things and people in defining clauses.
- Non-defining (non-restrictive) clauses: extra information; set off by commas. Cannot use 'that'. 'My uncle, who lives in Mumbai, is visiting.'
- Who = subject case; Whom = object case; Whose = possessive. 'The man whose car was stolen is angry.'
- Which = things/animals; That = people/things (defining only).
- Preposition + which/whom: formal structure. 'The house in which I live...' 'The man with whom I spoke...'
- Relative pronoun as object may be omitted: 'The book (that) I bought is good.'

Noun Clauses:
- Function as subject, object, or complement. Introduced by that, whether, if, wh- words.
- Subject position: 'That he failed is surprising.' 'What she said hurt me.'
- Object position: 'I know that he is honest.' 'She asked whether I was coming.'
- Complement position: 'The problem is that we lack funds.' 'The question is who will lead.'
- 'That' as conjunction does not serve as any element within the clause.
- 'If' vs. 'Whether': 'I don't know if/whether he will come' (both ok in informal); 'Whether he comes or not' (only 'whether'); after preposition, only 'whether': 'It depends on whether...'

Adverbial Clauses:
- Function as adverbs; modify verb, adjective, or adverb.
- Time: when, while, as, before, after, until, since, as soon as
- Reason/Cause: because, since, as, owing to the fact that
- Purpose: that, so that, in order that
- Condition: if, unless, provided that, as long as
- Concession: although, though, even though, however, no matter how
- Comparison: as...as, so...as, than, the...the
- Result: so...that, such...that
- Manner: as, as if, as though
- Important: Don't confuse adverbial clauses of time/concession with relative clauses. 'I remember the day when we met' (relative) vs. 'I remember the day when we met' (adverbial — same form, different analysis).

11. Participles, Gerunds, and Infinitives

These non-finite verb forms are high-yield topics for NDA & CDS grammar questions.

Participles:
- Present participle (-ing form): expresses active, ongoing action. Used in continuous tenses and as adjectives. 'The crying baby needs food.' 'I saw him crossing the road.'
- Past participle (V3): expresses completed action. Used in perfect tenses and passive. 'The broken window needs repair.' 'He has finished the job.'
- Perfect participle: 'Having + V3'. Shows action completed before another past action. 'Having finished the work, he went home.'
- Participle phrases can act as reduced relative clauses: 'The man who is standing there' → 'The man standing there.'
- Absolute participle construction: noun/participle combination not syntactically linked to main clause. 'Weather permitting, we will go.' 'He sat silent, his eyes closed.'
- Common error: dangling participle. 'Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful.' (Who was walking? Misplaced modifier.) Correct: 'Walking down the street, I noticed the beautiful trees.'

Gerunds:
- Verb+ing functioning as a noun. Can be subject, object, or complement. 'Swimming is good exercise.' 'I enjoy reading.' 'His hobby is collecting stamps.'
- Gerunds follow certain verbs: enjoy, finish, avoid, consider, deny, suggest, keep, practice, admit, risk, miss, fancy, imagine, cannot help, feel like.
- Gerunds follow prepositions: 'He is good at painting.' 'She left without saying goodbye.'
- Gerunds after certain expressions: 'It is no use crying.' 'There is no point in arguing.'

Infinitives:
- 'To + base verb'. Can function as noun, adjective, or adverb.
- Purpose infinitive: 'I came to see you.' (Why did I come? To see you.)
- Infinitive after certain verbs: want, hope, plan, decide, agree, refuse, offer, learn, pretend, fail, manage, promise, expect, intend, tend, afford.
- Object + to-infinitive: 'I want you to come.' 'She asked me to help.' 'He advised me to wait.'
- Wh- + infinitive: 'I don't know what to do.' 'She told me where to go.'
- Do/be/have + bare infinitive (without 'to'): 'What did you do?' 'I had him clean the room.' 'Let me go.'
- To as preposition (not infinitive marker): amount to, lead to, object to, accustomed to, used to (past habit), be/get used to + ing. These take gerund, not infinitive.
- Common error: splitting infinitive — traditionally discouraged but accepted in modern usage: 'To boldly go.' Often better avoided in formal exams.
- Gerund vs. Infinitive differences:
- Stop smoking (quit the habit) vs. Stop to smoke (pause in order to smoke)
- Remember locking the door (memory of past action) vs. Remember to lock the door (duty to perform in future)
- Try eating apple (experiment) vs. Try to eat apple (make effort)
- Regret saying vs. Regret to inform

12. Conditionals

Conditionals express the result of a possible (or imagined) condition. NDA & CDS tests all four standard types plus mixed/alternative forms.

Type 0 (Zero Conditional — General Truths):
- Structure: If + present simple, present simple
- Example: 'If you heat ice, it melts.' Used for scientific facts and universal truths.

Type 1 (First Conditional — Real/Possible Future):
- Structure: If + present simple, will/can/may + base verb
- Example: 'If it rains, I will stay home.'
- Variants with can, may, must, should instead of will: 'If you study hard, you can pass.'
- Alternative: Imperative in main clause: 'If you see him, tell him to call me.'

Type 2 (Second Conditional — Unreal Present/Hypothetical):
- Structure: If + past simple, would/could/might + base verb
- Example: 'If I were rich, I would travel the world.' (Note: 'were' used for all persons in formal English — 'If I were,' 'If he were')
- Used for imaginary situations contrary to present fact.
- 'Were' inversion (formal/literary): 'Were I rich, I would travel.' (Remove 'if' and invert subject and were)

Type 3 (Third Conditional — Unreal Past):
- Structure: If + past perfect, would have/could have/might have + past participle
- Example: 'If I had studied, I would have passed.' Used for regrets about the past.
- Inversion: 'Had I studied, I would have passed.'

Mixed Conditionals:
- Past condition → present result: 'If I had taken that job (past), I would be living in London now (present).' Structure: If + past perfect, would + base verb.
- Present condition → past result: 'If I were smarter (present/general trait), I would have passed (past).' Structure: If + past simple, would have + V3.

Other Conditional-Like Structures:
- Unless = if...not. 'Unless you study, you will fail.' (= If you do not study...)
- Provided that / Providing that = on condition that.
- As long as / So long as = on the condition that.
- Had it not been for = If it had not been for. 'Had it not been for your help, I would have failed.'
- But for = If it were not for / If it had not been for.
- Would rather + past simple for present unreal preference: 'I would rather you came tomorrow.'
- Would sooner = would rather.

13. Degrees of Comparison

Degrees of comparison show the difference in quality, quantity, or degree among things.

Regular Forms:
- Positive: good, bad, far, little, many, much, late, old
- Comparative: better, worse, farther/further, less, more, more, later, elder/eldest
- Superlative: best, worst, farthest/furthest, least, most, most, latest, eldest

Rules:
- One syllable: add -er/-est. 'tall → taller → tallest'
- Two syllables ending in -y: change -y to -i and add -er/-est. 'happy → happier → happiest'
- Two or more syllables: use more/most. 'beautiful → more beautiful → most beautiful'
- Irregular adjectives: good/better/best, bad/worse/worst, far/farther(farthest)/further(furthest), little/less/least, many/more/most, much/more/most

Structures:
- Comparative with 'than': 'Ram is taller than Shyam.'
- Superlative with 'the': 'Ram is the tallest in the class.'
- Double comparative (increasing degree): 'The more you study, the smarter you become.'
- 'Comparative + and comparative' (gradual increase): 'It is getting colder and colder.'
- 'Increasingly + positive adjective': 'The weather is becoming increasingly hot.'
- Comparative preceded by 'the' when referring to two specific items: 'Of the two brothers, Rahul is the taller.'
- 'No sooner...than' and 'scarcely...when' already covered under conjunctions.
- 'Rather than' vs. 'Other than': 'Rather than' = instead of; 'other than' = except.

Common Errors:
- Using 'more' or '-er' twice: 'more taller' is wrong; use 'taller'.
- Using double superlative: 'most tallest' is wrong.
- Using comparative without 'than' when comparing two: 'Ram is taller than Shyam' (NOT 'Ram is taller Shyam').
- Confusing 'farther' (physical distance) with 'further' (degree/figurative): both acceptable for distance in modern usage.
- Incorrect superlative with 'of the two': use comparative with 'the'. 'Of the two options, this is the better one.'

14. Sentence Improvement and Transformation

Sentence improvement asks the candidate to replace a underlined portion with the best alternative, or to identify the portion that improves the sentence. This tests grammar, style, and usage simultaneously.

Key Areas Tested:
- Grammatical correctness: subject-verb agreement, tense consistency, proper preposition use, correct article, correct pronoun case.
- Conciseness: remove redundancy. 'In my personal opinion, I think...' → 'I think...'
- Formality: avoid colloquialisms in formal writing. 'He ain't coming' → 'He is not coming.'
- Word order: correct placement of adverbs and modifiers. 'Only' position matters: 'I only ate an apple' vs. 'I ate only an apple' (different meanings).
- Parallel structure: items in a list must share the same grammatical form. 'She likes swimming, hiking, and to cycle' → 'She likes swimming, hiking, and cycling.'

Transformations (occasionally tested):
- Simple to Complex: 'Because he was ill, he did not come.' → 'Owing to his illness, he did not come.' (Complex to Simple)
- Complex to Compound: 'Though he is poor, he is happy.' → 'He is poor but happy.'
- Compound to Simple: 'He worked hard and he succeeded.' → 'Working hard, he succeeded.'
- Affirmative to Negative (and vice versa): Use equivalent negative expression. 'He is the best player' → 'No player is as good as he.'
- Exclamatory to Assertive: 'What a brave soldier he is!' → 'He is a brave soldier.'
- Assertive to Exclamatory: 'It is a wonderful sight.' → 'What a wonderful sight it is!'
- Interrogative to Assertive: 'Is it not true that honesty is the best policy?' → 'Honesty is the best policy.'
- Active to Passive (covered separately)
- Direct to Indirect Speech (covered separately)

Transformation Rules:
- Double negative = affirmative: 'I am not unaware' → 'I am aware.'
- Comparative = superlative: 'No other player is as good as Rahul' → 'Rahul is the best player.'
- Positive = superlative: 'Rahul is the best player' → 'No other player is as good as Rahul.'
- Universal affirmative → particular affirmative: 'All birds can fly' → 'Some birds can fly.' (Note: logically weaker)
- Universal negative → particular negative: 'No fish swims on land' → 'Some fish do not swim on land.'
- Negative universal → positive universal: 'None is immortal' → 'All are mortal.'

15. Commonly Confused Words and One-Word Substitution

These two sub-topics appear regularly in the NDA & CDS English paper.

Commonly Confused Words:
- Affect (verb: influence) vs. Effect (noun: result) — 'The drug affects the body.' 'The effect was immediate.'
- Accept (receive) vs. Except (exclude) — 'Accept the gift except for the broken ones.'
- Advice (noun) vs. Advise (verb) — 'Take my advice.' 'I advise you to rest.'
- Its (possessive) vs. It's (it is / it has) — 'The dog wagged its tail.' 'It's raining.'
- Their (possessive) vs. There (place) vs. They're (they are) — 'Their books are there. They're reading.'
- Who (subject) vs. Whom (object) — 'Who called?' 'To whom did you speak?'
- Lie (recline, no object) vs. Lay (place, requires object) — 'I lie down.' 'She lays the book down.' Past: lay/lain vs. laid/laid.
- Less (uncountable) vs. Fewer (countable) — 'Less water' vs. 'Fewer bottles.'
- Between (two) vs. Among (three or more) — 'Choose between A and B.' 'Divide among the children.'
- Ensure vs. Insure vs. Assurance — 'Ensure safety.' 'Insure the car.' 'Give assurance.'
- Principal (main/headmaster) vs. Principle (rule/truth) — 'The principal principle.'
- Discrete (separate) vs. Discreet (careful/tactful) — 'Discrete units.' 'Be discreet.'
- Complement (complete) vs. Compliment (praise) — 'The wine complements the meal.' 'Compliment her on her dress.'
- Stationary (not moving) vs. Stationery (writing materials) — 'The car was stationary.' 'Buy stationery.'

Important facts

  • NDA & CDS English paper is part of the General Ability Test (GAT) in NDA (200 marks) and a separate paper in CDS. Total grammar and usage questions form a significant portion alongside vocabulary and comprehension.
  • Spotting errors and sentence improvement are the most commonly tested formats — practise identifying the error type quickly (agreement, tense, preposition, article, etc.).
  • Subject-Verb Agreement is the single highest-weight grammar topic. Master collective nouns, indefinite pronouns, inverted sentences, and compound subjects.
  • In reported speech, remember: tense backshifts, pronoun shifts, time/place shifts. Unchanging tenses include historical facts and reporting verbs in present/future.
  • Active-Passive transformation: only transitive verbs can become passive. Intransitive verbs generally cannot, except a small set of idiomatic cases.
  • Modal perfects (must have, should have, could have, might have) are frequently tested for expressing past deduction, regret, possibility, and inability.
  • 'Scarcely/Hardly...when' and 'No sooner...than' require inversion and specific pairings — mixing 'when' with 'no sooner' or 'than' with 'scarcely' is a common error trap.
  • Articles: remember it is SOUND not spelling that governs a/an. Words starting with /j/ (university, unity) take 'a'; words with silent /h/ (hour, honor) take 'an'.
  • One-word substitutions commonly tested are Greek/Latin-derived terms related to character traits, literary devices, philosophical concepts, and historical/archetypal references.
  • Conditionals: Type 2 uses 'were' for all persons in formal English. Type 3 uses past perfect in if-clause and would have + V3 in main clause. Mixed conditionals combine time frames.

Formulas and relationships

Not applicable — English Grammar is a language-usage subject, not a formula-based discipline. Mastery comes through rule application and pattern recognition, not algebraic computation.

Common mistakes

  • Writing 'despite of' — 'despite' never takes 'of'. Correct: 'Despite the rain' or 'In spite of the rain.'
  • Using 'a' before vowel sounds and 'an' before consonant sounds based on spelling alone — e.g., 'a hour' (wrong, should be 'an hour') or 'an university' (wrong, should be 'a university').
  • Double negation: 'I don't need no help' — in standard English, use 'I don't need any help.'
  • Using 'although' and 'but' in the same sentence: 'Although he is poor, but he is happy' — use only one connector.
  • Subject-verb disagreement with 'each of', 'every one of', 'none of': 'Each of the boys have...' should be 'Each of the boys has...'
  • Confusing 'less' with 'fewer': 'less people' should be 'fewer people' (countable). 'Less water' is correct (uncountable).
  • Incorrect use of 'who' vs. 'whom': 'Who did you give the book to?' is acceptable informally but 'Whom did you give the book to?' is formally correct.
  • Misusing 'can' for past ability — use 'could' for general past ability. 'I can run fast when I was young' is wrong; use 'could'.
  • Using gerund after verbs that take infinitive and vice versa — memorize the list: enjoy/finish/avoid + ing; want/decide/hope + to inf.
  • Dangling participles: 'Walking down the street, the trees looked beautiful' — trees cannot walk. Correct: 'Walking down the street, I noticed the beautiful trees.'
  • Using 'marry with' — 'marry' is transitive: 'She married him' (correct), NOT 'She married with him'.
  • Incorrect article with superlative in comparison of two: 'the most tallest of the two' — should be 'the taller of the two'.
  • Adding 'of' after transitive verbs: 'discuss about', 'enter into the room' (unless meaning become member), 'reach to Delhi' — remove 'of/about/to'.
  • Wrong conditional pairing: 'No sooner...when' should be 'No sooner...than'; 'Scarcely...than' should be 'Scarcely...when'.
  • Confusing 'its' (possessive) with 'it's' (it is / it has). 'Its' never takes an apostrophe.
