1. Subject-Verb Agreement: Core Rules and Tricky Cases

Subject-verb agreement is the single most frequently tested grammar rule in the NDA & CDS spotting-error section. The fundamental principle is simple: a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. However, the exam tests this at a sophisticated level by disguising the true subject. Always locate the actual subject by ignoring prepositional phrases between the subject and the verb. For example, in 'The box of chocolates is on the table,' the subject is 'box' (singular), not 'chocolates,' so the verb must be 'is,' not 'are.'

When subjects are joined by 'and,' they normally take a plural verb: 'Ravi and Meena are going.' But if the two nouns refer to the same person or thing, or are considered a single unit, use a singular verb: 'The poet and philosopher is here' (one person), 'Bread and butter is my breakfast' (single item).

Subjects joined by 'or/nor/neither...nor/either...or' follow the proximity rule: the verb agrees with the subject closest to it. Thus, 'Neither the boys nor the girl was happy' is correct, while 'Neither the boy nor the girls was happy' is wrong.

Indefinite pronouns such as each, every, either, neither, one, another, someone, anybody, nobody, anyone, and no one are always singular: 'Each of the students has submitted the assignment.' Note the phrase 'each of' — the noun after 'of' may be plural, but 'each' governs the verb. Conversely, 'both,' 'few,' 'many,' 'several,' and 'all' when referring to a plural noun take plural verbs.

Collective nouns like team, committee, jury, government, audience, and army can take either singular or plural verbs depending on meaning. If the group acts as a single unit, use singular: 'The committee has decided.' If members act individually, use plural: 'The committee are arguing among themselves.'

Words ending in -ics such as physics, economics, mathematics, politics, and gymnastics are singular when they denote academic subjects but plural when referring to activities or traits: 'Physics is my favourite subject,' but 'His politics are questionable.'

'One-third of the population live here' uses a plural verb because 'population' in this context refers to individual people, whereas 'One-third of the land is barren' uses singular because 'land' is uncountable.

Inverted sentences beginning with 'here' or 'there' place the subject after the verb: 'There are many reasons for this,' not 'There is.' Similarly, interrogative sentences require the verb to agree with the actual subject, not the question word: 'What are your names?' rather than 'What is your names?'

The expressions 'the number of' takes a singular verb ('The number of applicants is increasing'), while 'a number of' takes a plural verb ('A number of applicants have arrived').

Common exam traps include sentences like 'The list of items are long' (error: should be 'is') and 'One of the boys are missing' (error: should be 'is' because 'one' is the subject). Always ask yourself: who or what is actually performing the action?

2. Pronoun: Reference, Case, and Agreement Errors

Pronoun errors in spotting-error questions typically involve three categories: incorrect case, vague reference, and agreement mismatches.

Case errors occur when a subjective pronoun is used where an objective one is required, or vice versa. A reliable test is to remove the other part of the compound subject/object and see which pronoun sounds natural. In 'He and I went to the market,' removing 'He and' leaves 'I went,' confirming the subjective case. In 'The teacher gave the book to him and me,' removing the other object leaves 'to me,' confirming the objective case. A common mistake is using 'me' in subject position: 'Me and Rohan went there' is wrong; it should be 'Rohan and I.'

Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves) must refer back to the subject of the same clause. They cannot serve as substitutes for simple objective pronouns. 'The manager called for myself' is wrong; it should be 'The manager called for me.' Similarly, 'Let us inform the director ourselves' is acceptable only when emphasizing that 'we' will do it personally; otherwise 'us' suffices. In NDA & CDS exams, a sentence like 'Between you and myself, the plan is flawed' is an error — it should read 'Between you and me.'

Vague reference happens when a pronoun could logically refer to more than one antecedent. 'Rahul told Suresh that he had made a mistake' is ambiguous — 'he' could be Rahul or Suresh. The sentence should be rephrased to identify clearly: 'Rahul told Suresh that Rahul had made a mistake.' Examiners flag such ambiguities as errors.

Agreement between pronoun and antecedent requires matching number and gender. A singular antecedent demands a singular pronoun: 'Every student must bring his or her ID card' (or 'their ID card' in modern usage, though traditional grammar prefers 'his or her'). Plural antecedents demand plural pronouns: 'The students submitted their projects.' An error appears in sentences like 'The team submitted its reports last week' when the context clearly intends the individual members; however, 'its' is not technically wrong if the team is viewed as a single entity — this distinction matters for exam interpretation.

Demonstrative pronouns (this, that, these, those) must agree in number with their referent. 'These types of problems is difficult' is wrong — it should be 'are difficult' because 'these types' is plural. Similarly, 'That information are correct' is wrong; it should be 'is correct' because 'information' is uncountable.

Relative pronouns also feature in pronoun errors. Use 'who' for persons, 'which' for things and animals, and 'that' for both (especially in restrictive clauses). 'The man which came yesterday' is wrong — it should be 'who.' Using 'that' after a non-restrictive clause set off by commas is also incorrect: 'My brother, that lives in Delhi, is visiting' is wrong; it should be 'who.'

Possessive pronouns (whose, whom, who) must match their antecedent. 'The company whose CEO resigned' is correct because 'company' is treated as an entity, though some traditional grammars accept 'the company that.' The possessive 'its' (without apostrophe) is the pronoun form; 'it is' or 'it has' contracts to 'its' only in the possessive sense — a frequent error is writing 'its' where 'it is' is meant.

3. Tense Consistency and Correct Usage

Tense errors are tested extensively in NDA & CDS. The central principle is tense consistency within a sentence or passage: once a time frame is established, subsequent verbs must remain within that frame unless there is a logical reason to shift.

A common error is inappropriate shifting from past to present or vice versa without justification. In 'He enters the room and sits down. He knows that he will succeed,' the first sentence is in past narrative style but mixes tenses illogically. If narrating a past event: 'He entered the room and sat down. He knew that he would succeed.'

Present perfect versus simple past is a major testing point. Use simple past for actions completed at a definite time in the past, and present perfect for actions that occurred at an indefinite time or have relevance to the present. 'I saw him yesterday' (definite time → simple past). 'I have seen him' (indefinite time → present perfect). An error appears in 'I have met him last week' — 'last week' is a specific past time, so it should be 'I met him last week.' Similarly, 'Did you finish your work yet?' is wrong; it should be 'Have you finished your work yet?' because 'yet' pairs with present perfect.

Past perfect is used for an action completed before another past action. The earlier action takes past perfect: 'When I reached the station, the train had left.' An error in sequence: 'The train had left when I reached the station' reverses the intended meaning — it suggests the train left first, which is actually correct. But 'I had reached the station and the train left' implies the reaching happened before the leaving — check the logical order carefully.

Future-in-the-past construction requires attention. When the main clause is in the past tense, the future relative to that past point uses 'would' rather than 'will': 'He said that he would come tomorrow.' An error is 'He said that he will come tomorrow' — 'will' should become 'would,' and 'tomorrow' may need adjustment depending on context.

Stative verbs (know, believe, understand, belong, contain, seem, appear, cost, weigh, own, possess, love, hate, prefer, remember, forget) are generally not used in continuous/progressive forms. 'I am knowing the answer' is wrong; it should be 'I know the answer.' Exceptions exist for special meanings: 'I am thinking about the problem' (meaning 'considering') is acceptable, but 'I think the problem' (meaning 'believe') is not progressive.

Present perfect continuous versus present perfect: 'I have been waiting for two hours' emphasizes duration up to now, while 'I have waited for two hours' states the fact. Both can be correct depending on emphasis, but an error arises when the context demands one over the other. For example, 'I have written the report for three hours' suggests the activity is still ongoing and unfinished, which may conflict with the meaning intended.

Subjunctive mood often appears in formal writing and fixed expressions. After verbs like suggest, recommend, insist, demand, propose, and after 'it is essential/important/necessary that,' use the base form of the verb regardless of the subject: 'The doctor recommended that he rest for a week' (not 'rests'). 'It is important that she be informed immediately' (not 'is informed'). This subjunctive is frequently tested.

Mixed conditionals and hypothetical past often generate tense errors: 'If I had studied harder, I would have passed' (third conditional). Mixing tenses incorrectly: 'If I studied harder, I would have passed' is wrong for a past hypothetical; it should be 'had studied.'

4. Articles: Definite, Indefinite, and Zero Article

Article errors are subtle but frequently appear in spotting-error questions. The indefinite articles 'a' and 'an' are used with singular countable nouns mentioned for the first time or used generically. Use 'a' before consonant sounds and 'an' before vowel sounds — the deciding factor is sound, not spelling. Thus, 'a university' (sounds like 'yoo-niversity'), 'a one-time event' (sounds like 'won'), 'an hour' (the 'h' is silent), 'an MBA degree' (sounds like 'em-bee-ay'), and 'an honest attempt' (silent 'h').

The definite article 'the' is used with specific or previously mentioned nouns, unique entities, superlatives, ordinals, and certain geographical names. 'The sun rises in the east.' 'The Ganga flows through North India.' 'The Taj Mahal is in Agra.' Use 'the' before rivers, seas, oceans, mountain ranges, deserts, and countries with plural or federal names: 'the Pacific Ocean,' 'the Alps,' 'the United States,' 'the Netherlands.' Do not use 'the' before most individual mountains, lakes, or single-word country names: 'Mount Everest,' 'Lake Superior,' 'India,' 'France.'

Zero article is used with plural and uncountable nouns in generic statements: 'Cats are domestic animals' (not 'The cats'). 'Music soothes the soul' (generic, zero article). 'Human beings need water.' However, add 'the' when referring to a specific group: 'The cats in my neighbourhood are friendly.'

Names of meals, languages, academic subjects, and diseases generally take zero article: 'We had breakfast at eight,' 'She studies English,' 'Diabetes is a chronic disease.' But add 'the' when the noun is specified: 'The breakfast we had yesterday was delicious.'

With ordinal numbers and superlatives, use 'the': 'the first chapter,' 'the best student.' However, omit 'the' when a possessive adjective already specifies: 'My first lesson,' 'Her best friend.' An error appears in 'She is the my best friend' — double specification is wrong.

Instrumental and mode-of-transport constructions use zero article: 'by bus,' 'by train,' 'by car,' 'on foot.' 'He goes to school by bus.' Adding an article here is incorrect: 'by the bus' changes the meaning entirely.

Certain fixed expressions with 'go' take no article: 'go to school, go to college, go to hospital, go to church, go to prison, go to market, go to sea.' However, adding 'the' changes the meaning: 'go to the school' means visiting the building, not attending as a student.

Institutional uses with 'in' sometimes differ: 'in hospital' (British usage meaning admitted as a patient) vs. 'in the hospital' (American usage, more general). NDA & CDS typically follows British conventions.

Geographical names that require no article: continents, most countries, states, cities, streets, planets, most lakes, single mountains. Names requiring 'the': island groups, straits, canals, channels, peninsulas, gulf, bays, river systems, desert regions, mountain ranges, oceanic bodies.

5. Prepositions: Fixed Combinations and Common Errors

Preposition errors test knowledge of fixed collocations and the relationship between verbs, adjectives, nouns, and the prepositions they govern. There is no universal logic to memorise every combination, but several systematic patterns exist.

Verb + preposition combinations are a major testing ground. Each verb has a specific preposition it governs: 'accuse of,' 'admit to,' 'apologize for,' 'believe in,' 'compare with/to,' 'depend on,' 'disagree with,' 'explain to,' 'insist on,' 'laugh at,' 'listen to,' 'look after,' 'look at,' 'wait for,' 'warn against.' An error like 'He accused me for stealing' is wrong — it should be 'accused me of stealing.' 'She insisted to go' is wrong — it should be 'insisted on going.'

Adjective + preposition combinations are equally important: 'afraid of,' 'angry with (person)/at (thing),' 'aware of,' 'capable of,' 'collaborate with,' 'different from/than,' 'fond of,' 'interested in,' 'good at,' 'proud of,' 'responsible for,' 'similar to.' 'He is good in maths' is a classic error — it should be 'good at maths.' 'She is afraid from snakes' is wrong — it should be 'afraid of snakes.'

Noun + preposition collocations: 'advantage of,' 'attention to,' 'cause of,' 'difference between,' 'effect of,' 'interest in,' 'knowledge of,' 'reason for,' 'respect for.' 'The difference between John and James' requires 'between' for two items and 'among' for three or more. An error: 'The difference among the two options' is wrong.

Time and place prepositions require careful distinction. Use 'in' for months, years, centuries, seasons, and parts of the day: 'in January,' 'in 1990,' 'in the morning.' Use 'on' for days and dates: 'on Monday,' 'on 15th August.' Use 'at' for specific clock times and fixed points: 'at 5 o'clock,' 'at noon,' 'at night.' An error: 'I will meet you on 5 pm' should be 'at 5 pm.'

'During' indicates a period spanning the whole duration of an event; 'throughout' is similar but stronger. 'In' is used with future time expressions after 'before' and 'after': 'I will finish this in two days' means within two days; 'I will finish this after two days' means two days from now. Confusion between 'during' and 'for' is common: 'He stayed there during three months' is wrong — use 'for' with a duration.

Agent versus instrument: 'by' marks the agent in passive voice ('The book was written by Tagore'), while 'with' marks the tool ('He cut the paper with a knife'). An error: 'The window was broken with the boy' is wrong — it should be 'by the boy.'

Phrasal prepositions (as well as, in spite of, on account of, by virtue of, with regard to) are tested as single units. Replace them with their simpler counterparts correctly: 'in spite of the rain' not 'although the rain.'

6. Parallelism: Balanced Structure in Sentences

Parallelism requires that elements in a sentence that serve the same grammatical function must be expressed in the same form. This is one of the most commonly tested concepts in NDA & CDS spotting-error questions because even slight imbalances feel unnatural to a native speaker's ear.

Items in a list or series must share the same grammatical structure. 'She likes reading, to swim, and hiking' is wrong because three infinitive/gerund/participial forms are mixed. It should be 'She likes reading, swimming, and hiking' (all gerunds) or 'She likes to read, to swim, and to hike' (all infinitives).

Correlative conjunctions demand parallel structure on both sides. With 'either...or': 'Either you come or you stay' (both imperative base forms). With 'neither...nor': 'Neither the teacher nor the students were present' — note that the verb agrees with the nearer subject, and both subjects share parallel noun forms. 'He is neither intelligent nor hardworking' (both adjectives). An error: 'He is neither intelligent nor works hard' breaks parallelism — 'works hard' is a verb phrase where an adjective is expected.

In balanced clauses connected by 'but,' 'and,' 'or,' or ';' — maintain symmetry: 'She wanted to leave, but she did not know how to ask' is parallel in structure. An error: 'She wanted to leave, but not knowing how to ask' leaves the second clause incomplete.

Infinitive and gerund pairing errors often occur. 'His hobby is collecting stamps' matches subject complement in gerund form. But 'His hobby is to collect stamps' is also acceptable. Mixed usage like 'His hobbies are reading books and to watch films' is wrong — it should be 'reading books and watching films.'

Comparative structures also require parallelism. 'She is smarter than him' is informal; formally, 'She is smarter than he is' maintains parallelism. In NDA & CDS, both may appear, but 'than he does' vs. 'than him does' creates structural imbalance.

When using 'such...as' and 'so...that,' maintain parallel structure. 'Such a man as respects others' is correct; 'Such a man as respecting others' is wrong.

In correlative constructions involving infinitives, omit 'to' in the second element when it is shared: 'She want to sing and dance' (not 'to sing and to dance'), but 'She wants to sing and to dance' is also acceptable when emphasis is needed.

Participial phrases at the beginning of a sentence must modify the subject that follows. 'Walking through the park, the flowers looked beautiful' is a dangling modifier — the flowers were not walking. Correct: 'Walking through the park, I noticed the beautiful flowers.' This overlaps with modifier errors but is frequently grouped under parallelism in exam contexts.

Balancing clauses of equal weight after semicolons is essential: 'He was tired; he continued working' is balanced. 'He was tired; continuing to work' is structurally mismatched.

7. Modifier and Dangling Modifier Errors

Modifiers are words, phrases, or clauses that describe or qualify another word in the sentence. Misplaced, dangling, or ambiguous modifiers produce logical absurdities and are standard sources of error in spotting-error questions.

A misplaced modifier is placed too far from the word it modifies, creating ambiguity or a false impression. 'She nearly drove the car for five hours' suggests she almost drove but did not drive; the intended meaning is likely 'She drove the car nearly for five hours' or better, 'She drove the car for nearly five hours.' 'I saw a man on the hill with a telescope' is ambiguous — did the man have the telescope, or did I? Rephrase for clarity: 'I saw a man on the hill, and he had a telescope' or 'Using a telescope, I saw a man on the hill.'

A dangling modifier occurs when the word being modified does not appear in the sentence at all. 'After finishing the homework, the television was turned on' implies the television finished the homework. Correct: 'After finishing the homework, he turned on the television.' 'Having waited for an hour, the bus finally arrived' wrongly suggests the bus waited. Correct: 'Having waited for an hour, we saw the bus arrive.'

Participle phrases must have a logical subject. In 'Running quickly, the finish line came into view,' the finish line is not running. Correct: 'Running quickly, I saw the finish line come into view.'

Adjective phrases and appositives must also be positioned correctly. 'The captain, accompanied by his officers, marched into the hall' is correct because 'the captain' follows immediately. 'Marching into the hall, accompanied by his officers, was the captain' is awkward but grammatically acceptable; however, exam setters may flag it as misplacement depending on the option.

Reduced relative clauses often cause confusion. 'The book published last year has sold well' — 'published last year' correctly modifies 'book.' But 'The book, published last year, has sold well' adds unnecessary non-restrictive commas. Both can be correct depending on whether the information is essential or additional.

Prepositional phrases functioning as modifiers must attach to the nearest appropriate noun. 'The soldier with the rifle stood guard near the border wearing a camouflage jacket' is ambiguous about who is wearing the jacket. Reposition: 'Wearing a camouflage jacket, the soldier with the rifle stood guard near the border.'

Words such as 'only,' 'almost,' 'nearly,' 'even,' and 'just' are limiting modifiers that must be placed directly before the word they modify. 'He almost drove five miles' suggests he nearly drove but did not; 'He drove almost five miles' is correct for the intended meaning. 'Only she knows the answer' (no one else knows) vs. 'She only knows the answer' (she knows it but cannot explain it) — position determines meaning entirely.

Comparative modifiers must compare like with like. 'Her salary is higher than a teacher' compares salary to a teacher, which is illogical. Correct: 'Her salary is higher than that of a teacher' or 'Her salary is higher than a teacher's.'

8. Conjunctions, Subordinators, and Clause Structure

Conjunctions join words, phrases, or clauses. Errors in their use involve incorrect pairing, redundancy, or misuse of subordinating conjunctions.

Coordinating conjunctions (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so — remembered by FANBOYS) join independent clauses of equal weight. A comma precedes the coordinating conjunction when it joins two independent clauses: 'She studied hard, but she failed the exam.' Do not use a comma before 'and' when joining only two predicates: 'He ran and jumped' (no comma needed). Using a comma incorrectly here is a minor error often tested.

Correlative conjunctions must be paired correctly and followed by parallel structure. 'Not only...but also': 'Not only did he finish the task, but he also helped others.' Note the inversion after 'not only' when it begins the sentence. 'Either...or' and 'neither...nor' require the verb to agree with the nearer subject: 'Neither the boys nor the girl is ready.' 'Both...and' always takes a plural verb: 'Both the teacher and the students are present.'

Subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent clauses. Common subordinators include because, since, as, although, though, even though, unless, if, when, while, whereas, until, after, before, as soon as, provided that, in case, so that, in order that. 'Because' and 'since' can sometimes be interchangeable, but 'because' indicates cause and 'since' often indicates time — though both can indicate cause in formal usage. An error: 'As he was ill, so he did not come' — 'as' and 'so' are redundant together. Use either 'As he was ill, he did not come' or 'He was ill, so he did not come.'

'Although' and 'but' cannot appear in the same sentence structure. 'Although he is poor, but he is honest' is wrong. Correct: 'Although he is poor, he is honest' or 'He is poor, but he is honest.' The same rule applies to 'because' and 'so' — they are mutually exclusive in standard written English.

'Despite' and 'in spite of' are prepositions followed by a noun or gerund, never a clause. 'Despite of the rain' is wrong — 'despite' never takes 'of.' Correct: 'Despite the rain' or 'In spite of the rain.' 'Although' is a conjunction followed by a clause: 'Although it rained, we went out.'

'However' functions differently from 'although.' 'However' means 'nevertheless' and connects two independent clauses, usually preceded by a semicolon or period: 'The task was difficult; however, he completed it.' It cannot replace 'although' inside a single clause.

'Whereas' and 'while' used for contrast: 'He likes tea, whereas she likes coffee.' Using 'whereas' to mean 'in the area where' is wrong — that requires 'where.' 'Whereas' indicates contrast or comparison, not location.

'That' clauses as subjects or complements must not drop the complementizer unnecessarily in formal writing: 'That he is late is unfortunate' is formal but correct. In everyday usage, 'It is unfortunate that he is late' is preferred.

Relative clauses must match their antecedent. Restrictive clauses (essential information) use 'that' or 'which' without commas. Non-restrictive clauses (additional information) use 'which' with commas, never 'that': 'My car, which is ten years old, still runs well.' Not 'My car, that is ten years old...'.

9. Voice, Mood, and Reported Speech Errors

Voice and reported speech errors test transformation rules and the ability to identify when those rules have been violated.

In active-to-passive transformation, the object of the active sentence becomes the subject of the passive. Only transitive verbs can be passivized. Intransitive verbs like 'sleep,' 'die,' 'arrive,' 'happen,' 'appear' cannot take passive voice. 'The murder was happened yesterday' is wrong — 'happen' has no passive form.

When the active subject is indefinite (one, people, someone, they, nobody), the passive can omit the agent or use 'by people' or 'by them.' 'They say that he is honest' → 'He is said to be honest' (impersonal passive with complex transformation).

Double-object verbs allow two passive constructions: 'He gave me a book' → 'I was given a book' or 'A book was given to me.' Prefer the version with the person as subject when both are possible.

Impersonal passives with reporting verbs (say, believe, know, consider, expect, report) follow a specific pattern: 'People say that he is rich' → 'He is said to be rich' or 'It is said that he is rich.' 'They consider him to be intelligent' → 'He is considered to be intelligent.' Errors arise when the infinitive form after 'to be' is incorrect.

Modal verbs in passive: 'Can be done,' 'must be done,' 'should be done,' 'might have been done.' The structure is modal + be + past participle. 'The work must done today' is wrong — missing 'be.' 'The work must be done today.'

10. Commonly Confused Words and Idiomatic Expressions

NDA & CDS frequently tests pairs of words that sound alike or look similar but have different meanings and usages. Mastery of these distinctions is essential for spotting errors.

'Its' vs. 'it is/it has': 'Its' is possessive; 'it is' or 'it has' is the contraction. 'Its coat was shed' (possessive) vs. 'It is raining' (contraction). Error: 'Its going to rain' is wrong — should be 'It is going to rain.'

'They're,' 'their,' 'there': 'They're' = they are; 'their' = possessive; 'there' = place or existential. 'They're going to their house over there.' Mixing any of these is a definitive error.

'Your' vs. 'you're': 'Your' = possessive; 'you're' = you are. 'Your book' vs. 'You're welcome.'

'Whose' vs. 'who's': 'Whose' = possessive relative; 'who's' = who is/who has. 'Whose bag is this?' vs. 'Who's coming?'

'Then' vs. 'than': 'Then' = time or sequence; 'than' = comparison. 'We ate dinner and then watched a film' vs. 'She is taller than me.'

'Accept' vs. 'except': 'Accept' = receive/agree; 'except' = exclude. 'I accept your offer except for the price.'

'Effect' vs. 'affect': 'Effect' is usually a noun (result); 'affect' is usually a verb (to influence). 'The effect was immediate' vs. 'The news affected him deeply.' Exception: 'effect' as a verb meaning 'to bring about' (rare in exam contexts).

'Loose' vs. 'lose': 'Loose' = not tight; 'lose' = misplace/fail to win. 'Don't loose your grip' is wrong — should be 'lose.'

'Lay' vs. 'lie': 'Lay' is transitive (requires an object): 'Lay the book on the table.' 'Lie' is intransitive (no object): 'Lie down on the bed.' Past tense: 'laid' (lay) vs. 'lay' (lie). Past participle: 'laid' vs. 'lain.' Confusing these is a very common error.

'Raise' vs. 'rise': 'Raise' is transitive; 'rise' is intransitive. 'Raise your hand' vs. 'The sun rises.'

'Complement' vs. 'compliment': 'Complement' = completes/enhances; 'compliment' = praise. 'The wine complements the meal' vs. 'She received a compliment.'

'Elicit' vs. 'illicit': 'Elicit' = draw out a response; 'illicit' = illegal. 'The question elicited no response' vs. 'Illicit trade flourished.'

'Aboard,' 'abroad,' 'about,' 'above': These homophones/heteronyms are tested for pronunciation-based substitution errors. 'He travelled abroad' (foreign country) vs. 'He boarded the ship aboard' (on board).

Fixed idioms and phrasal verbs are also tested. Using the wrong preposition in an idiom constitutes an error: 'accustomed to' (not 'accustomed with'), 'capable of' (not 'capable to'), 'used to' (habit) vs. 'be used to' (accustomed to something).

11. Sentence Structure: Fragments, Run-ons, and Faulty Comparisons

Sentence-structure errors test whether a sentence is complete, properly punctuated, or logically constructed.

A sentence fragment lacks a main clause — it has no subject-verb pair or is a subordinate clause standing alone. 'Because he was late.' is a fragment. 'Although she tried hard.' is a fragment. These must be attached to an independent clause: 'Because he was late, he missed the bus.' 'Although she tried hard, she failed.' In spotting-error questions, a fragment presented as a complete sentence is the error.

Run-on sentences (fused sentences) occur when two independent clauses are joined without punctuation or conjunction. 'She finished her work she went home' is wrong — it needs a period, semicolon, or comma plus conjunction. 'She finished her work; she went home.' 'She finished her work, and she went home.'

Comma splices are a type of run-on where a comma alone joins two independent clauses without a coordinating conjunction. 'He is smart, he is lazy' is a comma splice. Correct: 'He is smart, but he is lazy.' or 'He is smart; he is lazy.'

Faulty comparisons occur when two unlike things are compared or when the comparison lacks symmetry. 'His intelligence is greater than a teacher' compares intelligence to a person. Correct: 'His intelligence is greater than that of a teacher.' 'The climate of Mumbai is warmer than Chennai' is faulty — climates are compared, not a climate and a city. Correct: 'The climate of Mumbai is warmer than that of Chennai.'

Double negatives are standard errors in formal English. 'I don't need no help' is wrong — 'I don't need any help' or 'I need no help.' 'Nobody said nothing about it' → 'Nobody said anything about it.' In NDA & CDS, any double negative is treated as an error.

Redundancy errors involve saying the same thing twice. 'The actual fact is...' — 'fact' is already actual. 'End result' — results are inherently end products. 'Free gift' — gifts are inherently free. 'Close proximity' — proximity already means nearness. 'Each and every' — acceptable for emphasis but sometimes flagged as redundant in strict editing. 'Added additionally' is redundant.

Word-choice errors between synonyms with different registers or meanings: 'Historic' means important in history; 'historical' means relating to history. 'The historic treaty' vs. 'The historical novel.' 'Economic' relates to economy; 'economic' is different from 'economical' (thrifty). 'An economical car' saves fuel; 'an economic policy' relates to the economy.

Subject-complement agreement errors: 'The problem is they are late' → 'The problem is that they are late' (predicative complement). 'The reason is because' is redundant — 'The reason is that...' is correct.

12. Error-Spotting Strategy and High-Yield Revision Checklist

To excel in spotting-error questions, adopt a systematic scanning approach rather than reading the sentence once and guessing. The following strategy is proven effective for NDA & CDS:

Step 1: Read the entire sentence first to grasp the overall meaning. This helps you notice logical inconsistencies before you get caught in mechanical rules.

Step 2: Scan for the most commonly tested error types in this priority order:
(a) Subject-verb agreement — always check first, especially when the subject is separated from the verb by a long phrase.
(b) Pronoun reference — verify that every pronoun has a clear, singular antecedent.
(c) Verb tense — ensure consistency within the sentence.
(d) Article usage — check for unnecessary or missing articles with proper nouns and abstract nouns.
(e) Preposition collocations — recall fixed verb/preposition and adjective/preposition pairs.
(f) Parallelism — verify that coordinated elements share the same grammatical form.
(g) Modifier placement — confirm that modifiers attach to the correct noun.
(h) Conjunction pairing — eliminate redundant or mismatched conjunctions.
(i) Voice/transformation — check if passive voice is used correctly with transitive verbs.
(j) Confused words — look for commonly confused pairs.

Step 3: If no error is found in A, B, or C, select D (No error) only with confidence — do not choose D hastily. Many students incorrectly select 'No error' when a subtle mistake exists.

High-yield revision checklist:
- Subjects joined by 'or/nor' — proximity rule applies.
- 'Each of,' 'one of' — always singular.
- 'The number of' = singular; 'a number of' = plural.
- Collective nouns — singular when united, plural when individual.
- Reflexive pronouns — must refer to the subject of their clause.
- Present perfect with specific past-time words = error.
- 'Despite' never takes 'of'; 'in spite of' does.
- 'Although' and 'but' cannot coexist; 'because' and 'so' cannot coexist.
- 'Lay' takes an object; 'lie' does not.
- 'Than' for comparison; 'then' for time.
- In reported questions, word order is declarative, not interrogative.
- Double objects allow two passive forms; choose the one that keeps the meaning intact.
- Dangling modifiers = always an error.
- Faulty comparisons compare unequal items — use 'that of' or 'those of' for correction.
- Never choose 'No error' unless you have verified every category above.

Practice tip: Solve at least 50–70 spotting-error questions from past NDA & CDS papers, categorizing each error by type. This builds pattern recognition — the fastest route to accuracy under exam conditions.

Important facts

  • Subject-verb agreement is the most frequently tested grammar rule in NDA & CDS spotting-error questions.
  • Subjects joined by 'or/nor/neither...nor' follow the proximity rule — verb agrees with the nearer subject.
  • 'Each of,' 'one of,' 'every,' and 'either' always take singular verbs regardless of the noun in the prepositional phrase.
  • 'The number of' takes a singular verb; 'a number of' takes a plural verb.
  • Reflexive pronouns must refer back to the subject of the same clause — they cannot replace objective pronouns.
  • 'Despite' never takes 'of'; 'in spite of' is correct. 'Although' cannot coexist with 'but' in the same sentence.
  • 'Because' and 'so' are redundant together — use one or the other, not both.
  • 'Lay' is transitive (requires an object); 'lie' is intransitive. Past tense of 'lay' is 'laid'; past tense of 'lie' is 'lay'.
  • Present perfect cannot be used with specific past-time expressions like 'yesterday,' 'last week,' 'in 1990.'
  • In reported questions, word order is declarative (subject before verb), not interrogative.
  • Dangling modifiers are always errors — the noun being modified must appear immediately after the modifier.
  • Faulty comparisons compare unlike things — always compare equivalent items using 'that of' or 'those of.'
  • 'Than' is used for comparison; 'then' is used for time or sequence.
  • Only transitive verbs can be converted to passive voice; intransitive verbs like 'happen,' 'die,' 'arrive' have no passive form.
  • Correlative conjunctions demand parallel structure on both sides — mixed forms constitute an error.
  • Selecting 'No error' (option D) should be done only after systematically verifying all major error categories.
