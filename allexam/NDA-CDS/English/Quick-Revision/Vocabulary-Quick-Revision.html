Quick Revision — English Vocabulary (NDA & CDS)

NDA & CDSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Vocabulary refers to the collection of words known and used by a person; in NDA/CDS English, it specifically tests knowledge of high-frequency words, their meanings, usage in context, word formation, commonly confused pairs, and idiomatic expressions.
  • Synonym is a word having the same or nearly the same meaning as another word in the same language; NDA/CDS frequently asks synonym selection from four options.
  • Antonym is a word opposite in meaning to another word; these are tested through direct selection questions and fill-in-the-blank contexts.
  • One-word substitution is a single word or short phrase that replaces a longer expression carrying the same meaning — e.g., 'phobia' for fear of something; a regular and prominent question type in NDA/CDS.
  • Idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be deduced literally from its component words; examples: 'spill the beans', 'beat about the bush'.
  • Phrasal verb is a verb combined with one or more particles (preposition/adverb) that creates a meaning different from the base verb alone — e.g., 'give up', 'look into', 'carry out'.
  • Homophones are words that sound alike but have different meanings and spellings — e.g., 'there/their/they're', 'lose/loose', 'effect/affect'.
  • Lexical precision means choosing the exact word that fits a given contextual demand rather than a near-synonym; this is the underlying skill tested in cloze passages and sentence improvement.

Key concepts

  • PREFIXES AND THEIR MEANINGS — Dis- (not/opposite: disable, disagree), Mis- (wrong: mislead, mistake), Pre- (before: predict, preview), Un- (not/reverse: unlock, unfair), Im-/In-/Il-/Ir- (not: impossible, invisible, illegal, irregular), Super- (above: superhuman), Sub- (below: submarine), Inter- (between: international), Ex- (former: ex-president), Re- (again: rewrite), Over- (excess: overcook), Under- (insufficient: underpay).
  • SUFFIXES AND WORD-FORMATION — -tion/-sion (noun: education, decision), -ment (movement), -ness (kindness), -ity/-ty (capacity): ability, reality, -ful (hopeful), -less (hopeless), -able/-ible (readable, visible), -ous (dangerous), -ic(-ical) (basic/basically), -ize/-ise (realize), -ary/-ian (secretary, musician), -ent/-ant (different, dependent). Recognising suffixes helps infer meaning of unfamiliar words quickly during the exam.
  • HIGH-YIELD VOCABULARY SET FOR NDA/CDS — Positive/connotation words: benevolent, candid, eloquent, fortitude, glean, hiatus, imbibe, jubilation, keen, laconic, meticulous, nebulous, obfuscate, parsimonious, quixotic, sagacious, taciturn, ubiquitous, vacillate. Negative/neutral words: aberration, acrimonious, blatant, callous, dogma, equivocate, fallacy, garrulous, hypocritical, insipid, jocular, knavish, lax, mercurial, naivety, officious, parsimonious, querulous, recalcitrant, sycophant. Learn these in context (example sentences).
  • COMMONLY CONFUSED WORD PAIRS — Affect (verb: influence) vs Effect (noun: result); Alone (by oneself) vs All alone (completely alone); Complement (complete/match) vs Compliment (praise); Continue (go on) vs Continues (he/she continues); Council (body) vs Counsel (advise); Define vs Defy; Ensure vs Insure vs Assure; Elicit (draw out) vs Illicit (illegal); Empathy (understand feelings) vs Sympathy (feel pity); Lie (recline) vs Lay (place); Loose (not tight) vs Lose (misplace); Principal (head/fundamental) vs Principle (rule/truth); Then (time) vs Than (comparison); Waist (body part) vs Waste (squander).
  • ONE-WORD SUBSTITUTION HIGH-YIELD LIST — Philanthropist: lover of mankind; Bibliophile: book lover; Misogynist: hater of women; Misandrist: hater of men; Polyglot: person knowing many languages; Xenophobe: stranger/fear; Megalomaniac: obsessed with power; Ultramontane: beyond the mountains (pope's supporter); Agnostic: doubtful about religion; Ephemeral: lasting a day; Otiose: needless/redundant; Ubiquitous: present everywhere; Esoteric: meant for few; Didactic: intended to teach; Idiosyncratic: peculiar habit.
  • IDIOMS AND PHRASES — To let the cat out of the bag = reveal a secret; To beat about the bush = avoid the main point; To kill two birds with one stone = achieve two goals at once; To pull one's leg = joke with someone; To play devil's advocate = argue against popular opinion; To burn the candle at both ends = overwork; To cut corners = do something cheaply/poorly; To go back to the drawing board = start again from beginning; To sit on the fence = remain neutral; To throw in the towel = give up; To add insult to injury = make a bad situation worse; To cut a long story short = summarize; To read between the lines = understand hidden meaning.
  • PHRASAL VERBS FREQUENTLY ASKED — Give up (stop/surrender), Look up (search/inform), Bring up (raise/mention), Call off (cancel), Turn down (refuse), Break down (collapse/stop working), Come across (find by chance), Get along (have good relation), Hold on (wait), Set up (establish), Take after (resemble), Throw away (discard), Work out (exercise/resolve), Put off (postpone), Fill in (complete form), Go over (review), Make up (invent/reconcile).
  • ETYMOLOGY-BASED LEARNING — Many NDA/CDS words come from Latin/Greek roots. Root 'scrib/script' = write (describe, inscription, manuscript); 'dict/dict' = say (dictate, dictionary, contradict); 'vid/vis' = see (video, vision, advise); 'port' = carry (transport, portable); 'ject' = throw (inject, project); 'grat' = please (grateful, congratulate); 'loqu/loqu' = speak (eloquent, loquacious). Knowing roots allows guessing meanings of unseen words in the exam.

Important facts

  • NDA & CDS English section carries approximately 100–120 marks (out of 300 per paper) depending on the specific exam cycle and UPSC notification; vocabulary contributes roughly 15–25 direct questions across synonyms, antonyms, one-word substitution, idioms, and phrasal verbs.
  • Previous NDA/CDS vocabulary questions predominantly test high-frequency words found in competitive exam dictionaries like Wren & Martin, Objective General English by SP Bakshi, and Words Made Easy by Norman Lewis.
  • The typical question format is four-option multiple-choice: identify the synonym, identify the antonym, choose the correct one-word substitution, or select the correct idiom meaning/usage.
  • UpSC occasionally includes paragraph comprehension-based vocabulary questions where the candidate must choose the most appropriate word from four options to complete a sentence meaningfully.
  • Common traps include near-synonyms with subtle shade-of-meaning differences — e.g., 'frugal' vs 'economical' vs 'thrifty' vs 'parsimonious'; the examiner expects the word with the precise contextual fit.
  • Spelling variations: NDA/CDS follows standard British English conventions (colour, favour, recognise), so candidates should practice British spellings and do not assume American forms.
  • Idioms asked are generally from commonly used English expressions; obscure literary idioms from Shakespeare or archaic texts are rarely asked directly but may appear indirectly through one-word substitution.
  • The vocabulary syllabus does not prescribe an explicit chapter-wise list in UPSC notifications; preparation must be cumulative, covering all standard types without assuming any question type is excluded.

Memory aids

  • Prefix mnemonic: DISMISS UNDER REOVER — to recall Dis-, Mis-, Im-/In-, Under-, Re-, Over- with quick examples (disable, misuse, improve, underestimate, redo, overeat).
  • Suffix pattern anchor: TALE — -tion (narration), -able (comfortable), -less (careless), -ful (useful) — remembering these four covers roughly half the noun/adj suffixes seen in exams.
  • Root anchor with image: 'spect' (see) → inspect (look into), retrospect (look back), prospect (look forward), spectacular (worth seeing), perspicacious (seeing clearly → shrewd).
  • One-word substitution trick: link the Greek/Latin root with the meaning — 'biblio' = book → bibliophile/book-lover; 'mania' = madness → megalomania/madness for greatness.
  • Idiom-image technique: create a vivid mental picture for each idiom — 'spill the beans' → imagine knocking over a can of beans and secrets scattering everywhere, making it unforgettable.
  • Confused-pair rhyme: 'There/their/they're' — 'Their car is there because they're there'; 'Loose/lose' — 'A loose knot loses its grip'; such mini-sentences help lock the distinction.
  • Alphabet anchor for hard words: A-berrant, B-rusque, C-andid, D-eligent, E-lusory, F-urtive, G-nomonic, H-istrionic — using one-word starters builds a rapid daily revision habit.

Exam tips

  • Build a personal error log: maintain a notebook of every incorrectly attempted vocabulary question from mock tests, grouped by type (synonym, idiom, phrasal verb, one-word substitution), and revise it weekly.
  • Practice 20 new high-frequency words daily with at least one example sentence; retention improves dramatically when words are learned in context rather than as isolated definitions.
  • Use elimination strategy in MCQs: if two options are clear synonyms, the answer lies outside them; if two options are antonyms, the answer is often one of the remaining pair.
  • For idioms, read the sentence context carefully and substitute the idiom's figurative meaning; discard literal-meaning distractors immediately.
  • Spend no more than 45–60 seconds per vocabulary question; if stuck, mark for review and move on — time management matters more than perfect accuracy in this section.
  • Revision priority order: one-word substitution first (highest ROI because finite set), then commonly asked synonyms/antonyms, then phrasal verbs, and last idioms whose volume is larger.
  • When unsure between two close synonyms, choose the more formal/academic word — NDA/CDS tends to prefer elevated register in its cloze and paragraph-level items.
  • Solve at least 500 cumulative vocabulary MCQs from previous years and standard practice books before the exam; pattern familiarity improves speed and accuracy faster than rereading word lists.

Common traps

  • Choosing a near-synonym without checking the emotional connotation — 'slim' is positive, 'skinny' is often negative; 'frugal' is positive, 'stingy' is negative. The question context reveals which shade is needed.
  • Mistaking a noun form for a verb form — 'beautify' is a verb, 'beauty' is a noun; confusing suffixes leads to selecting the wrong grammatical option in cloze tests.
  • Idiom literal interpretation — taking 'cold feet' literally as temperature instead of understanding it means fear/hesitation before an event; always map idiom to situation, not words.
  • Assuming uncommon prefixes change the whole word class — 'unhappy' stays an adjective; changing prefix rarely changes noun to verb unless the base itself allows it.
  • Overlooking that some one-word substitutions have subtle differences — 'philanderer' (one who flirts/having casual affairs) vs 'adulterer' (one committing adultery) are distinct and examiners exploit this.
  • Ignoring British spelling in answer — selecting an American-spelled variant when other three options use British spelling consistently can eliminate doubt immediately.
  • Treating all phrasal verbs as interchangeable — 'look after' (take care) ≠ 'look for' (search) ≠ 'look up' (visit/inform); confusion here is a frequent score-killer.
  • Skipping context in cloze-style vocabulary — picking the first plausible word rather than reading the full sentence to identify whether the required sense is positive, negative, formal, or informal.
