Definitions

  • Limit: The value that f(x) approaches as x approaches a finite point or infinity. Symbolically, lim(x→a) f(x) = L.
  • Continuous Function: f is continuous at x = a if (i) f(a) exists, (ii) lim(x→a) f(x) exists, and (iii) lim(x→a) f(x) = f(a). A function continuous on an interval is said to be continuous there.
  • Differentiable Function: f is differentiable at x = a if the derivative f'(a) = lim(h→0) [f(a+h) − f(a)] / h exists as a finite real number. Every differentiable function is continuous, but the converse is not true.
  • Derivative: The rate of change of y with respect to x, denoted dy/dx or f'(x), giving the slope of the tangent to the curve at any point.
  • Tangent: The line touching the curve y = f(x) at point (a, f(a)) with slope f'(a). Equation: y − f(a) = f'(a)(x − a).
  • Normal: The line perpendicular to the tangent at (a, f(a)). Slope = −1/f'(a). Equation: y − f(a) = [−1/f'(a)](x − a).
  • Rate of Change: If Q depends on P, then dQ/dP is the instantaneous rate of change of Q w.r.t. P.
  • Approximation Formula: f(x + Δx) ≈ f(x) + f'(x)·Δx, valid for small Δx.

Important facts

  • lim(x→0) sin(nx)/mx = n/m. This shortcut avoids full L'Hôpital application.
  • lim(x→0) (aˣ − 1)/x = log a. For a = e this gives 1.
  • At a local extremum, the first non-zero derivative of odd order indicates the nature: if the first non-zero derivative at c is of odd order 2k+1 with sign determining max/min, while even-order derivatives give a flat inflection point.
  • If f is differentiable and periodic with period T, then ∫₀^{nT} f'(x) dx = 0 for any integer n.
  • For polynomial P(x) of degree n, Rolle's theorem guarantees at most n−1 roots of P'(x) = 0 in any interval where P has n distinct roots.
  • Sign of second derivative: f''(x) > 0 → concave up (holding water); f''(x) < 0 → concave down (spilling water). Inflection point where concavity changes.
  • Derivative of inverse function: If y = f⁻¹(x), then dy/dx = 1/(dx/dy) = 1/f'(y).
  • n-th derivative of sin(ax + b): dⁿ/dxⁿ sin(ax+b) = aⁿ · sin(ax + b + nπ/2). Similarly for cosine.
  • Derivatives of composite standard forms frequently tested: d/dx[sin⁻¹(2x√(1−x²))] = 2/√(1−x²) for |x| < 1/√2 (after simplification using 2sin⁻¹x substitution).

Memory aids

  • Chain Rule: 'Outside derivative times inside derivative.' Say it aloud: 'Deriv of outside at inside × deriv of inside.'
  • Product Rule Mnemonic: 'First d-second plus second d-first' (fdS + Sdf). Reverse order doesn't matter since addition is commutative.
  • Quotient Rule: 'Lo d-Hi minus Hi d-Lo, over Lo Lo' (low derivative of high minus high derivative of low, over low squared).
  • Trig Derivatives: Sin → Cos (positive), Cos → −Sin (the negative sneaks in), Tan → Sec² (always positive), Cot → −Cosec² (the negative stays).
  • Inverse Trig Derivatives: All inverse trig derivatives have the same denominator pattern as their non-inverse counterparts except with a square root (arcsin, arccos) or (1 + x²) (arctan, arccot). The negative for arccos and arccot mirrors the negative for cos and cot.
  • LMVT mnemonic: 'Mean Value Theorem: somewhere between a and b, the derivative equals the average slope.' Visualize the secant line sliding parallel to itself until it becomes tangent.
  • First Derivative Test: Think of a hill — going up (+) then down (−) means peak (max); going down (−) then up (+) means valley (min).

Exam tips

  • NDA/CDS frequently ask direct-application questions on standard derivatives. Memorize all 14 trigonometric and inverse-trigonometric derivative formulas cold.
  • L'Hôpital's rule questions appear regularly — practice identifying correct indeterminate forms quickly (0/0 or ∞/∞ only).
  • Maxima-minima questions on quadratic and cubic polynomials are guaranteed. Practice finding critical points, classifying them, and computing function values.
  • Tangent-normal problems often combine coordinate geometry with calculus — expect questions asking for length of tangent/subtangent or equation of normal at a given point.
  • Rolle's theorem and LMVT questions typically ask to 'verify the theorem' for a given function on a given interval — always write all three conditions explicitly before concluding.
  • For approximation questions, round numbers to nearby perfect squares/cubes/fourth powers (e.g., √401 ≈ √400, ∛27.03 ≈ ∛27) and apply the differential formula.
  • When asked to prove an inequality using LMVT, always define a suitable function f(x), identify the interval [a,b], compute f(b)−f(a)/(b−a), then relate to f'(c).
  • In MCQ format, elimination works well for limit problems — substitute near-values to narrow options when exact evaluation is time-consuming.
  • Parasitic differentiations (e.g., differentiating under integral sign) are generally beyond NDA/CDS scope — focus on single-variable calculus techniques listed here.
  • Practice writing answers in the 'verified' style for theorem questions: state conditions → show they hold → conclude existence of c → optionally find c's value.

Common traps

  • Assuming |x| is differentiable at x = 0. It is NOT — left derivative = −1, right derivative = +1.
  • Forgetting the chain rule factor when differentiating sin(x²), e^{3x}, or log(x²+1).
  • Applying L'Hôpital's rule without confirming the indeterminate form first. Applying it to 1/0 is invalid and produces wrong results.
  • Confusing f'(c) = 0 with extremum. f'(c) = 0 is necessary but not sufficient; must check sign change or second derivative.
  • Using second derivative test when f''(c) = 0 — the test is inconclusive, not a failure of the function.
  • Writing dy/dx = f'(x)/g'(x) for parametric equations. Correct formula: dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt), not the quotient of derivatives w.r.t. x.
  • Forgetting absolute value in d/dx(sec⁻¹x) and d/dx(cosec⁻¹x). The |x| in the denominator is essential for correctness on the full domain.
  • Mixing degrees and radians in calculus. ALL trig differentiation formulas assume x is in radians. Converting degrees to radians before differentiating is mandatory.
  • In approximation f(x+h) ≈ f(x) + hf'(x), assuming h is large. The formula is only accurate for very small h relative to the scale of variation of f.
  • Applying Rolle's theorem without checking continuity at endpoints or differentiability in the open interval. A single corner or jump discontinuity voids the theorem.
