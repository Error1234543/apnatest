Quick Revision — Statistics and Probability (NDA & CDS)

NDA & CDSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Statistics is the branch of mathematics dealing with collection, organisation, analysis, interpretation and presentation of data.
  • Data: raw numerical facts collected for a purpose; classified as primary (collected firsthand) or secondary (obtained from existing sources).
  • Frequency: the number of times a particular observation occurs in a data set.
  • Class interval / class size: the difference between the upper and lower boundaries of a class; also called width or magnitude.
  • Mean (Arithmetic Average): sum of all observations divided by the number of observations.
  • Median: the middle value when data is arranged in ascending or descending order; for even number of observations it is the average of the two middle values.
  • Mode: the value that occurs most frequently in a data set.
  • Range: the difference between the largest and smallest observation.
  • Variance: the mean of the squared deviations of observations from their arithmetic mean; denoted σ² for population or s² for sample.
  • Standard Deviation (σ): the positive square root of variance; it measures spread around the mean in the original units.
  • Mutually exclusive events: two events A and B are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur simultaneously, i.e., A ∩ B = ∅.
  • Exhaustive events: a set of events is exhaustive if their union covers the entire sample space S, i.e., A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ … = S.
  • Independent events: two events A and B are independent if occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other; P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B).
  • Conditional probability P(A|B): the probability of A given that B has already occurred; P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), provided P(B) > 0.
  • Bayes' theorem: relates conditional and reverse conditional probabilities; P(Eᵢ|A) = P(Eᵢ)·P(A|Eᵢ) / Σⱼ P(Eⱼ)·P(A|Eⱼ).
  • Binomial distribution: probability distribution of exactly k successes in n independent Bernoulli trials with success probability p; P(X=k) = nCk · pᵏ · qⁿ⁻ᵏ, q = 1−p.
  • Ogive: a graphical representation of cumulative frequency; less-than and more-than ogives intersect at the median point.

Important facts

  • n/2 (not n) is used in the median formula for grouped data — n is total frequency, NOT number of classes.
  • In the mode formula for grouped data, the denominator is (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂), not (f₁ − f₀ − f₂).
  • Coefficient of Variation has no units because σ and x̄ share the same unit; it is expressed as a percentage.
  • Changing origin (subtracting a constant) does NOT change standard deviation; changing scale (dividing by a constant) divides SD by that constant.
  • For P(A ∪ B ∪ C): P(A)+P(B)+P(C) − P(A∩B) − P(B∩C) − P(C∩A) + P(A∩B∩C). Remember the alternating signs.
  • If P(A) = 0.6 and P(B) = 0.4 and A, B are independent, then P(A ∪ B) = 0.6 + 0.4 − 0.24 = 0.76.
  • In Bayes' theorem problems, always verify that the causes E₁, E₂, ... form a complete partition (sum of their probabilities = 1).
  • For binomial distribution, mean and variance have the same units as the random variable squared for variance; SD returns to original units.
  • If two events are mutually exclusive AND independent, then at least one of them must have probability zero (both cannot have positive probability).
  • The median of grouped data always lies within the median class; never outside it.
  • For discrete distributions like binomial, P(X ≥ k) = 1 − P(X ≤ k−1), which often requires fewer calculations.
  • In frequency polygon construction, the first and last points are joined to the mid-points of the imaginary classes before and after, bringing the polygon to the X-axis.

Memory aids

  • Median formula mnemonic: 'Lower plus half minus preceding, divided by own and multiplied by width' → l + [(n/2 − cf)/f] × h.
  • Mode formula trick: remember 'excess over previous, over twice excess minus sum' → (f₁−f₀)/(2f₁−f₀−f₂).
  • Variance shortcut: 'mean of squares minus square of means' → σ² = (Σx²)/n − (Σx/n)². Quick mental check.
  • Binomial recall: 'n choose k, p to the k, q to the rest' → nCk·pᵏ·qⁿ⁻ᵏ. Mean np, variance npq.
  • Bayes' theorem story pattern: 'What is the chance this particular cause produced the result we saw?' — Prior × Likelihood ÷ Total.
  • Independence test: if knowing B happened doesn't change P(A), they're independent. P(A|B) = P(A) is the litmus test.
  • Ogive intersection: less-than and more-than curves meet exactly above the median on the X-axis. Visual memory anchor.
  • Probability addition sign: 'and' in sentence → + sign in formula for union; crossing out double-counted intersection.
  • CV memory: 'how variable relative to average' — pure ratio × 100. Higher CV = more dispersed data relatively.

Exam tips

  • NDA&CDS statistics questions are usually calculation-light but concept-heavy; spend no more than 3 minutes per question — if stuck, eliminate options first.
  • For mean/mode/median relationship in moderately skewed data: Mode ≈ 3·Median − 2·Mean — useful for quick verification in MCQs.
  • When a question asks 'which measure is most affected by extreme values', answer: Mean. When asking 'most representative', answer: Median.
  • In probability MCQs, if options are far apart, approximate p and q as simple fractions (e.g., 0.3 ≈ 3/10) for faster binomial calculations.
  • For Bayes' theorem MCQs with two bags: label Bag 1 and Bag 2 probabilities clearly on paper; write the fraction structure before substituting numbers.
  • Remember: median formula uses l, not U (upper limit). A very common option trap replaces l with the upper boundary.
  • For variance problems with changed origin/scale: use the property directly rather than recomputing — saves significant time.
  • If a probability question mentions 'replacement', trials are independent; if 'without replacement', use conditional probability or hypergeometric reasoning.
  • In NDA, Statistics and Probability together carry approximately 4–6 questions; master formulas and practice 15–20 standard problems per sub-topic.
  • For quick revision before exam: memorise only the five core formulas (mean, median, mode, variance/SD, binomial probability) and Bayes' theorem structure — everything else follows from these.

Common traps

  • Using n (number of observations) instead of cumulative frequency N when locating median class in grouped data; the formula requires N, not the count of data points in a partial sense.
  • Applying P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) without checking mutual exclusivity — this is only valid when A ∩ B = ∅.
  • Confusing 'at least one' with 'exactly one': P(at least one) = 1 − P(none), not P(exactly one). Extremely common error.
  • Treating mutually exclusive events as independent — they are actually negatively related (if one occurs, other cannot). They can both be true ONLY if one has probability zero.
  • Using the wrong class size h in grouped formulas; h must be the actual width, not just upper−lower without adjusting for continuous conversion.
  • For binomial problems: forgetting that order doesn't matter but the combination coefficient nCk already accounts for all arrangements.
  • In Bayes' theorem: using P(A|Eᵢ) as the final answer instead of computing the full fraction — missing the division by total probability.
  • Sign errors in combined variance: dᵢ² is always positive regardless of whether x̄ᵢ is above or below x̄_combined.
  • Assuming all probability questions use equally likely outcomes — classical definition applies ONLY when outcomes are equally likely.
  • In standard deviation properties: forgetting that multiplying all observations by a constant c multiplies SD by |c|, not c².
  • Computing range of grouped data using class boundaries instead of actual extreme values from raw data when available.
