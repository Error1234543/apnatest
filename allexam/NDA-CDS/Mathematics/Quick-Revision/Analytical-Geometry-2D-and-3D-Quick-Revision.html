NDA & CDS — Mathematics — Analytical Geometry (2D and 3D) — Quick Revision

NDA & CDSEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Analytical Geometry: Branch of mathematics that uses a coordinate system to represent geometric figures algebraically, enabling calculation of distances, slopes, angles, areas, and loci using equations rather than pure synthetic reasoning.
  • Slope of a Line: The tangent of the angle the line makes with the positive x-axis; slope m = tan θ = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁). A vertical line has undefined slope; a horizontal line has slope 0.
  • Distance Formula: The distance between P(x₁,y₁) and Q(x₂,y₂) is PQ = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]. In 3D, add (z₂−z₁)² under the radical.
  • Section Formula: Point dividing segment joining (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) internally in ratio m:n is ((mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂+ny₁)/(m+n)); externally use (m−n) in denominator with signs adjusted accordingly.
  • Locus: The set of all points satisfying a given geometric condition; obtained by eliminating parameters from the condition and writing an equation in x and y (or x, y, z).
  • Conic Sections: Curves obtained by intersecting a right circular cone with a plane; categorized as parabola, ellipse, or hyperbola depending on the eccentricity e (e=1, e<1, e>1 respectively).
  • Parabola: Locus of a point equidistant from a fixed point (focus) and a fixed line (directrix). Standard form: y² = 4ax opens right; vertex at origin.
  • Ellipse: Locus of a point whose sum of distances from two fixed foci is constant. Standard form: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, where a > b; major axis along x-axis.
  • Hyperbola: Locus of a point whose difference of distances from two fixed foci is constant. Standard form: x²/a² − y²/b² = 1.
  • Direction Cosines (DCs): The cosines of the angles a directed line makes with the positive x, y, z axes: l = cos α, m = cos β, n = cos γ. They satisfy l² + m² + n² = 1.
  • Direction Ratios (DRs): Any three numbers proportional to the direction cosines of a line. If DRs are a, b, c then l = a/√(a²+b²+c²), etc.
  • Plane: A flat surface such that every straight line joining any two points on it lies wholly on the surface. General equation: Ax + By + Cz + D = 0.
  • Sphere: Locus of a point moving in space such that its distance from a fixed point (centre) is constant (radius). Equation: (x−x₁)²+(y−y₁)²+(z−z₁)² = r².

Key concepts

  • General Equation of a Straight Line: ['Standard forms of a line in 2D:', 'Point-slope: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)', 'Slope-intercept: y = mx + c (c = y-intercept)', 'Two-point form: (y−y₁)/(x−x₁) = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁)', 'Intercept form: x/a + y/b = 1 (a = x-intercept, b = y-intercept)', 'Normal form: x cos ω + y sin ω = p (p = perpendicular from origin, ω = angle of normal)', 'General form: Ax + By + C = 0, slope = −A/B, intercepts = −C/A and −C/B']

Important facts

  • NDA/CDS frequently tests condition-of-tangency problems in analytical geometry, especially for circle, ellipse, hyperbola, and parabola — know all four tangent conditions by heart.
  • The parametric forms (t for parabola, θ for ellipse/hyperbola) are essential for solving chord, tangent, and focal-distance questions quickly without carrying x and y separately.
  • Direction cosine problems in NDA often involve finding the angle between two lines when their DRs satisfy a given linear relation; use the constraint to express one DR in terms of others.
  • For the circle, remember: the length of tangent from an external point equals √(S₁) where S₁ is obtained by substituting the point in the circle equation.
  • In 3D, the equation of a plane can also be written as x/l + y/m + z/n = d where l, m, n are DCs of the normal and d is the perpendicular from origin — useful for quick distance-from-origin problems.
  • NDA/CDS asks about locus questions involving moving points on conics; eliminate the parameter (t or θ) to obtain the Cartesian equation of the locus.
  • The orthoptic locus of a parabola (locus of intersection of perpendicular tangents) is its directrix — a favourite fact-based question.
  • For ellipse and hyperbola, recall the focal property: any ray from one focus reflects to the other focus (ellipse) or appears to come from the other focus (hyperbola) — this underlies several optical-application questions.
  • The director circle of x²/a²+y²/b²=1 is x²+y²=a²+b²; for parabola y²=4ax it is x+a=0 (the directrix).
  • In 3D, three points determine a unique plane unless they are collinear; four points determine a unique sphere unless they are coplanar.

Memory aids

  • For parabola y²=4ax: think "y-squared opens sideways" — if x²=4ay, it opens upward/downward.
  • Ellipse eccentricity e < 1; Hyperbola e > 1 — "Ellipse closes (circle-like, low e), Hyperbola splits open (high e)".
  • Tangent condition mnemonic: "CEPH" — Circle: c²=a²m²−r²; Ellipse: c²=a²m²+b²; Parabola: c=a/m; Hyperbola: c²=a²m²−b². Notice the pattern: ellipse ADDS b², hyperbola SUBTRACTS b², parabola has no square.
  • Direction cosines sum to 1: l²+m²+n²=1 — like a unit vector squared magnitude.
  • Parabola focal chord parameter product t₁t₂=−1; tangent slope at t is 1/t — opposite reciprocal hints at perpendicularity with normal.
  • For line-plane angle: sin φ = |Ax₀+By₀+Cz₀+D|/√(A²+B²+C²) · 1/√(a²+b²+c²) where (a,b,c) are line DRs — remember sine not cosine because angle is with the line, not the normal.
  • Standard forms quick lookup: Parabola has one squared term; Ellipse has both squared with same sign (+,+); Hyperbola has both squared with opposite signs (+,−).

Exam tips

  • In NDA, analytical geometry typically carries 8–12 questions. Master tangent conditions first — they give 3–4 direct questions per paper with minimal computation.
  • When time is short in the exam, use elimination: test whether the given point satisfies each option's equation rather than deriving the full equation from scratch.
  • For parabola focal chord questions, immediately write endpoints as (at₁², 2at₁) and (at₂², 2at₂) with t₁t₂=−1; this cuts computation time by half.
  • For 3D direction cosine problems with a constraint like a+l=0 or 2l=m=n, solve for all DCs in terms of one variable using l²+m²+n²=1 — then compute the angle.
  • Remember: for ellipse, the sum of focal distances is always 2a; for hyperbola, the absolute difference is always 2a. Use this to verify answers quickly.
  • If a question gives two tangents to a parabola and asks for the chord of contact, use yy₁=2a(x+x₁) directly — do not find the points of contact separately unless required.
  • In the exam, if you see a locus problem, first try a couple of particular positions of the moving point to guess the curve type, then derive rigorously.
  • For circle problems involving a moving chord of fixed length, the locus of its midpoint is always a concentric circle — use this as a shortcut check.
  • NDA/CDS sometimes combine two topics: e.g., find the equation of a tangent to a parabola that is also normal to an ellipse — identify both conditions and solve simultaneously.
  • Carry a one-page formula sheet in your mental notebook: all five conic standard forms, their foci, directrices, LR, eccentricity, and tangent conditions — practice recalling this under timed conditions before the exam.

Common traps

  • Writing y²=4ax for a left-opening parabola instead of y²=−4ax — check the sign of a carefully from the problem statement.
  • Using e = √(1+b²/a²) for ellipse — this is for hyperbola. For ellipse use e = √(1−b²/a²). Confusing these is extremely common.
  • For tangent condition of hyperbola, writing c² = a²m² + b² — the sign is MINUS, not plus. This is the single most tested trap in NDA/CDS analytical geometry.
  • In section formula, forgetting that external division changes the sign in the denominator (m−n instead of m+n) and in one of the numerator terms.
  • Assuming the locus of midpoint of chord of parabola y²=4ax with slope m is another parabola — it is actually a straight line (the diameter), y = 2a/m.
  • In 3D, computing angle between line and plane as cos θ instead of sin θ — the formula uses the angle between the line direction and the plane normal, giving sin φ for the line-plane angle.
  • When finding distance from origin to plane, dividing by √(A²+B²+C²) but forgetting to take absolute value — distance is always non-negative.
  • Confusing latus rectum of ellipse (2b²/a) with that of hyperbola (2b²/a) — the formula looks identical but a and b have different meanings (in hyperbola b²=a²(e²−1)).
  • For conjugate hyperbola, thinking the transverse axis becomes the conjugate axis of the original — actually the roles swap: 2b becomes the transverse axis of the conjugate.
  • Using general form of circle x²+y²+2gx+2fy+c=0 and reading radius as √(g²+f²+c) — it is √(g²+f²−c), note the minus sign.
