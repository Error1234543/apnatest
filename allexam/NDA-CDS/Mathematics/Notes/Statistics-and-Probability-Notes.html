Statistics and Probability — Complete Notes for NDA & CDS Mathematics

NDA & CDSEnglishNotes

1. Collection and Presentation of Data

Data is a collection of observations relevant to a particular problem. In NDA/CDS, the classification and tabulation of data are the foundation upon which all subsequent statistical calculations rest.

Primary vs Secondary Data: Primary data is collected firsthand by the investigator (surveys, experiments). Secondary data is obtained from existing sources (census reports, government publications). Primary data is more reliable but costlier; secondary data is quicker but may be outdated or unsuitable.

Frequency Distribution: When data is large, it is organized into a frequency table. Each value or class interval is paired with its frequency (number of occurrences). For continuous data, class intervals are used (e.g., 10–20, 20–30), where the lower limit is inclusive and the upper limit exclusive. The midpoint (class mark) of an interval l.u – l.l is (upper limit + lower limit)/2.

Graphical Representations:
• Bar Diagram: Rectangles of equal width with heights proportional to frequencies. Used for categorical/discrete data. Bars have equal widths and equal gaps between them.
• Histogram: Similar to a bar diagram but with no gaps between bars; used for continuous frequency distributions. Each rectangle's height equals the frequency of that class interval.
• Frequency Polygon: Obtained by joining the midpoints (class marks) of the tops of histogram rectangles with line segments. It can also be drawn independently by plotting (class mark, frequency) pairs. To close the polygon, add imaginary classes of frequency zero at both ends.
• Ogive (Cumulative Frequency Curve): Two types — "less than" ogive (plotted using upper class limits against cumulative frequencies) and "more than" ogive (plotted using lower class limits against cumulative frequencies). The point of intersection of the two ogives gives the median graphically. This graphical method of finding median is commonly asked in NDA.

2. Measures of Central Tendency — Mean, Median, Mode

Mean (Arithmetic Average):
For ungrouped data: X̄ = Σxᵢ / n, where xᵢ are observations and n is total number.
For grouped data: X̄ = Σ(fᵢxᵢ) / Σfᵢ, where fᵢ is frequency and xᵢ is class mark.
Shortcut (Step-Deviation) Method: Let A be an assumed mean. dᵢ = xᵢ − A. Then X̄ = A + Σ(fᵢdᵢ)/Σfᵢ. For step-deviation, h is the class width: dᵢ' = (xᵢ − A)/h, and X̄ = A + h · Σ(fᵢdᵢ')/Σfᵢ. The assumed mean A is chosen near the center of data to minimize computational errors.

Median:
The middle value when data is arranged in ascending order. For n odd: Median = ((n+1)/2)th item. For n even: Median = average of (n/2)th and (n/2 + 1)th items.
For grouped continuous data: Median = l + [(N/2 − CF)/f] · h, where l = lower limit of median class (the class whose cumulative frequency first exceeds N/2), CF = cumulative frequency of the class preceding the median class, f = frequency of the median class, h = class width, N = Σfᵢ.
Graphically, the median is found from the point of intersection of the two ogives.

Mode:
The value that occurs most frequently. For ungrouped data, simply identify the observation with highest frequency.
For grouped data: Mode = l + [(f₁ − f₀)/(2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] · h, where l = lower limit of modal class (class with highest frequency), f₁ = frequency of modal class, f₀ = frequency of preceding class, f₂ = frequency of succeeding class, h = class width. If all frequencies are equal, there is no unique mode.

3. Measures of Dispersion — Range, Mean Deviation, Standard Deviation, Variance

Range: Difference between the largest and smallest observations. Range = Maximum − Minimum. Simple but crude measure; sensitive to outliers.

Mean Deviation (MD): The average of absolute deviations from a central value (mean, median, or mode). MD(X̄) = Σfᵢ|xᵢ − X̄| / Σfᵢ. Though easy to compute, it is less commonly used in advanced statistics because absolute values complicate algebraic manipulation.

Variance (σ²) and Standard Deviation (σ):
For ungrouped data: σ² = Σ(xᵢ − X̄)²/n = [Σxᵢ²/n] − [X̄²]. The second form is computationally convenient: compute the mean of squares and subtract the square of the mean.
For grouped data: σ² = Σfᵢ(xᵢ − X̄)²/Σfᵢ = [Σfᵢxᵢ²/Σfᵢ] − [X̄²].
Shortcut method: Using step-deviation uᵢ = (xᵢ − A)/h: σ = h · √{[Σfᵢuᵢ²/Σfᵢ] − [Σfᵢuᵢ/Σfᵢ]²}.
Coefficient of Variation (CV) = (σ/X̄) × 100. It is a relative measure useful for comparing variability between two datasets with different units or widely differing means. A higher CV indicates greater spread.

Combined Standard Deviation: When two groups with sizes n₁, n₂, means X̄₁, X̄₂ and SDs σ₁, σ₂ are combined: σ²_combined = [n₁(σ₁² + d₁²) + n₂(σ₂² + d₂²)]/(n₁ + n₂), where d₁ = X̄₁ − X̄_combined and d₂ = X̄₂ − X̄_combined. NDA occasionally asks for the combined SD when merging two datasets.

Note: NDA prefers direct application of these formulas over long derivations. Be comfortable choosing the shortcut method when data has large values.

4. Basic Concepts of Probability — Definitions and Classical Approach

Random Experiment: An experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty before it is performed. Examples: tossing a coin, rolling a die, drawing a card from a deck.

Sample Space (S): The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. For a die roll, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, so n(S) = 6. For two coins, S = {HH, HT, TH, TT}, n(S) = 4.

Event: Any subset of the sample space. An event E occurs if the outcome of the experiment belongs to E.

Classical (A Priori) Probability: P(E) = n(E)/n(S), provided all outcomes are equally likely. Here n(E) is the number of favorable outcomes and n(S) is the total number of outcomes. This is the approach used for dice, coins, cards, and balls in urns.

Key Result: 0 ≤ P(E) ≤ 1 for any event E. P(S) = 1 (certain event). P(∅) = 0 (impossible event).

Complementary Events: If E is an event, then E' (not-E) is its complement. P(E') = 1 − P(E). This is extremely useful — sometimes it is easier to find the probability of the complement and subtract from 1.

Important Conventions for NDA:
• Cards: 52 cards, 4 suits (♠, ♥, ♦, ♣), 13 cards per suit, 4 face cards per suit (J, Q, K) → 12 face cards total, 4 aces, 26 red cards, 26 black cards.
• Dice: Each die has faces 1–6. Probability of a specific face = 1/6. Two dice: n(S) = 36.
• Balls in urn: Drawing without replacement vs with replacement makes a critical difference. After each draw without replacement, the total and favorable counts change.

5. Addition Theorem, Conditional Probability, and Independent Events

Addition Theorem of Probability:
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). This gives the probability that at least one of A or B occurs.
If A and B are mutually exclusive (disjoint), then P(A ∩ B) = 0, so P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B). Mutually exclusive events cannot happen simultaneously (e.g., getting a 2 and a 3 on a single die roll).
For three events: P(A ∪ B ∪ C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) − P(A∩B) − P(B∩C) − P(C∩A) + P(A∩B∩C).

Conditional Probability: The probability of event A occurring given that event B has already occurred is denoted P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B), provided P(B) > 0. This modifies the sample space to B only.
Multiplication Rule (derived from conditional probability): P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B)·P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A). For three events: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(A)·P(B|A)·P(C|A ∩ B).

Independent Events: Events A and B are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other. Equivalently: P(A|B) = P(A), P(B|A) = P(B), and P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B). Independence is the key property — when A and B are independent, P(A ∪ B) simplifies to P(A) + P(B) − P(A)·P(B).

NDA Common Trap: Students confuse "mutually exclusive" with "independent." Mutually exclusive events are dependent (unless one has probability 0), because if A occurs, B cannot occur. In independent events, knowing one occurred gives no information about the other.

With Replacement: If a ball is drawn, noted, and replaced, each draw is independent.
Without Replacement: Draws are dependent; the multiplication rule using conditional probabilities must be applied.

6. Bayes' Theorem and Its Applications

Bayes' Theorem: If events A₁, A₂, ..., Aₙ form a partition of the sample space S (i.e., they are mutually exclusive and exhaustive with P(Aᵢ) > 0), and E is any event, then:
P(Aᵢ|E) = [P(Aᵢ) · P(E|Aᵢ)] / [Σⱼ P(Aⱼ) · P(E|Aⱼ)]
The numerator is the probability that the event E occurred via path Aᵢ; the denominator is the total probability of E (Law of Total Probability).

Standard Problem Templates for NDA:
• Urn transfer problems: Two urns contain different compositions of balls. One ball is transferred, then another is drawn. Find the probability of drawing a ball of a certain color. Apply Bayes' theorem to determine which urn the transferred ball came from.
• Factory/production problems: Multiple machines produce items with different defect rates. An item is selected and found defective. Find the probability it came from a particular machine.
• Three bags/machines pattern: Always set up P(Aᵢ) = (number of paths)/total paths, and P(E|Aᵢ) from the composition of each bag/machine.

Working Strategy:
1. Identify all mutually exclusive hypotheses (A₁, A₂, ...).
2. Write down prior probabilities P(Aᵢ).
3. Find likelihoods P(E|Aᵢ) for the observed event E.
4. Compute each term P(Aᵢ)·P(E|Aᵢ).
5. Sum the terms for the denominator.
6. Divide each numerator term by the sum.

The theorem reverses conditional probability — it moves from "given the cause, what is the effect?" to "given the effect, what is the probability of each cause?" This inversion is the hallmark of Bayes' theorem problems in NDA.

7. Discrete Probability Distributions — Mean and Variance of Random Variables

Random Variable: A real-valued function defined on the sample space of a random experiment. For example, in tossing two coins, the number of heads is a random variable X taking values 0, 1, 2.

Probability Distribution: A table or formula giving the probability P(X = x) for each value x of the random variable X. Properties: (i) P(X = x) ≥ 0 for all x; (ii) Σ P(X = x) = 1.

Mean (Expected Value) of a Discrete Random Variable: E(X) = μ = Σ xᵢ · P(xᵢ). Interpretation: the long-run average value if the experiment were repeated many times.

Variance of a Discrete Random Variable: Var(X) = σ² = Σ (xᵢ − μ)² · P(xᵢ) = Σ xᵢ² · P(xᵢ) − μ². The second form is often faster for computation.

Standard Deviation: σ = √Var(X).

NDA Important: Be able to construct the probability distribution table from a word problem (e.g., "two dice are rolled. Let X be the sum. Find the mean and variance."). List all possible values of X, count favorable outcomes for each, divide by n(S), then apply the mean and variance formulas.

Two-point Distribution Example: If X takes values a and b with probabilities p and q (p+q=1): μ = ap + bq; σ² = ab(p−q)² (shortcut result). Knowing this can save time in MCQ exams.

8. Continuous Distributions and Exam-Focused Revision Checklist

Continuous Random Variable: A variable that can take any value in an interval. Described by a probability density function f(x) such that P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫[a to b] f(x) dx. Key properties: f(x) ≥ 0 for all x, and ∫[−∞ to ∞] f(x) dx = 1.

Uniform (Rectangular) Distribution on [a, b]: f(x) = 1/(b−a) for a ≤ x ≤ b. Mean = (a+b)/2. Variance = (b−a)²/12. This is the only continuous distribution regularly asked in NDA/CDS.

Exponential Distribution: f(x) = λe^(−λx), x ≥ 0, λ > 0. Mean = 1/λ, Variance = 1/λ². Occasionally appears in CDS at a descriptive level.

Normal Distribution (Gaussian): f(x) = (1/σ√(2π)) · e^(−(x−μ)²/(2σ²)), −∞ < x < ∞. Bell-shaped, symmetric about μ. About 68% of area lies within μ ± σ, about 95% within μ ± 2σ. In NDA, questions usually test conceptual understanding rather than integration.

Quick Formulas Summary for NDA/CDS:
• Mean (ungrouped): Σxᵢ/n
• Mean (grouped): Σfᵢxᵢ/Σfᵢ
• Median (grouped): l + [(N/2 − CF)/f] · h
• Mode (grouped): l + [(f₁ − f₀)/(2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)] · h
• Variance: Σfᵢxᵢ²/Σfᵢ − (X̄)²
• SD: √Variance
• P(A ∪ B): P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
• P(A|B): P(A ∩ B)/P(B)
• Bayes': P(Aᵢ|E) = P(Aᵢ)P(E|Aᵢ) / Σ P(Aⱼ)P(E|Aⱼ)
• Mean of RV: Σ xᵢP(xᵢ)
• Variance of RV: Σ xᵢ²P(xᵢ) − [Σ xᵢP(xᵢ)]²

Exam Tips: In NDA, Statistics questions are usually straightforward formula applications (1–2 steps). Probability questions are more common and range from basic (cards/dice) to moderate (conditional probability/Bayes'). Practice identifying mutually exclusive vs independent scenarios quickly.

Important facts

  • Mutually exclusive events satisfy P(A ∩ B) = 0; independent events satisfy P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B). They are distinct concepts — mutually exclusive events are generally dependent.
  • The combined standard deviation formula uses both internal spread (σ²) and separation of means (d²), reflecting overall variability when groups are merged.
  • Ogive intersection graphically gives the median. The 'less than' ogive rises from left to right; the 'more than' ogive falls from left to right.
  • P(E') = 1 − P(E) is frequently the fastest route in NDA MCQ problems.
  • Bayes' theorem is essentially reversing conditional probability: it answers 'given an outcome, which cause is more likely?'
  • In Bayes' problems, always verify that the events A₁, A₂, ... form an exhaustive partition (sum of their probabilities equals 1).
  • For drawing without replacement, each successive probability must be computed with updated counts. With replacement, probabilities remain constant across draws.
  • The coefficient of variation is dimensionless, making it the only valid tool to compare variability between datasets with different units or means.
  • Normal distribution is symmetric about the mean; the median, mean, and mode coincide at the center.
  • In grouped data, always check whether class intervals are continuous (e.g., 10–20, 20–30) or discontinuous (requiring adjustment: subtract 0.5 from lower and add 0.5 to upper limits).

Common mistakes

  • Assuming mutually exclusive events are independent — they are actually dependent (if one happens, the other cannot). Only if one event has probability zero can they be both mutually exclusive and independent.
  • Using P(A) + P(B) for P(A ∪ B) without checking mutual exclusivity. The subtraction of P(A ∩ B) is necessary unless events are disjoint.
  • Confusing conditional probability P(A|B) with joint probability P(A ∩ B). Remember: P(A|B) restricts the sample space to B; P(A ∩ B) refers to the original sample space S.
  • Applying the classical probability formula P(E) = n(E)/n(S) to problems where outcomes are NOT equally likely (e.g., biased coins, weighted dice).
  • Forgetting to update counts when drawing without replacement. After removing one item, both the total and the favorable count decrease.
  • Miscalculating the median class in grouped data by using cumulative frequency strictly greater than N/2 instead of the first cumulative frequency exceeding N/2.
  • Using the wrong formula for Mode in grouped data — confusing f₀ (frequency of preceding class) with f₂ (frequency of succeeding class) or misidentifying the modal class.
  • In Bayes' theorem, omitting the denominator (total probability) or computing it incorrectly by forgetting to sum over ALL mutually exclusive causes.
  • Not converting discontinuous class intervals to continuous ones before applying median or mode formulas (e.g., 1–10, 11–20 must become 0.5–10.5, 10.5–20.5).
  • For random variables, squaring the mean instead of mean of squares when computing variance: Var(X) ≠ (Σ xᵢ·P(xᵢ))²; it equals Σ xᵢ²·P(xᵢ) minus the square of the mean.
