1. Coordinate System, Distance Formula and Section Formula (2D)

Every point in a plane is represented by an ordered pair (x, y) referred to two perpendicular axes OX and OY. The distance between two points P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) is given by PQ = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]. This follows directly from the Pythagorean theorem.

The section formula gives the coordinates of a point R dividing the line segment joining P(x₁, y₁) and Q(x₂, y₂) in the ratio m : n. For internal division: R = [(mx₂ + nx₁)/(m + n), (my₂ + ny₁)/(m + n)]. For external division, replace n by −n: R = [(mx₂ − nx₁)/(m − n), (my₂ − ny₁)/(m − n)].

Special cases frequently tested:
• Midpoint (m = n = 1): ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
• Trisection points: divide in ratios 1:2 and 2:1
• Centroid of triangle with vertices (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), (x₃, y₃): G = ((x₁ + x₂ + x₃)/3, (y₁ + y₂ + y₃)/3)

Worked example: Find the point dividing the join of A(2, 3) and B(8, −3) internally in the ratio 2:1. Using section formula: x = (2·8 + 1·2)/(2 + 1) = 18/3 = 6; y = (2·(−3) + 1·3)/3 = (−6 + 3)/3 = −1. The point is (6, −1).

Area of triangle with vertices (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), (x₃, y₃): Δ = ½|x₁(y₂ − y₃) + x₂(y₃ − y₁) + x₃(y₁ − y₂)|. If Δ = 0, the three points are collinear. For a quadrilateral, split into two triangles or use the shoelace formula.

2. Locus and Its Equation (2D)

The locus is the path traced by a point satisfying a given geometric condition. To find the equation of a locus, replace the variable coordinates of the moving point by (h, k), apply the given condition algebraically, and then replace (h, k) by (x, y) in the final result.

Standard locus problems for NDA/CDS:
• Locus of a point equidistant from two fixed points A(x₁, y₁) and B(x₂, y₂) is the perpendicular bisector of AB. Equation: (x − x₁)² + (y − y₁)² = (x − x₂)² + (y − y₂)², which simplifies to a linear equation.
• Locus of a point P such that PA² + PB² = constant leads to a circle or straight line depending on the constant.
• Locus of point dividing a chord of a fixed circle in a given ratio is another circle (homothety).

NDA/CDS often asks: "Find the locus of points whose distance from (a, 0) is k times its distance from the line x = a/e." This is the definition of a conic with eccentricity e.

Example: Find the locus of P(x, y) such that PA = 2PB where A = (1, 3) and B = (2, 1). Squaring both sides: (x − 1)² + (y − 3)² = 4[(x − 2)² + (y − 1)²]. Expanding and simplifying gives x² + y² + 6x − 10y + 9 = 0, a circle.

3. Straight Lines — All Forms and Key Properties (2D)

A straight line is the most fundamental locus in 2D geometry. Its various forms and properties are essential for NDA/CDS:

Forms of a line:
• Slope-intercept form: y = mx + c, where m = tan θ (θ = angle with positive x-axis) and c = y-intercept.
• Point-slope form: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁), passing through (x₁, y₁).
• Two-point form: (y − y₁)/(x − x₁) = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁).
• Intercept form: x/a + y/b = 1, where a and b are x and y intercepts respectively.
• Normal form: x cos α + y sin α = p, where p is the perpendicular distance from origin and α is the angle the perpendicular makes with the positive x-axis.
• General form: Ax + By + C = 0. Slope = −A/B (if B ≠ 0).

Key relations between two lines A₁x + B₁y + C₁ = 0 and A₂x + B₂y + C₂ = 0:
• Parallel: A₁/A₂ = B₁/B₂ ≠ C₁/C₂ (or m₁ = m₂)
• Coincident: A₁/A₂ = B₁/B₂ = C₁/C₂
• Perpendicular: A₁A₂ + B₁B₂ = 0 (or m₁m₂ = −1)

Angle between two lines with slopes m₁ and m₂: tan θ = |m₁ − m₂|/(1 + m₁m₂).

Distance of point (x₁, y₁) from line Ax + By + C = 0: d = |Ax₁ + By₁ + C|/√(A² + B²).

Length of perpendicular from origin to ax + by + c = 0: p = |c|/√(a² + b²).

Family of lines: All lines passing through the intersection of L₁ = 0 and L₂ = 0 are given by L₁ + λL₂ = 0 (λ is a parameter).

4. Triangle Centers and Their Properties (2D)

For a triangle with vertices A(x₁, y₁), B(x₂, y₂), C(x₃, y₃):

Centroid G (intersection of medians): G = ((x₁ + x₂ + x₃)/3, (y₁ + y₂ + y₃)/3). It divides each median in the ratio 2:1 from the vertex.

Orthocentre H (intersection of altitudes): Found by using the fact that altitude from A is perpendicular to BC. Slope of BC = (y₃ − y₂)/(x₃ − x₂), so slope of altitude from A = −(x₃ − x₂)/(y₃ − y₂). Write two altitude equations and solve.
• Special case: If triangle is right-angled at A, H coincides with A.

Circumcentre O (equidistant from all three vertices; centre of circumcircle): Solve OA² = OB² = OC². Two equations from OA² = OB² and OB² = OC² give the coordinates.
• For a right triangle, circumcentre is the midpoint of the hypotenuse.
• Circumradius R = abc/(4Δ), where a, b, c are side lengths and Δ is area.

Incentre I (intersection of angle bisectors; centre of incircle): I = (ax₁ + bx₂ + cx₃)/(a + b + c), (ay₁ + by₂ + cy₃)/(a + b + c), where a, b, c are the lengths of sides opposite to A, B, C respectively.
• Inradius r = Δ/s, where s = (a + b + c)/2 is the semi-perimeter.

Euler's theorem: The centroid G lies on the line joining orthocentre H and circumcentre O, and HG : GO = 2 : 1. This line is called the Euler line.

Important triangle centers relationship: H, G, O are collinear (Euler line); IG relates to the incircle; the nine-point circle has centre at the midpoint of HO and radius R/2.

NDA/CDS frequently tests: finding the orthocentre of a triangle with vertices (1, 2), (3, 4), (2, 5), or identifying which center satisfies a given condition.

5. Circle — Standard Equations, Tangents, Normals and Chords (2D)

Standard forms of a circle:
• Centre (0, 0), radius r: x² + y² = r²
• Centre (h, k), radius r: (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²
• General form: x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0. Centre = (−g, −f), radius = √(g² + f² − c). Condition for real circle: g² + f² > c.
• Diameter form: If (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) are endpoints of a diameter, the circle is (x − x₁)(x − x₂) + (y − y₁)(y − y₂) = 0.

Position of a point (x₁, y₁) w.r.t. x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0:
• Outside if S₁ = x₁² + y₁² + 2gx₁ + 2fy₁ + c > 0
• On the circle if S₁ = 0
• Inside if S₁ < 0

Tangent to a circle:
• At point (x₁, y₁) on x² + y² = r²: xx₁ + yy₁ = r²
• At point (x₁, y₁) on (x − h)² + (y − k)² = r²: (x − h)(x₁ − h) + (y − k)(y₁ − k) = r²
• In general form: xx₁ + yy₁ + g(x + x₁) + f(y + y₁) + c = 0, where (x₁, y₁) lies on the circle.
• Line y = mx + c is tangent to x² + y² = r² if c² = r²(1 + m²).
• Length of tangent from (x₁, y₁) to circle S = 0 is √S₁.

Normal to a circle always passes through the centre. The normal at any point on the circle is the line joining that point to the centre.

Chord with given midpoint (x₁, y₁): T = S₁, i.e., xx₁ + yy₁ + g(x + x₁) + f(y + y₁) + c = x₁² + y₁² + 2gx₁ + 2fy₁ + c.

Length of chord intercepted by line lx + my + n = 0 on circle x² + y² = r²: 2√[r² − p²], where p is the perpendicular distance from centre to the line.

Intersection of two circles: The common chord (if they intersect) is S₁ − S₂ = 0 (a straight line called the radical axis). If they touch externally/internally, the radical axis is the common tangent.

Condition for a line to be a tangent, secant or non-intersecting to a circle is determined by comparing the distance from centre to the line with the radius.

6. Conic Sections — Parabola (2D)

Definition: A parabola is the locus of a point equidistant from a fixed point (focus) and a fixed straight line (directrix). Eccentricity e = 1.

Standard form: y² = 4ax (a > 0)
• Vertex: (0, 0)
• Focus: (a, 0)
• Directrix: x = −a (i.e., x + a = 0)
• Axis: y = 0 (the x-axis)
• Latus rectum: 4a (length of chord through focus perpendicular to axis)
• Endpoints of latus rectum: (a, ±2a)

Other standard forms:
• y² = −4ax: Focus (−a, 0), Directrix x = a
• x² = 4ay: Focus (0, a), Directrix y = −a
• x² = −4ay: Focus (0, −a), Directrix y = a

Parametric form: Any point on y² = 4ax can be written as (at², 2at), where t is a parameter.

Tangent at (at², 2at): ty = x + at². In slope form: y = mx + a/m (tangent exists only if m ≠ 0).
• Condition for tangency: Line y = mx + c is tangent to y² = 4ax if c = a/m.

Normal at (at², 2at): y + tx = 2at + at³. In slope form: y = mx − 2am − am³.

Chord of contact from external point (x₁, y₁): yy₁ = 2a(x + x₁).

Important properties:
• The portion of the tangent between the point of contact and the directrix subtends a right angle at the focus.
• The tangents at the extremities of a focal chord intersect at right angles on the directrix.
• The triangle formed by three points on a parabola has orthocentre also on the parabola.
• Area of triangle inscribed in parabola y² = 4ax with vertices at t₁, t₂, t₃: ½a²|(t₁ − t₂)(t₂ − t₃)(t₃ − t₁)|.

7. Conic Sections — Ellipse and Hyperbola (2D)

ELLIPSE:
Definition: Locus of a point whose sum of distances from two fixed points (foci) is constant and equal to 2a. Eccentricity 0 < e < 1.

Standard form: x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, where a > b > 0
• Centre: (0, 0)
• Vertices: (±a, 0)
• Foci: (±ae, 0), where e = √(1 − b²/a²)
• Directrices: x = ±a/e
• Length of major axis: 2a; minor axis: 2b
• Length of latus rectum: 2b²/a
• Endpoints of LR: (±ae, ±b²/a)
• Relationship: b² = a²(1 − e²)

Other form (vertical major axis, a < b): x²/a² + y²/b² = 1 with b > a. Foci at (0, ±be), directrices y = ±b/e.

Tangent at (x₁, y₁): xx₁/a² + yy₁/b² = 1. In slope form: y = mx ± √(a²m² + b²). Condition for tangency.

Normal at (x₁, y₁): a²x/x₁ − b²y/y₁ = a² − b².

Parametric form: (a cos θ, b sin θ).

POLAR FORM (from focus): r = a(1 − e²)/(1 − e cos θ).

HYPERBOLA:
Definition: Locus of a point whose difference of distances from two fixed foci is constant (equal to 2a). Eccentricity e > 1.

Standard form: x²/a² − y²/b² = 1
• Centre: (0, 0)
• Vertices: (±a, 0)
• Foci: (±ae, 0), where e = √(1 + b²/a²)
• Directrices: x = ±a/e
• Transverse axis: 2a; conjugate axis: 2b
• Length of latus rectum: 2b²/a
• Asymptotes: y = ±(b/a)x → x²/a² − y²/b² = 0 (pair of straight lines)
• Relationship: b² = a²(e² − 1)

Rectangular/hyperbola: a = b, so x² − y² = a², e = √2, asymptotes y = ±x (perpendicular).

Tangent at (x₁, y₁): xx₁/a² − yy₁/b² = 1. In slope form: y = mx ± √(a²m² − b²). Condition for tangency: c² = a²m² − b².

Normal at (x₁, y₁): a²x/x₁ + b²y/y₁ = a² + b².

Parametric form: (a sec θ, b tan θ).

Conjugate hyperbola: −x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, or x²/a² − y²/b² = −1.

8. Angle Between Two Curves and Orthogonal Trajectories (2D)

The angle between two curves at their point of intersection is defined as the angle between their tangents at that point. If curves C₁ and C₂ intersect at P, find the slopes m₁ and m₂ of their tangents at P. Then tan θ = |m₁ − m₂|/(1 + m₁m₂).

Two curves are orthogonal (cut each other at right angles) if m₁m₂ = −1 at every point of intersection.

Family of circles orthogonal to a given circle: If a family of circles S + λL = 0 is given (where S = 0 is a circle and L = 0 is a line), the orthogonal trajectories can be found by replacing λ by −1/λ in appropriate cases, or by solving the differential equation.

Key theorem: The circles x² + y² + 2g₁x + c = 0 and x² + y² + 2g₂x + c = 0 (same constant term c) cut orthogonally for all values of g₁ and g₂. More generally, two circles S₁ = 0 and S₂ = 0 cut orthogonally if 2g₁g₂ + 2f₁f₂ = c₁ + c₂.

NDA/CDS frequently tests:
• Whether two given curves intersect orthogonally
• Finding the value of a parameter for which two curves are orthogonal
• Finding the orthogonal trajectory of a family of curves

Orthogonal trajectory of family of curves f(x, y, c) = 0: Differentiate to get dy/dx = φ(x, y), then replace dy/dx by −dx/dy to get the differential equation of orthogonal trajectories, and solve it.

9. Three-Dimensional Coordinate System and Direction Cosines/Ratios (3D)

In 3D space, a point is represented by (x, y, z) referred to three mutually perpendicular planes: XY-plane, YZ-plane, and ZX-plane. The three coordinate axes are X, Y, Z.

Distance between P(x₁, y₁, z₁) and Q(x₂, y₂, z₂): PQ = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²].

Section formula in 3D: Point dividing join of P(x₁,y₁,z₁) and Q(x₂,y₂,z₂) in ratio m:n internally: [(mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂+ny₁)/(m+n), (mz₂+nz₁)/(m+n)].

Direction cosines (DCs) of a line: If a directed line makes angles α, β, γ with the positive directions of x, y, z axes respectively, then l = cos α, m = cos β, n = cos γ are its direction cosines. They satisfy l² + m² + n² = 1.

Direction ratios (DRs) a, b, c: Any three numbers proportional to the DCs. DCs = (a, b, c)/√(a² + b² + c²).

If P(x₁,y₁,z₁) and Q(x₂,y₂,z₂) are two points, DRs of PQ are (x₂−x₁), (y₂−y₁), (z₂−z₁).

Angle between two lines with DCs (l₁, m₁, n₁) and (l₂, m₂, n₂):
cos θ = |l₁l₂ + m₁m₂ + n₁n₂|
If lines have DRs (a₁,b₁,c₁) and (a₂,b₂,c₂):
cos θ = |a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ + c₁c₂|/√(a₁²+b₁²+c₁²)·√(a₂²+b₂²+c₂²)

Conditions:
• Parallel: a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂
• Perpendicular: a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ + c₁c₂ = 0

10. Equation of a Line in Space (3D)

Cartesian form: A line passing through point A(x₁, y₁, z₁) with direction ratios a, b, c has equation:
(x − x₁)/a = (y − y₁)/b = (z − z₁)/c = r (say)
If DCs are l, m, n, then (x − x₁)/l = (y − y₁)/m = (z − z₁)/n.

Vector form: r⃗ = a⃗ + λb⃗, where a⃗ is position vector of a point on the line and b⃗ is a vector parallel to the line.

Line through two points A(a⃗) and B(b⃗): r⃗ = a⃗ + λ(b⃗ − a⃗).

Symmetric form: When a line intersects the coordinate planes, the intercept form is useful. If a line makes intercepts p, q, r on the axes, its equation is x/p + y/q + z/r = 1 only for a plane, not a line. For a line, use the ratio form above.

Angle between two lines in 3D: If lines have DRs (a₁,b₁,c₁) and (a₂,b₂,c₂), the acute angle θ between them satisfies:
cos θ = |a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ + c₁c₂|/√(a₁²+b₁²+c₁²)·√(a₂²+b₂²+c₂²)

Parallel lines: (a₁/a₂) = (b₁/b₂) = (c₁/c₂)
Perpendicular lines: a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ + c₁c₂ = 0

Projection of line segment AB on a line with DCs (l, m, n): l(x₂−x₁) + m(y₂−y₁) + n(z₂−z₁).

Condition for coplanarity of two lines: Lines (x−x₁)/a₁ = (y−y₁)/b₁ = (z−z₁)/c₁ and (x−x₂)/a₂ = (y−y₂)/b₂ = (z−z₂)/c₂ are coplanar iff
|x₂−x₁ y₂−y1 z₂−z₁|
|a1 b1 c1 | = 0
|a2 b2 c2 |

11. Equation of a Plane in Space (3D)

A plane is a flat surface such that any line joining two points on it lies entirely on the plane.

General equation: Ax + By + Cz + D = 0, where (A, B, C) are DRs of the normal to the plane.

Normal form: px + qy + rz = d, where (p, q, r) are DCs of the normal from origin and d is the perpendicular distance from origin to the plane.

Plane through a point and perpendicular to a given line:
If plane passes through (x₁, y₁, z₁) and is perpendicular to line with DRs (a, b, c):
a(x − x₁) + b(y − y₁) + c(z − z₁) = 0.

Plane through three non-collinear points (x₁,y₁,z₁), (x₂,y₂,z₂), (x₃,y₃,z₃):
|x − x1 y − y1 z − z1|
|x2−x1 y2−y1 z2−z1| = 0
|x3−x1 y3−y1 z3−z1|

Intercept form: x/a + y/b + z/c = 1, where a, b, c are x, y, z intercepts respectively.

Plane parallel to a plane Ax + By + Cz + D = 0 is Ax + By + Cz + k = 0 (same normal, different constant).

Angle between two planes: For planes A₁x + B₁y + C₁z + D₁ = 0 and A₂x + B₂y + C₂z + D₂ = 0:
cos θ = |A₁A₂ + B₁B₂ + C₁C₂|/√(A₁²+B₁²+C₁²)·√(A₂²+B₂²+C₂²)
This equals the angle between their normals.

Perpendicular planes: A₁A₂ + B₁B₂ + C₁C₂ = 0.

Parallel planes: A₁/A₂ = B₁/B₂ = C₁/C₂.

Distance of point (x₁, y₁, z₁) from plane Ax + By + Cz + D = 0:
d = |Ax₁ + By₁ + Cz₁ + D|/√(A² + B² + C²).

Foot of perpendicular from P(x₁,y₁,z₁) to plane: Use the formula along with the line through P perpendicular to the plane.

12. Shortest Distance Between Two Lines (3D) and Skew Lines

Two lines in 3D are:
• Intersecting: meet at a point (coplanar)
• Parallel: same direction ratios
• Skew: neither parallel nor intersecting (non-coplanar)

For skew lines, there exists a unique shortest distance — the length of the common perpendicular.

Cartesian form: For lines (x−x₁)/a₁ = (y−y₁)/b₁ = (z−z₁)/c₁ and (x−x₂)/a₂ = (y−y₂)/b2 = (z−z₂)/c₂:
Shortest distance SD = |(x₂−x₁)a₁(b₁c₂−b₂c₁) + (y₂−y₁)b₁(c₁a₂−c₂a₁) + (z₂−z₁)c₁(a₁b₂−a₂b₁)| / √[(b₁c₂−b₂c₁)² + (c₁a₂−c₂a1)² + (a₁b₂−a₂b₁)²]

Compact determinant form:
SD = |
|x2−x1 y2−y1 z2−z1|
|a1 b1 c1 |
|a2 b2 c2 |
| / √[(b₁c₂−b₂c₁)² + (c₁a₂−c₂a1)² + (a₁b₂−a₂b₁)²]

Vector form: For r⃗ = a⃗₁ + λb⃗₁ and r⃗ = a⃗₂ + μb⃗₂:
SD = |(a⃗₂ − a⃗₁) · (b⃗₁ × b⃗₂)| / |b⃗₁ × b⃗₂|

If SD = 0, the lines intersect.
If b⃗₁ × b⃗₂ = 0, the lines are parallel.

Condition for lines to be coplanar (intersecting or parallel): (a⃗₂ − a⃗₁) · (b⃗₁ × b⃗₂) = 0, equivalently the scalar triple product [a⃗₂−a⃗₁, b⃗₁, b⃗₂] = 0.

Common perpendicular direction: b⃗₁ × b⃗₂.

NDA/CDS frequently tests: finding shortest distance, checking if lines intersect, finding equations of common perpendicular, and determining coplanarity of given lines.

13. Reflection, Image and Key Constructions in 2D Geometry

Image of a point in a line:
To find the image of P(x₁, y₁) in the line ax + by + c = 0:
Let the image be Q(h, k). The midpoint M of PQ lies on the line, and PQ is perpendicular to the line.
Formulas: (h−x₁)/a = (k−y₁)/b = −2(ax₁+by1+c)/(a²+b²).
So h = x₁ − 2a(ax₁+by₁+c)/(a²+b²), k = y₁ − 2b(ax₁+by₁+c)/(a²+b²).

Reflection of a point in various lines:
• In x-axis: (x, y) → (x, −y)
• In y-axis: (x, y) → (−x, y)
• In origin: (x, y) → (−x, −y)
• In line y = x: (x, y) → (y, x)
• In line y = −x: (x, y) → (−y, −x)
• In line x = k: (x, y) → (2k − x, y)
• In line y = k: (x, y) → (x, 2k − y)

Composition of reflections:
• Reflection in two parallel lines separated by distance d: equivalent to translation by 2d perpendicular to the lines.
• Reflection in two intersecting lines at angle α: equivalent to rotation by 2α about the intersection point.

Image of a line in another line: Replace each point (x, y) on the first line by its image in the second line and eliminate parameters.

Pedal triangle: The triangle formed by feet of perpendiculars from a point P to the sides of a given triangle. Special case: pedal triangle of circumcentre is the medial triangle.

Circle through three points: Find the general equation x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 by substituting the three points to get three linear equations in g, f, c.

14. Important Theorems and Results Frequently Asked in NDA/CDS

Collinearity of three points: Points A, B, C are collinear if area of triangle ABC = 0, or slope AB = slope BC, or if one point divides AB externally or internally in some ratio.

Concurrent lines: Three lines are concurrent if they meet at a single point. Check by solving any two and verifying the third is satisfied.

Cyclic quadrilateral condition: A quadrilateral is cyclic if and only if opposite angles sum to 180°. For points on a circle, this is automatically satisfied.

Power of a point: For a point P outside circle S = 0, the power is S₁ = x₁² + y₁² + 2gx₁ + 2fy₁ + c. Length of tangent = √S₁. Product of lengths of segments of any chord through P is constant = |S₁|.

Radical axis: The locus of points having equal power w.r.t. two circles is a straight line perpendicular to the line joining their centres. For circles S₁ = 0 and S₂ = 0, radical axis is S₁ − S₂ = 0.

Radical centre: For three circles taken in pairs, the three radical axes are concurrent at a point called the radical centre.

Family of circles through intersection of two circles: S₁ + λ(S₁ − S₂) = 0 (passes through common points). Family of circles touching a line L = 0 at point P: S + λL = 0.

Condition for general equation to represent a circle: Coefficient of x² = coefficient of y² and coefficient of xy = 0.

Orthogonality condition for two circles: 2g₁g₂ + 2f₁f₂ = c₁ + c₂.

Common tangent to two circles: Direct common tangents meet externally at the external centre of similitude; transverse common tangents meet internally at the internal centre of similitude. These divide the line joining centres in the ratio of radii externally and internally respectively.

Important facts

  • Distance formula in 2D: PQ = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]; in 3D add (z₂−z₁)².
  • Section formula for internal division in ratio m:n: [(mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), (my₂+ny₁)/(m+n)].
  • Centroid of triangle: average of vertices' coordinates; divides each median 2:1.
  • Incentre: weighted average of vertices with side lengths as weights: (ax₁+bx₂+cx₃)/(a+b+c).
  • Condition for perpendicular lines: m₁m₂ = −1 (2D) or a₁a₂ + b₁b₂ + c₁c₂ = 0 (3D).
  • Condition for parallel lines: slopes equal (2D) or direction ratios proportional (3D).
  • Circle general form x²+y²+2gx+2fy+c=0 has centre (−g,−f) and radius √(g²+f²−c).
  • Parabola y²=4ax: focus (a,0), directrix x=−a, latus rectum = 4a, parametric point (at², 2at).
  • Ellipse x²/a²+y²/b²=1: eccentricity e=√(1−b²/a²), foci (±ae,0), LR = 2b²/a.
  • Hyperbola x²/a²−y²/b²=1: e=√(1+b²/a²), foci (±ae,0), asymptotes y=±(b/a)x.
  • Orthogonal circles condition: 2g₁g₂ + 2f₁f₂ = c₁ + c₂.
  • Shortest distance between skew lines uses scalar triple product with cross product of direction vectors.
  • Plane normal form: px+qy+rz=d where (p,q,r) are DCs of normal and d is perpendicular distance from origin.
  • Coplanarity of two lines: scalar triple product [(r⃗₂−r⃗₁), b⃗₁, b⃗₂] = 0.
  • Euler line: orthocentre, centroid, and circumcentre are collinear with HG:GO = 2:1.
  • Image of point (x₁,y₁) in line ax+by+c=0: use (h−x₁)/a = (k−y₁)/b = −2(ax₁+by₁+c)/(a²+b²).
  • Angle between two lines with slopes m₁,m₂: tan θ = |m₁−m₂|/(1+m₁m₂).
  • Length of perpendicular from (x₁,y₁) to Ax+By+C=0: |Ax₁+By₁+C|/√(A²+B²).
  • Parametric form of ellipse: (a cos θ, b sin θ); of hyperbola: (a sec θ, b tan θ).
  • Director circle of ellipse x²/a²+y²/b²=1 is x²+y²=a²+b² (locus of point of intersection of perpendicular tangents).

Common mistakes

  • Confusing internal and external section formula: external division uses (mx₂−nx₁)/(m−n), not (mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n). Always check if the ratio point lies between the two given points.
  • Using wrong sign for parabola y²=−4ax: focus becomes (−a,0) and directrix becomes x=a, not the other way around.
  • Forgetting that the eccentricity of ellipse is always less than 1, while for hyperbola it is always greater than 1. Mixing up the formulas e=√(1−b²/a²) and e=√(1+b²/a²).
  • Writing tangent to parabola y²=4ax as y=mx+a/m instead of y=mx+a/m. The correct form is y = mx + a/m.
  • Applying 2D distance formula in 3D problems without adding the z-component. Always include (z₂−z₁)² in 3D.
  • Computing direction cosines without normalising: DCs must satisfy l²+m²+n²=1. DRs do not need this condition.
  • For circle orthogonality: writing 2g1g2+2f1f2=c1+c2 as 2g1g2+2f1f2=c1−c2. The signs of c matter carefully.
  • When finding shortest distance between skew lines, forgetting that the numerator is the absolute value of the scalar triple product and the denominator is the magnitude of the cross product of direction vectors.
  • Assuming all lines in 3D either intersect or are parallel — they can also be skew. Check coplanarity condition before assuming intersection.
  • Confusing the length of latus rectum: for parabola it is 4a, for ellipse it is 2b²/a, for hyperbola it is 2b²/a. Many students use 4a for all conics incorrectly.
  • For the general circle equation, forgetting the condition g²+f²>c for a real circle. If g²+f²<c, the radius is imaginary and no real circle exists.
  • Using slope-intercept form when the line is vertical (slope undefined). Vertical lines must be written as x = constant.
  • Finding angle between two planes by taking the angle between their position vectors instead of their normal vectors. The angle between planes equals the angle between their normals.
  • In parametric forms, confusing parameters: for ellipse use (a cos θ, b sin θ), NOT (a sin θ, b cos θ) unless specifically shifted. For hyperbola use (a sec θ, b tan θ), not trig functions interchangeably.
