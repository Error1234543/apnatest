Vector Algebra — Complete Notes for NDA & CDS

NDA & CDSEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Vectors and Scalars

['A scalar quantity is completely specified by its magnitude alone. Examples include mass, time, temperature, energy, and distance. A vector quantity requires both magnitude and direction for complete specification. Examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, and electric field.', 'In NDA/CDS Mathematics, vectors are typically represented as directed line segments. If A is the initial point and B is the terminal point, the vector is denoted as AB⃗ or simply a⃗. The magnitude (or length/modulus) of vector a⃗ is denoted by |a⃗| or ‖a⃗‖ and is always non-negative.', 'Two fundamental conditions define a physical quantity as a vector: it must possess both magnitude and direction, AND it must satisfy the laws of vector addition (triangle law or parallelogram law). Rotation, for instance, has magnitude and direction but does not satisfy vector addition — hence it is NOT a vector. Time has magnitude but no direction — hence not a vector.', 'Graphical representation: a vector is shown as an arrow. The length of the arrow represents the magnitude (to some scale), and the arrowhead indicates the direction. In analytical geometry, vectors are often expressed in terms of unit vectors i⃗, j⃗, k⃗ along the x, y, z axes respectively.']

2. Types of Vectors — Definitions and Distinctions

['Zero Vector (Null Vector): A vector whose initial and terminal points coincide. Denoted by 0⃗. Its magnitude is zero, and its direction is undefined. For any vector a⃗, a⃗ + 0⃗ = a⃗.', 'Unit Vector: A vector whose magnitude is exactly 1. The unit vector in the direction of a⃗ is given by â = a⃗/|a⃗|. If a⃗ = xi⃗ + yj⃗ + zk⃗, then â = (xi⃗ + yj⃗ + zk⃗)/√(x²+y²+z²). NDA frequently asks to find the unit vector along or opposite to a given vector.', 'Position Vector: The vector from the origin O to a point P(x,y,z) is called the position vector of P, denoted OP⃗ = xi⃗ + yj⃗ + zk⃗. This is crucial for deriving equations of lines and planes.', 'Equal Vectors: Two vectors are equal if they have the same magnitude and the same direction, regardless of the position of their initial points. Free vectors are equal if they can be made to coincide by translation.', 'Collinear (Parallel) Vectors: Two non-zero vectors a⃗ and b⃗ are collinear if and only if a⃗ = λb⃗ for some scalar λ ∈ ℝ. If λ > 0, they are in the same direction; if λ < 0, they are in opposite directions.', 'Coplanar Vectors: Three or more vectors are coplanar if they lie in the same plane (or parallel planes). Three vectors a⃗, b⃗, c⃗ are coplanar iff their scalar triple product [a⃗ b⃗ c⃗] = 0.', 'Co-initial Vectors: Vectors having the same initial point. Example: OA⃗, OB⃗, OC⃗ all starting from O.', 'Negative of a Vector: The vector with the same magnitude as a⃗ but opposite direction is denoted by −a⃗. Thus a⃗ + (−a⃗) = 0⃗.', 'Like and Unlike Vectors: Like vectors have the same direction; unlike vectors have opposite directions.']

3. Addition and Subtraction of Vectors — Laws and Properties

['Triangle Law of Vector Addition: If two vectors a⃗ and b⃗ are represented by two sides of a triangle taken in order, then their resultant is given by the third side taken in the opposite order. Mathematically: if AB⃗ = a⃗ and BC⃗ = b⃗, then AC⃗ = a⃗ + b⃗. In component form, if a⃗ = a₁i⃗ + a₂j⃗ + a₃k⃗ and b⃗ = b₁i⃗ + b₂j⃗ + b₃k⃗, then a⃗ + b⃗ = (a₁+b₁)i⃗ + (a₂+b₂)j⃗ + (a₃+b₃)k⃗.', 'Parallelogram Law of Vector Addition: If two vectors a⃗ and b⃗ are represented by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram, then the diagonal passing through their common point gives the resultant a⃗ + b⃗. The magnitude is |a⃗ + b⃗| = √(|a⃗|² + |b⃗|² + 2|a⃗||b⃗|cos θ), where θ is the angle between a⃗ and b⃗.', 'Polygon Law: An extension of the triangle law. If a number of vectors can be represented in magnitude and direction by the sides of a polygon taken in order, then their resultant is given by the closing side of the polygon taken in the opposite order.', 'Subtraction: a⃗ − b⃗ = a⃗ + (−b⃗). Geometrically, it is the vector from the tip of b⃗ to the tip of a⃗ when both are placed tail-to-tail.', 'Key Properties: Commutative: a⃗ + b⃗ = b⃗ + a⃗. Associative: (a⃗ + b⃗) + c⃗ = a⃗ + (b⃗ + c⃗). Existence of additive identity: a⃗ + 0⃗ = a⃗. Existence of additive inverse: a⃗ + (−a⃗) = 0⃗.', 'Scalar Multiplication: For scalar m and vector a⃗, ma⃗ has magnitude |m||a⃗| and direction along a⃗ (if m>0) or opposite to a⃗ (if m<0). Distributive laws: m(a⃗+b⃗) = ma⃗+mb⃗ and (m+n)a⃗ = ma⃗+na⃗.']

4. Direction Cosines, Direction Ratios, and Resolution of Vectors

["Direction Cosines: If a vector a⃗ makes angles α, β, γ with the positive directions of the x, y, z axes respectively, then cos α, cos β, cos γ are called the direction cosines (d.c.'s) of the vector, usually denoted by l, m, n respectively.", 'Fundamental Relation: l² + m² + n² = 1. This is derived from the fact that if a⃗ = |a⃗|(li⃗ + mj⃗ + nk⃗), then |a⃗|²(l²+m²+n²) = |a⃗|².', "Direction Ratios (d.r.'s): Any three numbers proportional to the direction cosines are called direction ratios. If l, m, n are d.c.'s, then a, b, c where a = kl, b = km, c = kn (for some k≠0) are d.r.'s. Conversely, if a, b, c are d.r.'s of a vector, then its d.c.'s are a/√(a²+b²+c²), b/√(a²+b²+c²), c/√(a²+b²+c²).", 'Resolution of a Vector into Components: A vector a⃗ = xi⃗ + yj⃗ + zk⃗ can be resolved into three rectangular components along the coordinate axes. The magnitude is |a⃗| = √(x² + y² + z²). The direction cosines are l = x/|a⃗|, m = y/|a⃗|, n = z/|a⃗|.', "Angle Between Two Vectors Using Direction Cosines: If l₁,m₁,n₁ and l₂,m₂,n₂ are the d.c.'s of two vectors, then the angle θ between them satisfies cos θ = l₁l₂ + m₁m₂ + n₁n₂.", 'NDA Frequently Asked: Finding direction cosines when given direction ratios, finding the angle between two vectors given their components, checking if three points are collinear using direction ratios of segments joining them.', 'Important Result: The direction cosines of a line making equal angles with the three coordinate axes are (1/√3, 1/√3, 1/√3).']

5. Scalar Product (Dot Product) — Formula, Properties and Applications

['Definition: The scalar (dot) product of two non-zero vectors a⃗ and b⃗ is defined as a⃗·b⃗ = |a⃗||b⃗|cos θ, where θ is the angle between them (0 ≤ θ ≤ π). It is a scalar quantity.', 'Component Form: If a⃗ = a₁i⃗ + a₂j⃗ + a₃k⃗ and b⃗ = b₁i⃗ + b₂j⃗ + b₃k⃗, then a⃗·b⃗ = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃. Also, |a⃗| = √(a₁²+a₂²+a₃²).', 'Formula for Angle: cos θ = (a⃗·b⃗) / (|a⃗||b⃗|). This is one of the most frequently tested results.', 'Properties: Commutative: a⃗·b⃗ = b⃗·a⃗. Distributive: a⃗·(b⃗+c⃗) = a⃗·b⃗ + a⃗·c⃗. For scalar m: (ma⃗)·b⃗ = m(a⃗·b⃗). Note: a⃗·b⃗ = a⃗·c⃗ does NOT imply b⃗ = c⃗ (this is a very common mistake).', 'Orthogonality Condition: Two non-zero vectors are perpendicular (θ = π/2) iff a⃗·b⃗ = 0. This is extensively used to find unknown parameters in vector problems.', 'Projection of a Vector: The scalar projection of a⃗ on b⃗ is (a⃗·b⃗)/|b⃗|. The vector projection of a⃗ on b⃗ is [(a⃗·b⃗)/|b⃗|²] · b⃗. Similarly, projection of a⃗ on itself has magnitude |a⃗|.', 'Work Done: In physics applications, W = F⃗·d⃗ (dot product of force and displacement).', 'NDA Tip: To prove two vectors are perpendicular, compute their dot product and show it equals zero. To find the angle, use the cosine formula.']

6. Cross Product (Vector Product) — Formula, Properties and Geometric Meaning

['Definition: The cross (vector) product of two non-zero vectors a⃗ and b⃗ is a vector defined as a⃗ × b⃗ = |a⃗||b⃗|sin θ · n̂, where θ is the angle between a⃗ and b⃗ (0 ≤ θ ≤ π), and n̂ is a unit vector perpendicular to both a⃗ and b⃗ following the right-hand rule.', 'Component Form (Determinant Method): If a⃗ = a₁i⃗ + a₂j⃗ + a₃k⃗ and b⃗ = b₁i⃗ + b₂j⃗ + b₃k⃗, then a⃗ × b⃗ = (a₂b₃ − a₃b₂)i⃗ − (a₁b₃ − a₃b₁)j⃗ + (a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)k⃗. This is computed as the determinant|i⃗ j⃗ k⃗; a₁ a₂ a₃; b₁ b₂ b₃|.', 'Properties: NOT commutative: a⃗ × b⃗ = −(b⃗ × a⃗) (anti-commutative). Distributive: a⃗ × (b⃗ + c⃗) = a⃗ × b⃗ + a⃗ × c⃗. For scalar m: (ma⃗) × b⃗ = m(a⃗ × b⃗). Note: a⃗ × b⃗ = a⃗ × c⃗ does NOT imply b⃗ = c⃗.', 'Magnitude and Geometric Interpretation: |a⃗ × b⃗| = |a⃗||b⃗|sin θ. This equals the area of the parallelogram formed by a⃗ and b⃗ as adjacent sides. The area of the triangle with these vectors as adjacent sides is ½|a⃗ × b⃗|.', 'Parallel/ Collinear Condition: a⃗ and b⃗ are parallel (collinear) iff a⃗ × b⃗ = 0⃗. Equivalently, sin θ = 0, so θ = 0 or π.', 'Perpendicularity: a⃗ × b⃗ is perpendicular to BOTH a⃗ and b⃗. This property is critical for finding normal vectors to planes.', 'i⃗×i⃗=j⃗×j⃗=k⃗×k⃗=0⃗; i⃗×j⃗=k⃗, j⃗×k⃗=i⃗, k⃗×i⃗=j⃗; j⃗×i⃗=−k⃗, k⃗×j⃗=−i⃗, i⃗×k⃗=−j⃗. These cyclic relations must be memorized.']

7. Scalar Triple Product and Vector Triple Product

['Scalar Triple Product [a⃗ b⃗ c⃗]: Defined as a⃗·(b⃗ × c⃗). It is a scalar. Cyclic property: a⃗·(b⃗ × c⃗) = b⃗·(c⃗ × a⃗) = c⃗·(a⃗ × b⃗). Interchange of any two vectors changes the sign: b⃗·(a⃗ × c⃗) = −a⃗·(b⃗ × c⃗). Determinant form: [a⃗ b⃗ c⃗] = |a₁ a₂ a₃; b₁ b₂ b₃; c₁ c₂ c₃|.', 'Geometric Interpretation: |a⃗·(b⃗ × c⃗)| = volume of the parallelepiped formed by a⃗, b⃗, c⃗ as concurrent edges. The volume of the tetrahedron with these edges is ⅙|a⃗·(b⃗ × c⃗)|.', 'Coplanarity Condition: Three vectors a⃗, b⃗, c⃗ are coplanar if and only if [a⃗ b⃗ c⃗] = 0. This is one of the highest-yield results for NDA/CDS.', "Vector Triple Product a⃗ × (b⃗ × c⃗): Called the 'back-cab' rule. a⃗ × (b⃗ × c⃗) = (a⃗·c⃗)b⃗ − (a⃗·b⃗)c⃗. Similarly, (a⃗ × b⃗) × c⃗ = (a⃗·c⃗)b⃗ − (b⃗·c⃗)a⃗. Key distinction: a⃗ × (b⃗ × c⃗) ≠ (a⃗ × b⃗) × c⃗ in general (cross product is not associative).", 'NDA Frequently Asked: Proving coplanarity of three vectors using scalar triple product, finding volume of parallelepiped/tetrahedron, expanding vector triple products.']

8. Projections and Key Applications of Dot and Cross Products

['Scalar Projection: The projection of vector a⃗ onto vector b⃗ (scalar form) = a⃗·b̂ = a⃗·b⃗/|b⃗| = |a⃗|cos θ. This is a scalar quantity representing the component of a⃗ in the direction of b⃗.', 'Vector Projection: The projection of a⃗ on b⃗ (vector form) = (a⃗·b⃗/|b⃗|²) · b⃗. Its magnitude is |a⃗|·|cos θ|.', "Projection of a⃗ on a line with direction cosines l, m, n: If a⃗ = a₁i⃗ + a₂j⃗ + a₃k⃗, the projection is al + bm + cn where (l,m,n) are d.c.'s of the line.", 'Area Applications: Area of triangle with vertices A, B, C: ½|AB⃗ × AC⃗|. Area of parallelogram with adjacent sides a⃗ and b⃗: |a⃗ × b⃗|. Area of triangle with sides a⃗ and b⃗ meeting at angle θ: ½|a⃗||b⃗|sin θ.', 'Finding a Unit Vector Perpendicular to Two Vectors: n̂ = (a⃗ × b⃗)/|a⃗ × b⃗|. This is widely used in 3D geometry for finding normals.', 'Work and Moment Applications: Work = F⃗·d⃗. Moment of force about a point = r⃗ × F⃗ (vector product). These appear occasionally in NDA Mathematics.', 'Common Mistake: Confusing scalar projection (a scalar) with vector projection (a vector). The scalar projection can be negative; the vector projection carries direction information.']

9. Vector Equation of a Line — Parametric and Cartesian Forms

['Line Passing Through a Point and Parallel to a Vector: The vector equation of a line passing through a point with position vector a⃗ and parallel to vector b⃗ is r⃗ = a⃗ + λb⃗, where λ is a scalar parameter. Every point on the line corresponds to a value of λ.', 'Cartesian Form from Vector Form: If a⃗ = x₁i⃗ + y₁j⃗ + z₁k⃗ and b⃗ = b₁i⃗ + b₂j⃗ + b₃k⃗, and r⃗ = xi⃗ + yj⃗ + zk⃗, then substituting into r⃗ = a⃗ + λb⃗ gives x = x₁ + λb₁, y = y₁ + λb₂, z = z₁ + λb₃. Eliminating λ: (x−x₁)/b₁ = (y−y₁)/b₂ = (z−z₁)/b₃. This is the standard symmetric (Cartesian) form.', 'Line Through Two Points: If a⃗ and b⃗ are position vectors of two points A and B, the line through A and B is r⃗ = a⃗ + λ(b⃗ − a⃗). In Cartesian form: (x−x₁)/(x₂−x₁) = (y−y₁)/(y₂−y₁) = (z−z₁)/(z₂−z₁).', 'Angle Between Two Lines: If b⃗₁ and b⃗₂ are direction vectors of two lines, the acute angle θ between them satisfies cos θ = |b⃗₁·b⃗₂|/(|b⃗₁||b⃗₂|). Note the absolute value — we take the acute angle.', 'Condition for Parallel and Perpendicular Lines: Two lines are parallel iff their direction vectors are proportional (b⃗₁ = kb⃗₂). They are perpendicular iff b⃗₁·b⃗₂ = 0.', 'Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines: For lines r⃗ = a⃗₁ + λb⃗₁ and r⃗ = a⃗₂ + μb⃗₂, the shortest distance is |(a⃗₂ − a⃗₁)·(b⃗₁ × b⃗₂)| / |b⃗₁ × b⃗₂|. Lines are intersecting if this distance is zero.']

10. Vector Equation of a Plane — Normal, Point-Normal, and Interception Forms

['Plane Through a Point with Given Normal: The vector equation of a plane passing through a point with position vector a⃗ and having normal vector n⃗ is (r⃗ − a⃗)·n⃗ = 0, which simplifies to r⃗·n⃗ = a⃗·n⃗. This is a cornerstone result.', 'Cartesian Form (Point-Normal): If a⃗ = (x₁,y₁,z₁) and n⃗ = (A,B,C), then A(x−x₁) + B(y−y₁) + C(z−z₁) = 0. Expanding: Ax + By + Cz = D where D = Ax₁ + By₁ + Cz₁.', 'Normal Form: If p is the perpendicular distance from the origin to the plane and n̂ is the unit vector along the normal, the equation is r⃗·n̂ = p. In Cartesian form with direction cosines l,m,n of the normal: lx + my + nz = p.', 'Plane Through Three Points: If a⃗, b⃗, c⃗ are position vectors of three non-collinear points, the plane through them is given by (r⃗ − a⃗)·[(b⃗ − a⃗) × (c⃗ − a⃗)] = 0, or equivalently [r⃗ − a⃗, b⃗ − a⃗, c⃗ − a⃗] = 0 (scalar triple product = 0).', 'Interception Form: A plane cutting intercepts a, b, c on the x, y, z axes respectively has equation x/a + y/b + z/c = 1. Derived from the general plane equation.', 'Angle Between Two Planes: For planes r⃗·n⃗₁ = d₁ and r⃗·n⃗₂ = d₂, the angle between the planes equals the angle between their normals: cos θ = |n⃗₁·n⃗₂|/(|n⃗₁||n⃗₂|).', 'Plane Parallel to a Vector: A plane r⃗·n⃗ = d is parallel to a vector b⃗ if n⃗·b⃗ = 0 (the direction vector lies in the plane, hence perpendicular to the normal).', 'Distance of a Point from a Plane: The perpendicular distance from point with position vector a⃗ to the plane r⃗·n⃗ = d is |a⃗·n⃗ − d|/|n⃗|. In Cartesian form, distance from (x₁,y₁,z₁) to Ax+By+Cz+D=0 is |Ax₁+By₁+Cz₁+D|/√(A²+B²+C²).']

Important facts

  • Scalar product: a⃗·b⃗ = |a⃗||b⃗|cos θ = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃. Used for finding angles and projections.
  • Cross product: a⃗ × b⃗ = |a⃗||b⃗|sin θ · n̂. Magnitude equals area of parallelogram; half for triangle.
  • Scalar triple product [a⃗ b⃗ c⃗] = a⃗·(b⃗ × c⃗). Vectors are coplanar iff [a⃗ b⃗ c⃗] = 0.
  • Vector triple product: a⃗ × (b⃗ × c⃗) = (a⃗·c⃗)b⃗ − (a⃗·b⃗)c⃗ (back-cab rule).
  • Direction cosines satisfy l² + m² + n² = 1. Direction ratios are proportional to direction cosines.
  • Unit vector in direction of a⃗: â = a⃗/|a⃗|. Always normalize before using as a direction indicator.
  • Two vectors are perpendicular iff a⃗·b⃗ = 0. Two vectors are parallel iff a⃗ × b⃗ = 0⃗.
  • Line through point a⃗ parallel to b⃗: r⃗ = a⃗ + λb⃗. Plane through point a⃗ with normal n⃗: (r⃗ − a⃗)·n⃗ = 0.
  • Volume of parallelepiped = |[a⃗ b⃗ c⃗]|. Volume of tetrahedron = ⅙|[a⃗ b⃗ c⃗]|.
  • Scalar projection of a⃗ on b⃗ = a⃗·b⃗/|b⃗|. Vector projection = (a⃗·b⃗/|b⃗|²)b⃗.
  • Shortest distance between skew lines = |(a⃗₂−a⃗₁)·(b⃗₁×b⃗₂)|/|b⃗₁×b⃗₂|. Zero means lines intersect.
  • i⃗×j⃗=k⃗, j⃗×k⃗=i⃗, k⃗×i⃗=j⃗; reversed order gives negative. i⃗×i⃗=j⃗×j⃗=k⃗×k⃗=0⃗.
  • Plane in normal form: r⃗·n̂ = p where p is perpendicular distance from origin.
  • Plane through three points: [r⃗−a⃗, b⃗−a⃗, c⃗−a⃗] = 0 (scalar triple product condition).

Common mistakes

  • Confusing dot product with cross product: dot product gives a scalar, cross product gives a vector perpendicular to both input vectors.
  • Forgetting that a⃗·b⃗ = a⃗·c⃗ does NOT imply b⃗ = c⃗. Many students incorrectly cancel vectors in dot products.
  • Same error with cross product: a⃗ × b⃗ = a⃗ × c⃗ does NOT imply b⃗ = c⃗. Cross product cancellation is invalid.
  • Mixing up scalar triple product [a⃗ b⃗ c⃗] = a⃗·(b⃗ × c⃗) with vector triple product a⃗ × (b⃗ × c⃗). These are fundamentally different operations.
  • Using a⃗ × (b⃗ × c⃗) = (a⃗ × b⃗) × c⃗. The cross product is NOT associative. Always apply back-cab rule carefully.
  • Forgetting the absolute value in cos θ = |a⃗·b⃗|/(|a⃗||b⃗|) when finding the acute angle between two lines or planes.
  • Writing direction cosines as direction ratios without normalizing. d.c.'s must satisfy l²+m²+n²=1; d.r.'s do not.
  • In the plane equation (r⃗ − a⃗)·n⃗ = 0, mistakenly writing r⃗·n⃗ = a⃗ instead of r⃗·n⃗ = a⃗·n⃗. The RHS must be a scalar.
  • Using |a⃗ + b⃗| = |a⃗| + |b⃗|. This is false in general; the correct formula uses the cosine rule: |a⃗+b⃗|² = |a⃗|²+|b⃗|²+2|a⃗||b⃗|cos θ.
  • Confusing the scalar projection (a⃗·b̂, a scalar) with the vector projection [(a⃗·b̂)b̂, a vector].
  • In coplanarity problems, setting the scalar triple product to zero but computing it as a dot product of three vectors directly without first taking the cross product of two.
  • Sign errors in the cross product determinant expansion. Remember the middle term j⃗ gets a negative sign: −(a₁b₃−a₃b₁)j⃗.
  • Assuming three vectors are coplanar just because they share a common point. Coplanarity depends on the scalar triple product being zero, not on shared initial points.
  • When finding the angle between two planes, using the direction ratios of the planes instead of their normal vectors. The angle between planes equals the angle between normals.
