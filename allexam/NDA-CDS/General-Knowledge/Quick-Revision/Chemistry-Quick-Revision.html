Definitions

  • Atom: The smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical properties, consisting of protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Mole: The amount of substance containing exactly 6.022 × 10²³ elementary entities (Avogadro constant).
  • Element: A pure substance consisting of atoms with the same number of protons (atomic number); cannot be broken down by chemical means.
  • Compound: A substance formed when two or more different elements combine chemically in a fixed ratio.
  • Ion: An atom or molecule carrying a net electric charge due to loss or gain of one or more electrons.
  • Isotope: Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers (different neutron count).
  • Allotrope: Different structural forms of the same element in the same physical state (e.g., diamond, graphite, fullerenes of carbon).
  • Oxidation: Loss of electrons or increase in oxidation state during a chemical reaction.
  • Reduction: Gain of electrons or decrease in oxidation state.
  • Electrolyte: A substance that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water by the movement of ions.
  • Catalyst: A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed in the process.
  • Colloid: A heterogeneous mixture where one substance is dispersed as fine particles throughout another; particle size 1–1000 nm.
  • pH: The negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration; pH = −log[H⁺]. Neutral at 7, acidic <7, alkaline >7.

Important facts

  • Neutron was discovered by James Chadwick (1932). Electron by J.J. Thomson (1897). Proton by Rutherford ( canal rays experiment ).
  • Rutherford's gold foil experiment led to discovery of the atomic nucleus; most of the atom is empty space.
  • Moseley established that atomic number (not atomic mass) is the fundamental property of an element.
  • Avogadro number = 6.022 × 10²³. One mole of any gas at STP occupies 22.4 litres.
  • Diamond is the hardest natural substance; Graphite is the softest and a good conductor of electricity.
  • Heavy water (D₂O) contains deuterium; used as moderator in nuclear reactors.
  • Liquid air separation gives N₂ (boils at −196°C) before O₂ (boils at −183°C).
  • The pH of human blood is approximately 7.35–7.45 (slightly alkaline).
  • Haber's process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ at 450°C, 200 atm, Fe catalyst; ammonia is precursor for fertilisers, explosives, and nylon.
  • Contact process: manufacture of H₂SO₄; V₂O₅ catalyst; key step is SO₂ → SO₃.
  • Lindane (666), DDT — organochlorine pesticides; persist in environment, biomagnify up food chains.
  • Bhopal gas tragedy (1984): methyl isocyanate (CH₃NCO) leak from Union Carbide plant in Madhya Pradesh.
  • Ozone hole was detected over Antarctica; treaty addressing it is the Montreal Protocol (1987).
  • Chernobyl nuclear disaster (1986) released radiation mainly as radioiodine and caesium-137.
  • Rusting equation: 4Fe + 3O₂ + xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O; prevention by painting, oiling, galvanisation, sacrificial protection.
  • Biogas composition: ~60% CH₄ + ~40% CO₂, produced by anaerobic decomposition of organic waste by methanogens.
  • Natural gas main component is methane (CH₄); LPG is propane (C₃H₈) + butane (C₄H₁₀).
  • Coal types in order of carbon content: Peat (<60%) → Lignite (~60–70%) → Bituminous (~70–85%) → Anthracite (>90%).
  • CNG (Compressed Natural Gas) is mainly methane; cleaner fuel producing less pollution than petrol/diesel.
  • Teflon (PTFE) is resistant to chemicals and heat; used in non-stick cookware.
  • Nylon was the first fully synthetic fibre (polyamide); developed by Carothers at DuPont.

Memory aids

  • Alkali metals flame colours mnemonic: Litmus turns red, Sodium is Yellow, Potassium looks Lilac → Li red, Na yellow, K lilac.
  • Metal reactivity series (most to least): K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Sn > Pb > (H) > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au. Remember: 'Keep Napoleon Carefully Maintaining his Zinc Armoured Fighting Tank safely near the Pub, as Copper handles silver and gold well.'
  • pH scale: 0–6 Acidic (sour), 7 Neutral (water), 8–14 Alkaline (bitter, soapy). Memory: 'Acids bite, bases slide.'
  • Oxidation is loss, Reduction is gain — mnemonic: OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons). Also LEO says GER.
  • Colorful ions in flame test: Cu²⁺ blue-green, K⁺ lilac, Na⁺ yellow, Ca²⁺ brick-red, Ba²⁺ apple-green, Sr²⁺ crimson-red.
  • Halogens mnemonic for states at room temperature: F₂ (pale yellow gas), Cl₂ (greenish-yellow gas), Br₂ (reddish-brown liquid), I₂ (violet solid) — 'Flowers Can Blossom In gardens' by state: Gas, Gas, Liquid, Solid.
  • Periodic trends memory aid: 'Across the period, atoms shrink and hold electrons tighter (smaller radius, higher IE). Down the group, atoms grow larger and lose electrons more easily (larger radius, lower IE).' This explains metallic character increasing down and to the left.

Exam tips

  • NDA/CDS Chemistry questions are generally from Class 9–12 NCERT level; focus on clarity of concepts rather than extreme numericals.
  • Expect 5–10 Chemistry questions per NDA General Ability Test paper; manage time — do not spend more than 30–40 seconds per question.
  • Memorise the reactivity series and solubility rules; these appear frequently in inorganic chemistry questions.
  • For organic chemistry, know functional group tests: Lucas test (alcohols), Tollen's reagent (aldehydes), Iodoform test (methyl ketones/ethanol), Bromine water test (unsaturation).
  • Be prepared for statement-based questions on environmental chemistry (pollution, greenhouse effect, ozone depletion, acid rain) — read each statement carefully.
  • In NDA, negative marking is 1/3rd; if unsure between two options, guess; avoid blind guesses when eliminating nothing.
  • Revision priority order: first master Organic basics (naming, reactions), then inorganic (periodic table, p-block, s-block), then physical (gases, solutions, electrochemistry).
  • Previous-year trends show recurring questions on: allotropes of carbon, noble gases, water purification, fertilisers, plastics, corrosion, and the periodic table.
  • For CDS GK, questions may include defence-related chemistry (explosives, propellants, smoke screens) — know basics of TNT, RDX, dynamite, phosphorus bombs.
  • Use elimination technique: many Chemistry MCQs can be solved by ruling out one or two obviously wrong options rather than deriving the exact answer.

Common traps

  • Confusing atomic mass with atomic number: atomic number = number of protons (defines the element); mass number = protons + neutrons.
  • Assuming all isotopes are radioactive: most isotopes are stable; only certain combinations are unstable (e.g., C-14, U-235).
  • Thinking a larger atom has higher ionization energy: opposite is true — larger atoms hold outer electrons more loosely, so IE decreases down a group.
  • Confusing covalent and ionic compound conductivity: ionic compounds conduct only when molten/dissolved (ions mobile); covalent compounds generally do not conduct.
  • Mixing up exothermic and endothermic: exothermic = heat exits (surroundings warm, ΔH negative); endothermic = heat enters (surroundings cool, ΔH positive).
  • Forgetting that noble gases have full outer shells (He has 2, others have 8); they do not readily form bonds under normal conditions.
  • Mistaking soap for works in hard water: hard water contains Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ which form insoluble scum with soap; detergents (sulfonates) do not form scum.
  • Assuming benzene has three alternating double bonds (Kekulé structure): benzene is a resonance hybrid with delocalised π-electrons; all C–C bonds are equal in length.
  • Confusing empirical and molecular formula: empirical = simplest whole-number ratio; molecular = actual number of atoms (e.g., glucose: empirical CH₂O, molecular C₆H₁₂O₆).
  • Forgetting Van't Hoff factor for dissociating/associating solutes in colligative property calculations (use i multiplier).
  • Treating diamond as a molecular solid: diamond is a giant covalent network solid; it has no discrete molecules.
  • Believing all oxides are acidic or basic: some are amphoteric (Al₂O₃, ZnO), some are neutral (CO, NO), some are acidic (CO₂, SO₂, P₂O₅).
