Indian Armed Forces: Organisation and Command Structure

India maintains three uniformed services under the Ministry of Defence: the Indian Army, the Indian Navy, and the Indian Air Force. The President of India is the Supreme Commander of the Armed Forces. The Chiefs of Staff Committee comprises the Chief of the Army Staff (COAS), the Chief of the Naval Staff (CNS), and the Chief of the Air Staff (CAS), with the Chairman COSC (now integrated as the Chief of Defence Staff, CDS) providing single-point military advice to the government.

The Indian Army, the largest service, is organised into commands. As of recent restructuring, there are seven operational commands — four field armies (Northern, Southern, Western, Eastern), one Central Command, one Armoured Corps Headquarters, and the Strategic Forces Command. Each army command is headed by a Lieutenant General. The Army has approximately 1.45 lakh active personnel plus an extensive reserves system including the Territorial Army.

The Indian Navy operates from two main fleet commands — the Western Fleet (headquartered at Mumbai) and the Eastern Fleet (headquartered at Visakhapatnam). There are also operational commands for the Andaman and Nicobar Command (a tri-service theatre command), the Maritime Air Command, and logistics commands. India's blue-water capability has expanded significantly with the induction of aircraft carriers INS Vikramaditya and the indigenous INS Vikrant.

The Indian Air Force is organised into six functional commands — Central, Eastern, Northern, South Western, Southern, and Maintenance Command — headquartered at various stations including New Delhi, Lucknow, Srinagar, Jaipur, Trivandrum, and Kolhapur respectively. The IAF maintains approximately 42 squadrons of combat aircraft including MiG-21 variants, Su-30MKI, Rafale, Mirage 2000, Jaguar, Tejas, and helicopter units.

Defence Research and Production Organisations

The Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO), headquartered in New Delhi, is the primary agency responsible for defence technology research and development. Established in 1958, DRDO operates over 50 laboratories across India. Key systems developed include the Agni missile series (intermediate-range ballistic missiles), Prithvi tactical missiles, Akash surface-to-air missiles, Nag anti-tank guided missiles, and the Barak naval air-defence system. DRDO also works on cyber warfare, drone technology, and electronic combat systems.

The Ordnance Factory Board (OFB), restructured in 2021 into seven Defence Public Sector Enterprises — Bharat Forge Limited, Bharat Dynamics Limited, Military Engineering Services, Advanced Weapons and Equipment, etc. — was historically the largest ordnance manufacturing network. These DPSUs manufacture ammunition, weapons, vehicles, and allied equipment.

Key defence PSUs under the Ministry of Defence include Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL), Bharat Heavy Electricals Limited (BHEL), Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders Limited (MDL), Garden Reach Shipbuilders & Engineers (GRSE), Hindustan Shipyard Limited (HSL), and Bharat Dynamics Limited (BDL). HAL manufactures and maintains military aircraft including Tejas, Dhruv helicopter, and engages in deep maintenance and modernisation.

Other important bodies include the Defence Intelligence Agency (DIA), established in 2010 as India's primary civilian intelligence agency for military purposes; the National Investigation Agency (NIA) for counter-terrorism; and the National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC) for cybersecurity of critical sectors.

Nuclear Doctrine and Strategic Forces

India's nuclear doctrine was formally adopted in January 2003 after a draft version in 1999. The core principles are: no-first-use (NFU) — India will not be the first to use nuclear weapons, only retaliating after a massive conventional or nuclear attack; credible minimum deterrence; and flexible options proportional to the scale of provocation.

The Strategic Forces Command (SFC), established in 2003 and headquartered in New Delhi, serves as the tri-service command responsible for operating India's nuclear arsenal. It falls under the Political Council of the Nuclear Command Authority (NCA), chaired by the Prime Minister, while the Executive Council, chaired by the National Security Advisor, handles day-to-day operations.

India's nuclear delivery systems form a strategic triad: land-based Agni missiles (Agni-I through Agni-V, with ranges from 700 km to over 5,000 km), submarine-launched K-series missiles (K-15 from INS Chakra and upcoming K-4/K-5 from INS Arihant-class SSBNs), and air-delivered bombs carried by Mirage 2000 and Su-30MKI aircraft.

The Ballistic Missile Defence (BMD) programme aims to create a layered shield against incoming missiles. It includes the Prithvi Air Defence (PAD) interceptors for exo-atmospheric engagement and the Advanced Air Defence (AAD) systems for endo-atmospheric interception. The India-Russia cooperation on the S-400 Triumf air-defence system represents another significant capability enhancement.

India conducted its first nuclear test in 1974 (Smiling Buddha), followed by five underground tests at Pokhran in 1998 (Operation Shakti), declaring itself a nuclear-weapon state. India is not a signatory to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) or the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT).

Border Management and Security Infrastructure

India shares land borders totaling approximately 15,106 km with seven countries: Pakistan (3,323 km), China (3,488 km), Nepal (1,751 km), Bhutan (470 km), Myanmar (1,643 km), Bangladesh (4,096 km), and Afghanistan (short section via Siachen). Managing these borders involves multiple agencies.

The Border Security Force (BSF), raised in 1965, is the primary agency guarding the western borders with Pakistan and Punjab/Rajasthan sectors. It also guards the international border with Bangladesh in several states. The Central Reserve Police Force (CRPF) and Indo-Tibetan Border Police (ITBP) are deployed along the northern borders, particularly the sensitive China border.

The Sashastra Seema Bal (SSB) is primarily tasked with guarding the India-Nepal and India-Bhutan borders, though its responsibilities extend to internal security roles as well. The Assam Rifles, the oldest armed force on the Indian mainland (raised 1835), guards the India-Myanmar border and operates under the Ministry of Defence but is operationally controlled by the Indian Army.

The coastguard function is performed by the Indian Coast Guard, which was established in 1977 and came under the Ministry of Defence in 2001. It monitors India's exclusive economic zone (EEZ) covering approximately 2.02 million square kilometers.

Modern border infrastructure includes the Integrated Border Management System (IBMS) using thermal imaging, microwave links, and fiber-optic fencing along the Pakistan border. The concept of Smart Fencing incorporates sensors, drones, and AI-based surveillance.

India's Defence Diplomacy and Strategic Partnerships

India has pursued an active defence diplomacy since independence, evolving from non-alignment during the Cold War to multi-alignment in the post-Cold War era. Key partnerships include:

- India-Russia: The longest-standing strategic partnership, encompassing major arms supply (S-400, Su-30MKI, insourcing of Russian nuclear submarines, BrahMos co-production), joint exercises Indra, and growing defence industrial collaboration. Russia remains India's largest defence supplier despite efforts at diversification.

- India-USA: The partnership has deepened significantly, highlighted by the 2016 Communications Compatibility Security Agreement (COMCASA) and the Basic Exchange and Cooperation Agreement (BECA), both foundational to major defence transactions. Key acquisitions include MQ-9 Reaper drones, P-8I maritime patrol aircraft, and MH-60R helicopters. The 2020 LEMOA and 2022 Fundamental Agreement for Exchange and Cooperation (FEMA) complete the four foundational agreements framework.

- India-France: A cornerstone partnership featuring Dassault Rafale procurement, Scorpene submarine co-development (Kalvari-class), and the BrahMos partnership. France is now India's second-largest defence supplier.

- India-Israel: Strategic cooperation covers missile defence (Barak-8 co-development),无人机 technology, and intelligence sharing. Israel is among India's top defence suppliers.

Regional partnerships include India-Maldives, India-Sri Lanka, and India-Myanmar engagements through Operation Madad and disaster relief operations.

International Organisations and Multilateral Forums

The United Nations (UN), established in 1945 after the failure of the League of Nations, is the primary global intergovernmental organisation. Its six principal organs are the General Assembly, Security Council, Economic and Social Council, Trusteeship Council, International Court of Justice, and Secretariat. India is a founding member and contributes significantly to UN peacekeeping missions, having been among the top troop-contributing nations historically.

The United Nations Security Council (UNSC) has 15 members: 5 permanent (P5 — USA, UK, France, Russia, China) and 10 non-permanent elected for two-year terms. India has long advocated for UNSC reform and a permanent seat, forming the G4 bloc with Germany, Japan, and Brazil.

Key multilateral groupings relevant to India include:

- ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations): India follows an Act East Policy, engaging through the East Asia Summit (EAS) and ASEAN-India cooperation frameworks.

- BRICS: Originally BRIC (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa), expanded in 2024 to include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, and Saudi Arabia. Focuses on economic cooperation and reform of global financial architecture.

- G20: India held the presidency in 2023, focusing on inclusive development and climate finance. The G20 brings together major advanced and emerging economies.

- Quad (Quadrilateral Security Dialogue): Australia, India, Japan, and the USA. Originally formed in 2007, revitalized around 2017, focusing on Indo-Pacific freedom, openness, and connectivity.

- Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO): India and Pakistan joined in 2017. The SCO focuses on regional security, counter-terrorism, and economic cooperation among member states including China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, and Iran.

Major Treaties, Conventions and Strategic Agreements

Several key international agreements and conventions shape India's defence and foreign policy landscape:

- Nuclear-related: India is not a signatory to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT), or the Fissile Material Cut-off Treaty (FMCT). However, India signed the 123 Agreement with the USA in 2008, enabling civilian nuclear trade despite not being an NPT member.

- Maritime: The UNCLOS (United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea) of 1982 governs maritime rights. India ratified it and claims a 12-nautical-mile territorial sea, 200-nautical-mile Exclusive Economic Zone, and extended continental shelf claims.

- Counter-terrorism: India is a signatory to numerous UN conventions on terrorism, including the International Convention for the Suppression of Terrorist Bombings (1997) and the International Convention for the Suppression of Terrorist Financing (1999). India has consistently advocated for a Comprehensive Convention on International Terrorism (CCIT) at the UN.

- Trade and commerce: India is a member of the World Trade Organization (WTO) since 1995. Regional trade frameworks include BIMSTEC (Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation) and RCEP discussions (India did not join the final agreement).

- Border agreements: Multiple boundary treaties exist with neighbors including the 1950 Indo-Nepal Treaty of Peace and Friendship, the 1960 Indo-Bhutan Treaty, and the 1974 Indo-Myanmar Boundary Protocol.

Current Developments in Defence and International Affairs

Recent developments shaping India's defence and international posture include:

- Atmanirbhar Bharat in Defence: The 2020 Defence Procurement Procedure emphasizes indigenization through positive indigenization lists (PILs banning imports of select weapon systems). Major acquisitions have shifted toward domestic platforms — Tejas MK1A fighter production, Arjun MK1T tank upgrades, and INS Vikrant commissioning in 2022.

- One Country Many Solutions initiative: Launched to leverage India's defence industry for global supply chain diversification, particularly appealing to nations seeking alternatives to traditional suppliers.

- Defense Technology and Industry Initiative (DTII): With the USA to enhance defense industrial cooperation and technology transfer.

- Indo-Pacific strategy: India's SAGAR (Security and Growth for All in the Region) doctrine emphasizes maritime security cooperation, capacity building, and freedom of navigation in the Indo-Pacific, counterbalancing Chinese assertiveness.

- Ukraine conflict impact (2022-present): India maintained strategic autonomy, continuing energy imports from Russia while strengthening ties with Western nations and providing humanitarian assistance.

- Defense exports: India emerged as a notable defense exporter, shipping BrahMos missiles, Sukhoi engines, trucks, and military hardware to countries like Vietnam, Philippines, and Middle Eastern nations.

- CHAI and global health: India's vaccine diplomacy during COVID-19 (Supply of COVID vaccines to over 100 countries) demonstrated soft power utilization.

- Recent military modernization programs include the Indian Navy's plan for three aircraft carrier groups, 55 warships, and 11 submarines by 2030; the IAF's acquisition of 114 Rafale and ongoing Tejas production; and the Army's induction of Pinaka multi-barrel rocket launchers and K9 Vajra self-propelled howitzers.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing BSF (border security force) with ITBP (Indo-Tibetan Border Police) — BSF handles Pakistan/Bangladesh borders while ITBP handles China border
  • Thinking India is a signatory to NPT — India has NOT signed the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty
  • Mixing up First Chief of Defence Staff (Bipin Rawat) with the current CDS — check appointment dates carefully
  • Confusing Agni missiles (ballistic) with BrahMos (cruise missile) — different trajectories and uses
  • Assuming SSB ( Sashastra Seema Bal) handles all international borders — it specifically deals with Nepal and Bhutan borders
  • Not recognizing that INS Vikrant is India's FIRST indigenous aircraft carrier (not INS Vikramaditya which was acquired from Russia)
  • Confusing NDA and CDS — NDA is the recruitment exam; CDS is a position within the armed forces
  • Forgetting that Chief of Defence Staff (CDS) is a three-star rank position, not a four-star equivalent
  • Assuming India joined BRICS early — India was a founding member in 2009 (as BRIC)
  • Not distinguishing between strategic forces (nuclear) and conventional forces in command structures
  • Overlooking that India-Russia defense cooperation includes co-production (BrahMos) and co-development projects
  • Confusing the year of nuclear tests: Pokhran-I (1974, one device) vs Pokhran-II (1998, five devices)
