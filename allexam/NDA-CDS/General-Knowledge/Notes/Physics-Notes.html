Physical World and Measurement

Physics is the study of matter, energy, and their interactions across space and time. The subject rests on fundamental concepts that unify all branches — conservation laws, symmetry principles, and dimensional analysis.

Physical Quantities and Units: Every measurable quantity has a numerical value and a unit. There are seven SI base quantities: length (metre, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s), electric current (ampere, A), thermodynamic temperature (kelvin, K), amount of substance (mole, mol), and luminous intensity (candela, cd). All other units are derived from these. For example, force is measured in newtons (N = kg·m/s²), energy in joules (J = kg·m²/s²), and power in watts (W = J/s).

Dimensions: Every physical quantity can be expressed as a product of powers of the seven base dimensions [M], [L], [T], [A], [K], [mol], [cd]. Common dimensional formulas include: velocity [LT⁻¹], acceleration [LT⁻²], force [MLT⁻²], work/energy [ML²T⁻²], power [ML²T⁻³], momentum [MLT⁻¹], pressure [ML⁻¹T⁻²], density [ML⁻³], and angular velocity [T⁻¹]. Dimensional analysis is used to check equation consistency, convert between unit systems, and derive relationships between physical quantities.

Significant Figures: These reflect the precision of measurement. Rules for significant figures: all non-zero digits are significant; zeros between non-zero digits are significant; leading zeros are not significant; trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant; trailing zeros without a decimal point are ambiguous. In calculations, addition/subtraction follows the least number of decimal places, while multiplication/division follows the least number of significant figures.

Errors in Measurement: Systematic errors arise from faulty instruments or methods and can be corrected. Random errors occur unpredictably and are reduced by averaging repeated measurements. Absolute error is the magnitude of difference between measured and true value. Relative error is absolute error divided by true value. Percentage error is relative error × 100.

NDA/CDS Focus: Expect direct dimensional formula questions, unit conversion problems, and identification of correct/incorrect equations using dimensional homogeneity. Questions like 'Which of the following pairs have the same dimensions?' (e.g., work and energy; impulse and momentum) are very common.

Kinematics — Motion in One and Two Dimensions

Types of Motion: Motion is classified as translational (all points move equally), rotational (about an axis), oscillatory/vibratory (to-and-fro about a mean position), and random (no definite path). Rest and motion are relative terms depending on the frame of reference.

Scalar and Vector Quantities: Scalars have magnitude only (distance, speed, mass, energy, temperature). Vectors have both magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum). Vectors obey triangle, parallelogram, and polygon laws of addition. A zero vector has zero magnitude; a unit vector has magnitude one and indicates direction.

Equations of Motion (Constant Acceleration):
- v = u + at
- s = ut + ½at²
- v² = u² + 2as
where u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, s = displacement.
These apply only when acceleration is constant and motion is along a straight line. They do NOT apply when acceleration varies with time.

Projectile Motion: A projectile is an object thrown at an angle to the horizontal under gravity alone. The horizontal component of velocity remains constant (neglecting air resistance); the vertical component changes uniformly due to g.
- Time of flight: T = 2u sinθ / g
- Maximum height: H = u² sin²θ / 2g
- Horizontal range: R = u² sin2θ / g
- Equation of trajectory: y = x tanθ − gx²/(2u² cos²θ) — a parabola.
Range is maximum at θ = 45°. Two complementary angles (θ and 90°−θ) give the same range. The trajectory equation shows the path is parabolic.

Relative Velocity: If body A moves with velocity v⃗_A and body B with v⃗_B, then the velocity of A relative to B is v⃗_AB = v⃗_A − v⃗_B. For objects moving in the same direction, subtract speeds; for opposite directions, add them. This is crucial in river-boat and rain-man problems.

NDA/CDS Focus: Numerical problems on equations of motion, identifying correct statements about projectile motion, relative velocity in 1D, and graphical interpretation of displacement-time and velocity-time graphs (slope of v-t graph gives acceleration; area under v-t graph gives displacement).

Laws of Motion and Friction

Newton's First Law (Law of Inertia): An object remains at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force. Inertia is the inherent property of a body to resist change in its state of rest or motion. Mass is the measure of inertia — greater mass means greater inertia.

Newton's Second Law: The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction of the force. Mathematically, F = ma (when mass is constant). Momentum p = mv. Impulse J = F·t = change in momentum = m(v − u). This law is the fundamental equation of dynamics.

Newton's Third Law: To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Action and reaction forces act on different bodies and therefore do not cancel each other. Example: when you walk, you push the ground backward (action), and the ground pushes you forward (reaction).

Conservation of Linear Momentum: In the absence of external forces, the total linear momentum of a system remains conserved. This explains recoil of a gun, rocket propulsion (action of escaping gases produces forward thrust), and swimming.

Friction: Friction opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact. It arises due to interlocking of microscopic irregularities and molecular adhesion.
- Static friction (f_s): acts when there is no relative motion; f_s ≤ μ_s N. It is self-adjusting — it equals the applied force up to its limiting value.
- Limiting friction (f_s,max = μ_s N): the maximum value of static friction just before motion starts.
- Kinetic friction (f_k = μ_k N): acts when surfaces are already sliding; μ_k < μ_s, so kinetic friction is less than limiting static friction.
- Rolling friction is much smaller than sliding friction — this is why wheels are used.
- Friction is independent of the area of contact (for moderate pressures) but depends on the nature of surfaces and the normal reaction.

Centripetal Force: For circular motion, a center-seeking force is required: F_c = mv²/r = mω²r. This is not a new force but the net force toward the center provided by tension, friction, gravity, or normal reaction depending on the situation. Examples: car on a banked road, cyclist leaning, satellite orbiting Earth.

NDA/CDS Focus: Assertion-reason questions on Newton's laws are frequently asked. Distinguish between static, limiting, and kinetic friction. Banked road problems and centripetal force concept questions appear regularly. Understand that centrifugal force is a pseudo-force felt only in a rotating (non-inertial) frame.

Work, Energy and Power

Work: Work is done when a force displaces a body. W = F·s·cosθ = F⃗ · s⃗ where θ is the angle between force and displacement. Work is a scalar quantity measured in joules (J). Positive work: force has a component in the direction of displacement (0° ≤ θ < 90°). Negative work: force opposes displacement (90° < θ ≤ 180°). Zero work when θ = 90° (e.g., centripetal force does no work since it is perpendicular to velocity).

Energy: The capacity to do work. Measured in joules. Two main forms:
- Kinetic Energy (KE): Energy of motion. KE = ½mv² = p²/2m where p is momentum.
- Potential Energy (PE): Stored energy due to position or configuration. Gravitational PE = mgh (near Earth's surface). Elastic PE in a spring = ½kx² where k is the spring constant and x is the extension/compression.

Work-Energy Theorem: The net work done on a body equals the change in its kinetic energy: W_net = ΔKE = ½mv² − ½mu². This theorem is extremely useful for solving problems without calculating acceleration or time.

Conservation of Mechanical Energy: In the absence of non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance), the sum of kinetic and potential energy remains constant: KE + PE = constant. A freely falling body, a pendulum (ideal), and a body on a smooth incline are classic examples. At the highest point of vertical projection, KE = 0 and PE is maximum; at the lowest point, PE = 0 and KE is maximum.

Power: Rate of doing work. P = W/t = F·v (when force and velocity are in the same direction). Measured in watts (W). 1 horsepower (hp) = 746 W ≈ 3/4 kW.

Collisions: When two bodies collide, momentum is always conserved (if no external force acts). In elastic collisions, kinetic energy is also conserved. In inelastic collisions, KE is lost (to heat, sound, deformation). A perfectly inelastic collision is one where the bodies stick together after collision.

NDA/CDS Focus: Relationship between KE and momentum (KE = p²/2m) is frequently tested — if two bodies have equal momentum, the lighter body has more KE. Identify which quantities are conserved in different types of collisions. Numerical problems on work-energy theorem are common.

Gravitation and Properties of Matter

Newton's Law of Gravitation: Every particle of matter attracts every other particle with a force directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres. F = GMm/r² where G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² is the universal gravitational constant.

Acceleration due to Gravity (g): Near Earth's surface, g = GM/R² ≈ 9.8 m/s². Important variations:
- With height (h << R): g' = g(1 − 2h/R)
- With depth (d): g' = g(1 − d/R) — at the centre of Earth, g = 0.
- Latitude variation: g is maximum at poles (≈ 9.83 m/s²) and minimum at equator (≈ 9.78 m/s²) due to Earth's rotation and oblate shape.
- g is independent of the mass of the falling body.

Orbital and Escape Velocities: Orbital velocity is the tangential speed needed to maintain a circular orbit: v_o = √(GM/r) = √(gr) near surface. For low Earth orbit, v_o ≈ 7.9 km/s. Escape velocity is the minimum speed to escape Earth's gravity: v_e = √(2GM/R) = √(2gR) ≈ 11.2 km/s. Note that v_e = √2 · v_o. Escape velocity is independent of the mass and angle of projection.

Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion:
1. Law of orbits: Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus.
2. Law of areas: The line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times (conservation of angular momentum).
3. Law of periods: T² ∝ r³ where T is the orbital period and r is the semi-major axis.

Gravitational Potential Energy: U = −GMm/r (zero at infinity). The negative sign indicates that gravity is attractive — work must be done against gravity to move a body away.

Elasticity: When a deforming force is applied, a body changes shape. Elastic limit is the maximum stress up to which the body regains its original shape completely. Stress = restoring force per unit area (N/m² or Pa). Strain = fractional change in dimension (dimensionless).
- Hooke's Law: Within elastic limit, stress ∝ strain. Young's modulus Y = longitudinal stress/longitudinal strain.
- Bulk modulus K = volumetric stress/volumetric strain (resistance to volume change).
- Shear modulus (modulus of rigidity) η = tangential stress/shear strain.
- Poisson's ratio = lateral strain/longitudinal strain.

Pressure in Fluids: Pressure at depth h in a liquid: P = P₀ + ρgh (P₀ = atmospheric pressure). Pascal's law: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions — basis of hydraulic press and brakes.
- Archimedes' principle: A body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid. This determines whether an object floats or sinks.
- Density ρ = mass/volume; relative density = ρ_body/ρ_water (dimensionless).
- Continuity equation: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ for incompressible fluids.
- Bernoulli's theorem: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline (conservation of energy for flowing fluid).

NDA/CDS Focus: Variation of g with height/depth is a favourite. Differentiate between mass and weight. Questions on Kepler's laws, orbital/escape velocities, and buoyancy are regular. Bernoulli's theorem applications (aerodynamic lift, venturi meter) may be asked qualitatively.

Heat and Thermodynamics

Temperature and Heat: Temperature measures the hotness or coldness of a body (kinetic energy of molecules). Heat is energy transferred due to temperature difference. Scales: Celsius (°C), Fahrenheit (°F), Kelvin (K). Conversion: (T_F − 32)/9 = T_C/5 = T_K − 273.15. Absolute zero = 0 K = −273.15°C.

Thermal Expansion: On heating, most substances expand. Linear expansion: ΔL = αL₀ΔT, where α is the coefficient of linear expansion. Area expansion: ΔA = βA₀ΔT where β = 2α. Volume expansion: ΔV = γV₀ΔT where γ = 3α. Water is anomalous — it contracts on heating from 0°C to 4°C and reaches maximum density at 4°C.

Heat Transfer: Three modes:
- Conduction: transfer through direct contact via molecular collision and electron movement. Good conductors: metals. Poor conductors: wood, air, wool.
- Convection: transfer by actual movement of heated fluid (hot fluid rises, cold fluid sinks). Causes sea breeze, land breeze, and wind patterns.
- Radiation: transfer via electromagnetic waves, no medium required. Dark, rough surfaces are good absorbers and emitters; shiny, light surfaces are poor absorbers/emitters.

Specific Heat and Latent Heat: Heat required to raise temperature: Q = mcΔT where m = mass, c = specific heat capacity. Water has a high specific heat (4200 J/kg·K), explaining coastal climate moderation. Latent heat is the heat absorbed/released during phase change at constant temperature: Q = mL where L is latent heat of fusion (solid↔liquid) or vaporization (liquid↔gas).

Laws of Thermodynamics:
- Zeroth Law: If two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. Defines temperature.
- First Law: ΔQ = ΔU + ΔW — heat supplied equals increase in internal energy plus work done by the system. Conservation of energy. For an adiabatic process (Q = 0): ΔU = −ΔW.
- Second Law: (Kelvin-Planck statement) No cyclic engine can convert all heat into work. (Clausius statement) Heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder to a hotter body. Introduces entropy.
- Third Law: Entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero is zero.

Ideal Gas Laws: PV = nRT (ideal gas equation). Boyle's law (T constant): PV = constant. Charles's law (P constant): V/T = constant. Gay-Lussac's law (V constant): P/T = constant. Root mean square speed: v_rms = √(3kT/m) = √(3RT/M). Average kinetic energy per molecule = ³/₂kT.

NDA/CDS Focus: Conversions between temperature scales, identification of heat transfer modes, statement of thermodynamic laws, and understanding that internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature. Conceptual questions on adiabatic vs isothermal processes are also asked.

Sound and Waves

Wave Basics: A wave transfers energy without transferring matter. Transverse waves: particles oscillate perpendicular to propagation direction (light, waves on strings). Longitudinal waves: particles oscillate parallel to propagation (sound in air). Sound cannot travel in vacuum because it needs a material medium.

Speed of Sound: v = √(γP/ρ) for gases (Laplace correction). Speed is maximum in solids, less in liquids, minimum in gases. Speed increases with temperature (v ∝ √T), decreases with humidity (moist air is less dense than dry air at same T and P). Wind affects the effective speed: v_effective = v_sound ± v_wind (depending on direction).

Sound Wave Characteristics: Frequency (f), wavelength (λ), amplitude, time period (T = 1/f). Relationship: v = fλ. Frequency determines pitch; amplitude determines loudness. Intensity is proportional to amplitude² and frequency². Decibel (dB) is the logarithmic unit for sound intensity level: β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) where I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² is the threshold of hearing.

Echo and Reverberation: Echo is the reflection of sound heard after a time delay. Minimum distance for echo perception is about 17 m (since human ear retains sound for ~0.1 s). Reverberation is multiple reflections causing sound to persist.

Doppler Effect: Apparent change in frequency when source and observer are in relative motion. When approaching each other, apparent frequency increases; when receding, it decreases. f' = f(v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s) where v_o is observer speed and v_s is source speed (signs depend on direction). Used in radar, medical ultrasound, and astronomy (redshift/blueshift).

Beats: When two sound waves of slightly different frequencies superpose, the intensity varies periodically. Beat frequency = |f₁ − f₂|. Used in tuning musical instruments.

Standing Waves and Musical Instruments: In a closed pipe (one end closed), only odd harmonics are present. Fundamental frequency: f = v/4L. In an open pipe (both ends open), all harmonics are present. Fundamental: f = v/2L. Stretched string fundamentals: f = v/2L where v = √(T/μ) and μ is mass per unit length.

NDA/CDS Focus: Doppler effect direction (approach → higher pitch), conditions for echo, beat frequency calculation, and comparison of sound speed in different media are very frequently asked. Distinguish between longitudinal and transverse waves.

Light and Optics

Reflection of Light: The bouncing back of light from a surface. Laws of reflection: (i) angle of incidence = angle of reflection. (ii) Incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane. Plane mirrors produce virtual, erect, laterally inverted images of the same size. Number of images between two inclined mirrors at angle θ: n = 360/θ − 1 (when 360/θ is even) or n = 360/θ (when odd), assuming symmetric placement.

Refraction of Light: Bending of light when it passes from one medium to another. Laws of refraction (Snell's Law): n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r. Refractive index n = c/v = sin i/sin r (for air to medium). When light goes from rarer to denser medium, it bends towards the normal; denser to rarer, away from the normal. Apparent depth = real depth/n.

Total Internal Reflection (TIR): When light travels from denser to rarer medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, all light is reflected back into the denser medium. Critical angle: sin C = n₂/n₁ (n₁ > n₂). Applications: optical fibres, mirages, sparkling of diamonds, vision through a diver's mask.

Prism and Dispersion: A prism splits white light into its constituent colours (VIBGYOR) due to dispersion — different wavelengths refract by different amounts (violet bends most, red least). The angle of deviation δ = i + e − A where A is the prism angle. Minimum deviation occurs when the ray passes symmetrically through the prism.

Lens Theory: Convex (converging) lens: thicker at centre. Forms real, inverted images when object is outside focal length; virtual, erect, magnified image when object is inside focal length (used as simple magnifier). Concave (diverging) lens: always forms virtual, erect, diminished images. Lens maker's formula: 1/f = (n−1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂). Thin lens equation: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f (using sign convention). Power of a lens P = 1/f (in metres), measured in dioptres (D).

Human Eye and Defects: The eye focuses light on the retina using the crystalline lens. Near point (distinct vision): 25 cm for a normal adult. Far point: infinity. Accommodation is the ability to change focal length. Defects: myopia (near-sightedness, corrected by concave lens); hypermetropia (far-sightedness, corrected by convex lens); presbyopia (age-related, corrected by bifocal lens); cataract (clouding of lens, treated surgically).

Optical Instruments: Simple microscope uses a single convex lens (magnification = 1 + D/f for near-point adjustment). Compound microscope uses two convex lenses — objective (short focal length) and eyepiece (longer focal length). Telescope (refracting) uses an objective of long focal length and large aperture, and an eyepiece of short focal length. Angular magnification M = f_o/f_e for normal adjustment.

NDA/CDS Focus: Critical angle and TIR applications, lens/mirror formula numericals, defects of vision and their corrections, and the relationship between refractive index and speed of light are extremely high-yield topics. Rainbow formation (internal reflection + dispersion in water droplets) is also frequently asked.

Electricity and Magnetism

Electric Charge and Coulomb's Law: Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge is quantised (q = ne where e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) and conserved. Coulomb's law: F = kq₁q₂/r² where k = 1/(4πε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². In a dielectric medium, the force is reduced by the dielectric constant K: F_medium = F_air/K.

Electric Field and Potential: Electric field E = F/q = kQ/r² (point charge). Electric field lines originate from positive charges and terminate on negative charges; they never cross. Electric potential V = kQ/r (scalar). Potential difference (voltage) V = W/q. Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to electric field lines. A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium has constant potential throughout its volume.

Capacitance: A capacitor stores charge. Capacitance C = Q/V. For a parallel plate capacitor: C = ε₀A/d (in vacuum). With a dielectric: C = Kε₀A/d. Capacitors in parallel: C_total = C₁ + C₂ + ... Capacitors in series: 1/C_total = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + ...

Current, Resistance and Ohm's Law: Electric current I = Q/t = nAv_d e where v_d is drift velocity. Current density J = I/A. Ohm's law: V = IR (valid for ohmic conductors at constant temperature). Resistance R = ρL/A where ρ is resistivity. Resistivity depends on material and temperature (increases with temperature for metals). Series resistance: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ... Parallel resistance: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...

Electrical Power and Energy: Power P = VI = I²R = V²/R. Energy consumed E = Pt = Vit. 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. Heating effect of current (Joule heating): H = I²Rt — used in electric heaters, fuses, and incandescent lamps.

Magnetic Effects of Current (Electromagnetism): Oersted discovered that a current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field. Right-hand thumb rule gives field direction around a straight conductor. Biot-Savart law and Ampere's circuital law give quantitative field values. A solenoid produces a uniform field inside; a toroid confines the field entirely within. Force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field: F = BIl sinθ (motor effect). Moving charge in a magnetic field: F = qvB sinθ.

Electromagnetic Induction: Faraday's laws: (1) An emf is induced in a circuit when the magnetic flux through it changes. (2) Induced emf = −dΦ/dt. Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change that produced it (conservation of energy). Self-inductance L relates flux linkage to current: Φ = LI. Mutual inductance between two coils allows transformer operation.

AC and DC Circuits: Direct current (DC) flows in one direction. Alternating current (AC) reverses direction periodically. For AC: V = V₀ sin ωt, I = I₀ sin(ωt ± φ). RMS values: V_rms = V₀/√2, I_rms = I₀/√2. In India, AC frequency is 50 Hz. Power factor = cosφ. Transformers step up/down voltage using mutual induction — they work only with AC, not DC.

NDA/CDS Focus: Coulomb's law and electric field direction, Ohm's law and series-parallel combinations, magnetic field direction rules, electromagnetic induction principles, and transformer working are very high yield. Simple numericals on resistance, power, and capacitance are expected.

Modern Physics and Nuclear Physics

Atomic Structure: Thomson's plum pudding model was replaced by Rutherford's nuclear model (α-particle scattering experiment showed a small, dense, positively charged nucleus). Bohr's model explained hydrogen spectrum: electrons orbit in discrete energy levels without radiating; radiation is emitted/absorbed only when jumping between levels. Energy of nth level: E_n = −13.6/n² eV. Later, the quantum mechanical model (Schrodinger) replaced orbits with orbitals (probability regions).

Fundamental Particles: Proton (positive, found in nucleus), neutron (neutral, found in nucleus), electron (negative, orbits nucleus). Other particles: positron (anti-electron), neutrino (nearly massless, weakly interacting), photon (massless quantum of EM radiation). Nucleons = protons + neutrons.

Isotopes, Isobars, Isotones: Isotopes have same Z (protons) but different A (neutrons) — same element, different mass (e.g., ¹H, ²H, ³H). Isobars have same A but different Z. Isotones have same number of neutrons.

Radioactivity: Discovered by Becquerel; studied by the Curies. Three types of radiation:
- Alpha (α) particles: He nuclei (²He⁴), +2 charge, low penetration (stopped by paper), high ionizing power.
- Beta (β) particles: high-speed electrons (β⁻) or positrons (β⁺), moderate penetration (stopped by aluminium), moderate ionizing power.
- Gamma (γ) rays: high-energy photons, no charge, high penetration (requires thick lead/concrete), low ionizing power.
Half-life (T₁/₂): time for half the radioactive nuclei to decay. N = N₀(½)^(t/T₁/₂). Decay constant λ = ln2/T₁/₂. Activity = λN, measured in becquerels (Bq) or curies (Ci).

Nuclear Reactions: Fission: heavy nucleus splits into lighter nuclei releasing energy (used in nuclear reactors and atomic bombs). Example: ²³⁵U + n → Ba + Kr + 3n + energy. Fusion: light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus releasing enormous energy (powers the Sun). Example: ¹H + ¹H → ²He + energy. E = mc² (mass-energy equivalence).

Laser: Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Properties: monochromatic, coherent, highly directional, intense. Applications: surgery, communications, barcode scanners, lithography, military targeting.

Semiconductors: Materials with conductivity between conductors and insulators. Intrinsic: pure silicon/germanium. Extrinsic: doped with impurities — n-type (donor impurity adds electrons), p-type (acceptor impurity adds holes). p-n junction diode: conducts in forward bias, blocks in reverse bias. Used in rectifiers, LEDs, solar cells.

Transistors: Three-layer semiconductor device (npn or pnp). Acts as amplifier or switch. Essential building block of modern electronics. Common configurations: common emitter (voltage and current amplification).

NDA/CDS Focus: Comparison of α, β, γ rays (charge, penetration, ionization), half-life calculations, nuclear fission vs fusion, E=mc² applications, laser properties, and basic semiconductor/p-n junction concepts are very important. Bohr's model limitations and key experiments (Rutherford, photoelectric effect) may also appear.

Important facts

  • SI base units: metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), ampere (A), kelvin (K), mole (mol), candela (cd). Seven in total.
  • Newton's third law pairs act on different bodies and never cancel each other.
  • Escape velocity v_e = √(2gR) ≈ 11.2 km/s for Earth; orbital velocity v_o = √(gR) ≈ 7.9 km/s; v_e = √2 × v_o.
  • g decreases with height and depth but is maximum at poles and minimum at equator.
  • At Earth's centre, g = 0; at Earth's centre of mass but far from any surface, gravitational force is zero by symmetry.
  • Total internal reflection occurs only when light travels from denser to rarer medium at angle greater than critical angle.
  • Critical angle for water-air interface is approximately 48.6°.
  • Convex lens converges light; concave lens diverges light. Convex mirror diverges; concave mirror converges.
  • Myopia (near-sightedness) → corrected by concave lens. Hypermetropia (far-sightedness) → corrected by convex lens.
  • Transformer works only on AC; it cannot step up or step down DC voltage.
  • In a series circuit, current is the same through all components; in parallel, voltage is the same across all branches.
  • Power in AC circuit: P = VI cosφ where cosφ is the power factor.
  • Fusion powers the Sun; fission powers nuclear reactors.
  • Alpha decay reduces atomic number by 2 and mass number by 4. Beta-minus decay increases atomic number by 1.
  • Half-life is independent of temperature, pressure, and chemical state — it is a nuclear property.
  • The photoelectric effect demonstrated the particle nature of light; Compton effect further confirmed it.
  • Sound cannot travel in vacuum; light can.
  • Speed of sound in air at room temperature ≈ 343 m/s. Speed of light in vacuum = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.
  • KE = p²/2m: for equal momenta, the lighter body has greater kinetic energy.
  • Impulse = change in momentum; unit is N·s or kg·m/s.
  • Bernoulli's theorem is based on conservation of energy for flowing fluids.
  • The first law of thermodynamics is essentially conservation of energy applied to thermal systems.
  • Nuclear reactor uses controlled fission; nuclear bomb uses uncontrolled fission (atomic bomb) or fusion (hydrogen bomb).
  • LASER light is coherent, monochromatic, and highly directional — unlike ordinary light.

Common mistakes

  • Applying equations of motion when acceleration is not constant — they are ONLY valid for uniform acceleration.
  • Confusing mass and weight: mass is constant everywhere; weight (mg) varies with location (different on Moon vs Earth).
  • Assuming action-reaction forces cancel — they act on DIFFERENT bodies and cannot cancel each other.
  • Thinking centrifugal force is a real force — it is a pseudo-force experienced only in a rotating reference frame.
  • Assuming potential energy is always positive — gravitational PE is defined as negative (zero at infinity).
  • Forgetting that escape velocity is independent of the mass of the projecting body.
  • Using n = 360/θ − 1 for asymmetric mirror placement — that formula applies only when the mirrors are symmetrically placed about the object.
  • Confusing refraction and reflection: refraction involves bending due to medium change; reflection involves bouncing back from a surface.
  • Assuming a convex mirror can form real images — it always forms virtual, erect, diminished images regardless of object position.
  • Believing transformers work on DC — they require changing magnetic flux, which only AC provides.
  • Thinking sound travels faster in denser media generally — speed depends on both elasticity and density; in solids it is fastest despite higher density because elasticity dominates.
  • Assuming half-life means all material decays in two half-lives — after two half-lives, ¼ of the original sample remains, not zero.
  • Confusing fission and fusion: fission = splitting heavy nuclei (uranium); fusion = combining light nuclei (hydrogen to helium).
  • Forgetting that the power factor cosφ equals 1 only in purely resistive AC circuits; it is less than 1 in inductive or capacitive circuits.
  • Applying Coulomb's law in a medium without dividing by the dielectric constant K — the force is reduced in a dielectric.
