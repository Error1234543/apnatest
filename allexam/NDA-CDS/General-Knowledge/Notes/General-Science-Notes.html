1. Physics — Mechanics, Motion, and Gravitation

Mechanics is the foundation of NDA/CDS physics and carries consistent weightage. Every concept must be understood in terms of its formula, conditions, units, and typical application.

Laws of Motion
Newton's First Law defines inertia as the natural tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion. The greater the mass, the greater the inertia. Newton's Second Law gives F = ma, where force is measured in newtons (1 N = 1 kg·m/s²). This law also implies that force equals the rate of change of momentum: F = dp/dt. For constant mass, this reduces to F = ma. Impulse is defined as the product of force and time (J = Ft) and equals the change in momentum (Δp). Newton's Third Law states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction; these forces act on different bodies and therefore do not cancel each other out. A common error is confusing action-reaction pairs with balanced forces acting on the same body.

Friction
Friction opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact. Static friction adjusts itself to match the applied force up to its limiting value (fs,max = μsN). Kinetic friction (fk = μkN) is generally less than limiting static friction. The coefficient of friction (μ) is a dimensionless quantity. Friction is essential for walking, vehicle motion, and braking. The angle of friction (λ) is related to μ by tan λ = μ.

Work, Energy, and Power
Work done by a constant force is W = F·d·cos θ, where θ is the angle between force and displacement. Work is a scalar quantity measured in joules (J). The work-energy theorem states that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy: Wnet = ΔKE = ½mv² - ½mu². Potential energy is stored energy due to position; gravitational PE = mgh near Earth's surface. Mechanical energy is conserved when only conservative forces act. Power is the rate of doing work: P = W/t = F·v. The unit is watt (W = J/s).

Circular Motion
In uniform circular motion, speed is constant but velocity changes direction continuously, producing centripetal acceleration ac = v²/r = ω²r directed toward the centre. The required centripetal force is Fc = mv²/r = mω²r. For a vehicle on a curved road banked at angle θ without friction: tan θ = v²/(rg). For a car on a flat road: v_max = √(μrg). These formulas are frequently tested numerically.

Gravitation
Newton's Law of Gravitation: F = Gm₁m₂/r², where G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². The acceleration due to gravity at Earth's surface is g = GM/R² ≈ 9.8 m/s². At height h above the surface: g' = g/(1 + h/R)². At depth d below the surface: g' = g(1 - d/R), assuming uniform density. Escape velocity from Earth's surface is ve = √(2gR) ≈ 11.2 km/s. Orbital velocity for a satellite near Earth's surface is vo = √(gR) ≈ 7.9 km/s. The time period of a satellite in circular orbit is T = 2π√(r³/GM). Geostationary satellites orbit at approximately 36,000 km altitude with a period of 24 hours. Kepler's Laws govern planetary motion: (I) orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus, (II) equal areas are swept in equal times, (III) T² ∝ a³ where a is the semi-major axis.

2. Physics — Heat, Thermodynamics, Sound, and Light

Heat and Temperature
Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of molecules. The three scales relate as: (T_F - 32)/9 = (T_C - 0)/5 = (T_K - 273)/0. Absolute zero is 0 K = -273.15°C = -459.67°F. Heat (Q) is energy in transit due to temperature difference. Specific heat capacity (c) is the heat required to raise 1 kg of substance by 1°C: Q = mcΔT. Latent heat accounts for phase change without temperature change: Q = mL, where Lfusion for ice = 3.36 × 10⁵ J/kg and Lvaporization for water = 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg. Thermal expansion: linear α, area β = 2α, volume γ = 3α. For water, anomalous expansion occurs between 0°C and 4°C — it contracts on heating below 4°C and reaches maximum density at 4°C.

Laws of Thermodynamics
The First Law is conservation of energy for thermal systems: ΔQ = ΔU + ΔW, where ΔU is change in internal energy and ΔW is work done by the system. For an isothermal process (constant T): ΔU = 0, so ΔQ = ΔW. For an adiabatic process (no heat exchange): ΔQ = 0, so ΔU = -ΔW, and PV^γ = constant where γ = Cp/Cv. For an isochoric process (constant V): ΔW = 0, so ΔQ = ΔU. For an isobaric process (constant P): ΔW = PΔV. The Second Law states that heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body (Clausius statement) and no heat engine can have 100% efficiency (Kelvin-Planck statement). Entropy of an isolated system always increases.

Sound Waves
Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave requiring a medium. Speed of sound in air at STP is approximately 332 m/s. It increases with temperature (~0.6 m/s per °C), decreases with humidity inversely, and is greater in solids than liquids than gases. The relationship is v = fλ. For a stretched string: v = √(T/μ), where T is tension and μ is mass per unit length. The fundamental frequency of a pipe open at both ends is f = v/(2L); for a pipe closed at one end: f = v/(4L). Beats occur when two sounds of slightly different frequencies interfere; beat frequency = |f₁ - f₂|. The Doppler Effect changes apparent frequency when source or observer moves relative to each other.

Optics — Reflection and Refraction
Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, with the sign convention: distances measured against incident light are negative. Magnification m = -v/u for mirrors and m = v/u for lenses. For a convex mirror, f is positive; for a concave mirror, f is negative (using the standard Cartesian sign convention). Lens maker's formula: 1/f = (μ - 1)(1/R₁ - 1/R₂). Total internal reflection occurs when light travels from denser to rarer medium at an angle greater than the critical angle θc, where sin θc = 1/μ. This principle is used in optical fibres, mirages, and diamond brilliance. Prisms deviate light; the angle of deviation depends on the prism angle and refractive index. For small-angle prisms: δ = (μ - 1)A.

3. Physics — Electricity, Magnetism, and Modern Physics

Electricity
Ohm's Law: V = IR, valid for ohmic conductors at constant temperature. Resistance R = ρl/A, where ρ is resistivity (Ω·m), l is length, and A is cross-sectional area. Resistivity increases with temperature for metals but decreases for semiconductors. Series combination: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + ... Parallel combination: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... Electric power: P = VI = I²R = V²/R, measured in watts. Electrical energy: E = Pt = VIt, commonly expressed in kWh (1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J). Capacitance C = Q/V; for parallel plates: C = ε₀A/d. Capacitors in series: 1/C_total = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + ... Capacitors in parallel: C_total = C₁ + C₂ + ...

Magnetism and Electromagnetic Induction
A moving charge produces a magnetic field. Force on a charge q moving with velocity v in magnetic field B: F = qvB sin θ (maximum when perpendicular). Force on a current-carrying conductor: F = BIl sin θ. Fleming's Left-Hand Rule determines the direction of force. Electromagnetic induction: Faraday's Law states that induced EMF equals the rate of change of magnetic flux (ε = -dΦ/dt). Lenz's Law gives the direction — the induced current opposes the change causing it. Self-inductance L = NΦ/I, measured in henry (H). Mutual inductance links two coils. AC generator converts mechanical energy to electrical energy using electromagnetic induction. Transformer equation: V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p = I_p/I_s. Step-up transformers increase voltage; step-down decrease it.

Modern Physics
Photoelectric effect: Einstein's equation KE_max = hν - φ, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant, ν is frequency, and φ is the work function. This confirmed the particle nature of light. de Broglie wavelength: λ = h/p = h/mv, establishing wave-particle duality for matter. Atomic models: Thomson's plum pudding model was replaced by Rutherford's nuclear model (α-scattering experiment), which had stability problems resolved by Bohr's model. Bohr's postulates: electrons orbit in discrete energy levels without radiating; radiation is emitted/absorbed during transitions: ΔE = hν. Nuclear physics: binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56, making it the most stable nucleus. Nuclear fission splits heavy nuclei (e.g., ²³⁵U) releasing energy; nuclear fusion combines light nuclei (e.g., hydrogen in stars) releasing far more energy per unit mass. Radioactivity: α-decay reduces atomic number by 2 and mass by 4; β-decay increases atomic number by 1; γ-decay changes neither. Half-life t½ = 0.693/λ, where λ is the decay constant.

Semiconductors
Intrinsic semiconductors (Si, Ge) have a small band gap (~1 eV). Doping creates n-type (donor impurity like phosphorus adds electrons) and p-type (acceptor impurity like boron adds holes). A p-n junction diode conducts in forward bias and blocks in reverse bias. Transistors (npn or pnp) amplify signals and act as switches. Logic gates: AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR are the building blocks of digital electronics.

4. Chemistry — Atomic Structure, Bonding, and Periodic Table

Atomic Structure
Atoms consist of protons (+ve charge), neutrons (neutral), and electrons (-ve charge). Atomic number Z = number of protons = number of electrons in a neutral atom. Mass number A = protons + neutrons. Isotopes have same Z but different A; isobars have same A but different Z; isotones have same number of neutrons. Electron configuration follows the Aufbau principle (lowest energy first), Pauli exclusion principle (max 2 electrons per orbital with opposite spins), and Hund's rule (fill degenerate orbitals singly before pairing). Quantum numbers describe electron state: principal (n = shell), azimuthal (l = subshell, 0 to n-1), magnetic (m_l = orbital orientation, -l to +l), spin (m_s = ±½). The s-subshell holds 2 electrons, p holds 6, d holds 10, f holds 14.

Chemical Bonding
Ionic (electrovalent) bonds form by complete transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal, producing oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic force (e.g., NaCl, MgO). Covalent bonds form by sharing electron pairs; they can be single, double, or triple. Polar covalent bonds have unequal sharing due to electronegativity difference. Coordinate (dative) bonds involve one atom supplying both shared electrons. Metallic bonding involves a sea of delocalized electrons among positive metal ions. Van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding are intermolecular forces; hydrogen bonds significantly raise boiling points of water, NH₃, and HF. VSEPR theory predicts molecular geometry based on electron pair repulsion around the central atom.

Periodic Table and Periodic Trends
Modern periodic law: properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. The table has 7 periods and 18 groups. Elements in the same group have similar valence electron configurations and chemical properties. Key trends across a period (left to right): atomic radius decreases, ionization energy increases, electronegativity increases, metallic character decreases. Key trends down a group: atomic radius increases, ionization energy decreases, electronegativity decreases, metallic character increases. The noble gases (Group 18) have full valence shells and are chemically inert. Alkali metals (Group 1) are highly reactive metals; alkaline earth metals (Group 2) are less reactive. Halogens (Group 17) are reactive non-metals; chalcogens (Group 16) include oxygen and sulfur.

States of Matter
Gases obey ideal gas law PV = nRT at low pressure and high temperature. Real gases deviate; van der Waals equation corrects for molecular size and intermolecular forces. Liquids exhibit surface tension (contracting force at surface), viscosity (internal friction), and capillarity. Solids are classified as crystalline (regular lattice, definite melting point) or amorphous (irregular, softening over a range). Crystalline types include ionic, molecular, covalent network, and metallic solids.

5. Chemistry — Acids, Bases, Salts, and Reactions

Acids, Bases, and Salts
Arrhenius definition: acids produce H⁺ ions in water; bases produce OH⁻ ions. Brønsted-Lowry definition: acids are proton donors; bases are proton acceptors. pH = -log[H⁺]; pH < 7 is acidic, pH = 7 neutral, pH > 7 basic. The pH scale ranges from 0 to 14. Strong acids (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄) dissociate completely; weak acids (CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃) dissociate partially. Strong bases (NaOH, KOH) dissociate completely; weak bases (NH₄OH) dissociate partially. Salts are formed by neutralization: acid + base → salt + water. Salt hydrolysis can make solutions acidic, basic, or neutral depending on the parent acid and base. Common pH values: stomach acid ~1-2, lemon juice ~2, rainwater ~5.6, pure water ~7, blood ~7.4, seawater ~8, soap ~10, bleach ~12.

Important Chemical Reactions and Equations
Displacement reactions follow reactivity series: more reactive metal displaces less reactive metal from solution (e.g., Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu). Combustion reactions release heat and light (e.g., CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O). Redox reactions involve simultaneous oxidation and reduction; oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain. Balancing redox equations uses the half-reaction method. Chemical equilibrium occurs when forward and reverse reaction rates are equal; Le Chatelier's principle predicts how equilibrium shifts under stress (concentration, pressure, temperature changes).

Oxidation States and Naming
Oxidation states help track electron transfer. Rules: element in free state = 0; monatomic ion = its charge; O is usually -2 (except peroxides where it is -1); H is +1 with non-metals, -1 with metals; sum of oxidation states equals overall charge. Common naming: -ide for binary compounds (NaCl = sodium chloride), -ate/-ite for oxyanions with more/fewer oxygens (SO₄²⁻ sulphate, SO₃²⁻ sulphite), prefixes di-, tri-, tetra- for covalent compounds (CO = carbon monoxide, CO₂ = carbon dioxide).

Important Compounds and Uses
NaCl (common salt): raw material for chlorine, NaOH, baking soda. NaHCO₃ (baking soda): antacid, fire extinguisher, baking agent. Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda): water softener, glass manufacturing. CaO (quick lime): cement, steel industry. Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime): whitewash, neutralizing acidity. Bleaching powder Ca(OCl)Cl: disinfectant, bleaching agent. NaOH (caustic soda): soap, paper, petroleum refining. H₂SO₄ (sulphuric acid): fertilizers, batteries, paints — called the 'king of chemicals'. HNO₃ (nitric acid): fertilizers, explosives. NH₃ (ammonia): refrigerant, fertilizers, nitric acid manufacture (Ostwald process).

6. Chemistry — Organic Chemistry and Everyday Chemistry

Basic Organic Chemistry Concepts
Organic chemistry centres on carbon compounds due to carbon's unique property of catenation (forming long chains and rings). Hydrocarbons are classified as saturated (alkanes, CnH2n+2, single bonds) and unsaturated (alkenes CnH2n with double bonds, alkynes CnH2n-2 with triple bonds). IUPAC nomenclature: identify the longest carbon chain (parent), number to give substituents the lowest locants, name substituents alphabetically (methyl, ethyl, chloro, bromo), and add suffixes (-ane, -ene, -yne) for the principal functional group. Functional groups determine chemical properties: -OH (alcohols), -CHO (aldehydes), -COOH (carboxylic acids), -CO- (ketones), -NH₂ (amines), -X (halides), -COO- (esters).

Important Organic Reactions and Compounds
Combustion: hydrocarbons burn in oxygen to give CO₂ and H₂O. Addition reactions: H₂ added across double/triple bonds using Ni/Pt catalyst (hydrogenation of oils to fats — vanaspati ghee). Substitution reactions: H atoms replaced by halogens (chlorination of methane). Polymerization: small molecules (monomers) join to form large chains (polymers). Ethanol (C₂H₅OH): solvent, fuel, beverage; produced by fermentation. Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH): vinegar, ester formation. Esters have fruity smells; formed by reaction of alcohol with carboxylic acid in presence of conc. H₂SO₄. Soaps are sodium/potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids; they form micelles to trap grease. Detergents are synthetic and work in hard water. Petroleum refining separates fractions by boiling point: petroleum gas (<40°C), petrol (40-110°C), kerosene (110-150°C), diesel (150-250°C), lubricating oil (>250°C). Bitumen is used for road surfacing. Plastics: thermoplastics (soften on heating — PVC, polythene) vs thermosetting (harden permanently — bakelite, melamine). Natural polymers include cellulose, proteins, rubber.

7. General Science — Human Physiology and Biology

Cell — Structural Unit of Life
The cell is the smallest unit of life. Prokaryotic cells (bacteria, blue-green algae) lack a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles. Eukaryotic cells (plant and animal) have a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Plant cells have a cell wall (cellulose), large central vacuole, and chloroplasts; animal cells lack these. Key organelles: nucleus (contains DNA, controls cell activities), mitochondria (powerhouse — ATP production via respiration), ribosomes (protein synthesis), Endoplasmic reticulum (transport — rough ER has ribosomes, smooth ER lacks them), Golgi apparatus (packaging and secretion), lysosomes (digestive waste disposal — 'suicide bags'), chloroplasts (photosynthesis in plants), vacuoles (storage). Cell membrane is selectively permeable. Transport mechanisms: diffusion (movement down concentration gradient), osmosis (diffusion of water across a semipermeable membrane), active transport (requires energy, against gradient).

Human Body Systems
Digestive system: starts in mouth (salivary amylase begins starch digestion), food passes through esophagus to stomach (HCl and pepsin digest proteins), small intestine is the major site of digestion and absorption (bile from liver emulsifies fats, pancreatic juice contains trypsin, lipase, amylase), large intestine absorbs water. Liver produces bile and processes nutrients; pancreas produces digestive enzymes and hormones (insulin, glucagon). Respiratory system: air enters through nostrils → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Gas exchange occurs in alveoli by diffusion; O₂ binds to haemoglobin in RBCs, CO₂ is transported as bicarbonate. Circulatory system: heart has 4 chambers (right/left atria and ventricles). Double circulation: pulmonary circuit (heart-lungs-heart) and systemic circuit (heart-body-heart). Blood components: RBCs (carry O₂ via haemoglobin), WBCs (immune defense), platelets (clotting), plasma (liquid medium). Arteries carry blood away from heart (usually oxygenated); veins carry blood toward heart (usually deoxygenated); capillaries connect them.

Excretory and Nervous Systems
Kidneys filter blood to remove urea, excess salts, and water as urine. Each kidney has ~1 million nephrons — the functional units. The nervous system comprises the CNS (brain and spinal cord) and PNS (peripheral nerves). Neurons transmit electrical impulses: dendrite receives signal → cell body → axon transmits → synapse passes to next neuron. Brain regions: cerebrum (thinking, memory, sensation), cerebellum (balance and coordination), medulla oblongata (involuntary functions like heartbeat and breathing). Endocrine system: glands secrete hormones directly into blood. Pituitary (master gland), thyroid (thyroxine — metabolic rate), adrenal (adrenaline — fight or flight), pancreas (insulin/glucagon — blood sugar), gonads (sex hormones). Vitamins: A (night blindness deficiency), B-complex (beriberi, etc.), C (scurvy), D (rickets), K (blood clotting). Minerals: calcium (bones/teeth), iron (haemoglobin), iodine (thyroxine synthesis).

8. General Science — Environment, Ecology, and Applied Science

Environmental Science Fundamentals
An ecosystem comprises biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) components interacting in a defined area. Food chains show energy flow: producers (plants) → primary consumers (herbivores) → secondary consumers (carnivores) → decomposers (bacteria/fungi). Energy decreases at each trophic level (~10% transferred — Lindeman's law). Biomagnification is the increasing concentration of toxins (e.g., DDT, mercury) at higher trophic levels. Biodiversity refers to the variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels. Hotspot regions have exceptional endemism under threat. Major biomes: tropical rainforest, grassland, desert, tundra, temperate forest.

Pollution and Its Control
Air pollution: primary pollutants (SO₂, NOx, CO, particulate matter, VOCs) and secondary pollutants (ozone, acid rain). Photochemical smog forms from NOx + VOCs + sunlight. Acid rain (H₂SO₄, HNO₃) damages buildings, soils, and aquatic life. Greenhouse effect: CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, CFCs trap infrared radiation, causing global warming. Ozone depletion: CFCs release chlorine atoms that catalytically destroy stratospheric ozone (Antarctic ozone hole). Water pollution: industrial effluents, agricultural runoff (pesticides, fertilizers), sewage. Eutrophication causes algal blooms that deplete dissolved oxygen. Noise pollution: sustained exposure >80 dB causes hearing damage; measured in decibels. Soil pollution: pesticides, heavy metals, plastic waste degrade land quality.

Applied Science and Defence Relevance
Radar (Radio Detection and Ranging) uses reflected radio waves to detect objects; principle of pulse-echo timing. Sonar uses sound waves underwater. Nuclear energy: fission reactors use controlled chain reactions (U-235); hydrogen bombs use uncontrolled fusion. Lasers produce coherent, monochromatic light; applications include surgery, communication, targeting. Fibre optics transmit light by total internal reflection; used in endoscopy and telecommunications. Rockets work on Newton's third law; liquid propellants (liquid hydrogen + liquid oxygen) and solid propellants are used. Stealth technology reduces radar cross-section through shape and materials. Radiation detection uses Geiger-Müller counters. Biotechnology applications: recombinant DNA, genetic engineering, cloned organisms, vaccines. Greenhouse gases ranking by impact: CO₂ (most abundant anthropogenic), CH₄ (more potent per molecule), N₂O, CFCs (also deplete ozone).
