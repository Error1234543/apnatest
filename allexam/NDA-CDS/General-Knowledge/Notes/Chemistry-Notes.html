Introduction to Chemistry and Fundamental Concepts

Chemistry is the branch of science that deals with the composition, structure, properties, and transformation of matter. In the NDA & CDS GK paper, chemistry questions test your understanding of basic definitions, laws, and concepts rather than deep derivations.

1. Matter: Anything that has mass and occupies space. It exists in three fundamental states — solid, liquid, and gas — and a fourth state called plasma (ionized gas at very high temperatures). A fifth state, Bose-Einstein condensate, is formed near absolute zero.

2. Classification of Matter:
- Pure substances: Elements and Compounds.
- Mixtures: Homogeneous (uniform composition, e.g., salt solution) and Heterogeneous (non-uniform composition, e.g., sand and water).

3. Key Laws of Chemical Combination (very important for NDA):
- Law of Conservation of Mass (Lavoisier): Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. Total mass of reactants equals total mass of products.
- Law of Definite Proportions (Proust): A chemical compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by weight.
- Law of Multiple Proportions (Dalton): If two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers.
- Law of Gaseous Volumes (Gay-Lussac): When gases react, they do so in simple volume ratios if measured at the same temperature and pressure.
- Avogadro's Law: Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. One mole of any gas at STP occupies 22.4 litres.

4. Atoms and Molecules:
- Atom: The smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction but cannot exist independently (except noble gases).
- Molecule: The smallest particle of a substance (element or compound) that can exist independently and exhibits all the properties of that substance.
- Molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule; empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio.
- Molecular mass = sum of atomic masses of all atoms in the molecule. Molar mass is the mass of one mole expressed in grams.

Atomic Structure

Understanding the internal structure of the atom is essential. NDA/CDS frequently asks about subatomic particles and historical models.

1. Subatomic Particles:
- Electron: Discovered by J.J. Thomson (1897). Charge = -1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, mass ≈ 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg (considered negligible). Symbol: e⁻.
- Proton: Discovered by E. Goldstein (1886). Charge = +1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, mass ≈ 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Found in the nucleus. Symbol: p⁺.
- Neutron: Discovered by James Chadwick (1932). No charge, mass ≈ 1.675 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (slightly heavier than proton). Found in the nucleus.

2. Important Models of the Atom:
- Thomson's Model (Plum Pudding Model): Proposed electrons embedded in a positively charged sphere like raisins in a pudding. Successfully explained electrical neutrality but could not explain scattering experiments.
- Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911): Based on the alpha-particle scattering experiment. Concluded that most of the mass and positive charge is concentrated in a tiny central nucleus, and electrons revolve around it. Could not explain why revolving electrons do not lose energy and spiral into the nucleus.
- Bohr's Model (1913): Introduced quantised energy levels. Electrons revolve in discrete orbits without radiating energy. Energy is emitted or absorbed only when an electron jumps between orbits. Successfully explained the spectrum of hydrogen. Limitation: could not explain spectra of multi-electron atoms.

3. Quantum Mechanical Model (Modern Model):
- Electrons do not move in definite orbits but in orbitals (regions of space where probability of finding an electron is maximum).
- Four quantum numbers: n (principal → shell/energy level), l (azimuthal → subshell/s=0, p=1, d=2, f=3), ml (magnetic → orientation), ms (spin → +½ or -½).

4. Electronic Configuration Rules:
- Aufbau Principle: Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p...).
- Pauli's Exclusion Principle: No two electrons can have the same set of four quantum numbers; an orbital holds a maximum of 2 electrons with opposite spins.
- Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: Pairing of electrons in p, d, and f orbitals does not occur until each orbital of the same subshell contains one electron.

5. Isotopes, Isobars, and Isotones:
- Isotopes: Same atomic number, different mass number (same protons, different neutrons). Example: ¹H¹, ¹H², ¹H³ (protium, deuterium, tritium). Chemical properties are identical; physical properties differ.
- Isobars: Different atomic numbers but same mass number. Example: ¹⁸Ar⁴⁰ and ²⁰Ca⁴⁰.
- Isotones: Same number of neutrons but different protons and mass numbers.

The Periodic Table and Periodicity

The periodic table organises all known elements based on their atomic number and recurring chemical properties. UPSC-defence exams love factual periodic-table questions.

1. Evolution of the Periodic Table:
- Dobereiner's Triads (1829): Groups of three elements with similar properties where the middle element's atomic mass was approximately the average of the other two. Only a few triads could be identified.
- Newlands' Law of Octaves (1866): Every eighth element had properties similar to the first, like musical octaves. Worked well only up to calcium; failed for heavier elements.
- Mendeleev's Periodic Table (1869): Arranged elements by increasing atomic mass with groups having similar properties. Predicted existence of undiscovered elements (Eka-boron → Scandium, Eka-aluminium → Gallium, Eka-silicon → Germanium). Left gaps and did not accommodate isotopes.
- Modern Periodic Table (Moseley, 1913): Based on increasing atomic number. Contains 7 periods (rows) and 18 groups (columns). This is the form used today.

2. Types of Elements:
- s-block: Groups 1 and 2 (alkali and alkaline earth metals). General configuration ns¹ and ns².
- p-block: Groups 13 to 18. General configuration ns² np¹ to ns² np⁶. Includes metals, metalloids, non-metals, halogens, and noble gases.
- d-block: Groups 3 to 12. Transition metals. General configuration (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns⁰⁻².
- f-block: Lanthanoids (4f) and Actinoids (5f). Inner transition elements.

3. Periodic Trends (direction and reason are critical for assertion-reason questions):
- Atomic Radius: Decreases across a period (increasing nuclear charge pulls electrons closer); increases down a group (new shells added). Noble gases have the largest radius in their period (measured as van der Waals radius).
- Ionisation Enthalpy: Energy required to remove the outermost electron. Increases across a period (smaller size, higher nuclear charge); decreases down a group. Exception: Be > B and N > O due to extra stability of filled/half-filled subshells.
- Electron Gain Enthalpy: Energy released when an electron is added. Becomes more negative across a period; less negative down a group. Halogens have the most negative values. Fluorine has less negative electron gain enthalpy than chlorine due to its small size causing inter-electronic repulsion.
- Electronegativity: Ability to attract shared electrons in a bond. Increases across a period; decreases down a group. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (Pauling scale: 4.0).
- Metallic Character: Loses electrons easily. Decreases across a period; increases down a group. Francium is the most metallic naturally occurring element.

4. Important Blocks and Their Properties:
- Alkali Metals (Group 1): Highly reactive, soft, low melting points, form +1 ions, burn in oxygen giving oxides/peroxides/superoxides. Stored in kerosene.
- Alkaline Earth Metals (Group 2): Less reactive than Group 1, harder, higher melting points, form +2 ions.
- Halogens (Group 17): Most reactive non-metals, exist as diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂), form -1 ions, strong oxidising agents.
- Noble Gases (Group 18): Completely filled valence shells, chemically inert, used in lighting and as shielding gases.

Chemical Bonding

Chemical bonding explains how atoms combine to form molecules. The exam tests both ionic and covalent bonding concepts.

1. Ionic Bond (Electrovalent Bond):
- Formed by complete transfer of one or more electrons from a metal to a non-metal, creating oppositely charged ions held together by electrostatic forces.
- Conditions: Low ionisation enthalpy of metal, high electron gain enthalpy of non-metal, large lattice energy.
- Properties: High melting and boiling points, soluble in water (polar solvents), conduct electricity in molten/aqueous state but not in solid state, hard and brittle crystals.
- Example: NaCl — Na donates one electron to Cl; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ form an ionic lattice.

2. Covalent Bond:
- Formed by mutual sharing of electron pairs between two non-metals.
- Single bond (sharing 1 pair), Double bond (sharing 2 pairs), Triple bond (sharing 3 pairs).
- Properties: Lower melting and boiling points than ionic compounds, insoluble in water (generally), poor conductors of electricity, directional bonds.
- Example: H₂O — Oxygen shares electrons with two hydrogens forming two single covalent bonds.

3. Coordinate (Dative) Bond:
- A special covalent bond where both shared electrons come from the same atom. Denoted by an arrow (→).
- Example: NH₄⁺ (ammonium ion) — the fourth H⁺ accepts a lone pair from NH₃. Also in CO, SO₂, and H₃O⁺.

4. Bond Parameters:
- Bond Length: Distance between nuclei of two bonded atoms.
- Bond Angle: Angle between the axes of two bonds originating from the same atom.
- Bond Enthalpy: Energy required to break one mole of a particular bond in gaseous state.
- Dipole Moment: Measure of polarity in a covalent bond due to unequal sharing of electrons (μ = q × d).

5. VSEPR Theory (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion):
- Electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves to minimise repulsion.
- 2 electron pairs → Linear (180°)
- 3 electron pairs → Trigonal planar (120°)
- 4 electron pairs → Tetrahedral (109.5°)
- 5 electron pairs → Trigonal bipyramidal
- 6 electron pairs → Octahedral
- Lone pairs occupy more space than bond pairs, reducing bond angles (e.g., H₂O is bent ~104.5°, NH₃ is pyramidal ~107°).

6. Valence Bond Theory:
- Sigma (σ) bond: Head-on overlap along the internuclear axis; stronger.
- Pi (π) bond: Sideways/lateral overlap; weaker; always present with a sigma bond in double and triple bonds.

States of Matter and Solutions

This section covers the behaviour of solids, liquids, and gases, plus solutions — all frequently tested through conceptual and calculation-based questions.

1. Gaseous State:
- Ideal Gas Equation: PV = nRT, where P = pressure, V = volume, n = number of moles, R = universal gas constant (0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹), T = temperature in Kelvin.
- Boyle's Law: P ∝ 1/V at constant T (PV = constant).
- Charles's Law: V ∝ T at constant P (V/T = constant).
- Gay-Lussac's Law: P ∝ T at constant V (P/T = constant).
- Avogadro's Law: V ∝ n at constant T and P.
- Kinetic Molecular Theory assumptions: Gas consists of tiny particles in continuous random motion; collisions are elastic; no intermolecular forces; average kinetic energy ∝ absolute temperature.
- Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature; described by Van der Waals equation.

2. Liquid State:
- Inter molecular forces: London dispersion forces (temporary dipoles, weakest), dipole-dipole interactions, hydrogen bonding (strongest among intermolecular forces, occurs when H is bonded to F, O, or N).
- Surface tension: Tendency of liquid surface to contract; caused by cohesive forces. Mercury has very high surface tension.
- Viscosity: Resistance to flow. Increases with stronger intermolecular forces; decreases with temperature.
- vapour pressure: Pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with its liquid. Increases with temperature; decreases with addition of non-volatile solute.

3. Solid State:
- Crystalline solids have regular repeating pattern (e.g., NaCl, diamond, quartz). Amorphous solids lack long-range order (e.g., glass, rubber, plastics).
- Unit cell: Smallest repeating unit of a crystal lattice. Seven systems: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, rhombohedral, monoclinic, triclinic.
- Common cubic unit cells: Simple cubic (SCC), Body-centred cubic (BCC), Face-centred cubic (FCC).
- Packing efficiency: FCC = 74%, BCC = 68%, SCC = 52%.

4. Solutions:
- A solution is a homogeneous mixture of solute (minor component) and solvent (major component).
- Concentration terms: Mass percentage, volume percentage, mass/volume percentage, molarity (M = moles of solute / volume of solution in L), molality (m = moles of solute / mass of solvent in kg), mole fraction (χ = moles of one component / total moles).
- Solubility: Maximum amount of solute that dissolves in 100 g of solvent at a given temperature. Increases with temperature for most solids; decreases with temperature for gases (Henry's Law).
- Henry's Law: Solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid (S = K_H × P).
- Colligative properties depend on the number of solute particles, not their nature: relative lowering of vapour pressure, elevation of boiling point, depression of freezing point, osmotic pressure (π = CRT).

Acids, Bases, and Salts

A foundational topic with direct real-life applications and frequent numerical/conceptual questions in NDA/CDS.

1. Definitions:
- Arrhenius Definition: Acid produces H⁺ ions in aqueous solution; base produces OH⁻ ions.
- Bronsted-Lowry Definition: Acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; base is a proton acceptor. Conjugate acid-base pair differs by one proton.
- Lewis Definition: Acid is an electron-pair acceptor; base is an electron-pair donor. BF₃ and AlCl₃ are classic Lewis acids; NH₃ is a Lewis base.

2. Strong vs Weak Acids and Bases:
- Strong acids (completely dissociate): HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, HBr, HI, HClO₄.
- Weak acids (partial dissociation): CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, H₃PO₄, HF, HCN.
- Strong bases: NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂.
- Weak base: NH₄OH (NH₃ in water).

3. pH Scale:
- pH = -log[H⁺]. Range 0 to 14.
- pH < 7: Acidic; pH = 7: Neutral; pH > 7: Basic (alkaline).
- pOH = -log[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C).
- Indicator range: Litmus (red < 4.5, blue > 8.3), Phenolphthalein (colourless < 8.2, pink > 10), Methyl orange (red < 3.1, yellow > 4.4).

4. Properties and Reactions:
- Acids react with metals to produce salt + hydrogen gas (e.g., Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂).
- Acids react with bases to form salt + water (neutralisation).
- Acids react with metal carbonates/bicarbonates to give salt + water + CO₂ (CO₂ turns limewater milky).
- Bases react with ammonium salts on heating to release ammonia gas (NH₃, turns red litmus blue, pungent smell).

5. Salts:
- Normal salt: From complete replacement of replaceable hydrogen (e.g., NaCl, Na₂SO₄).
- Acid salt: Partial replacement (e.g., NaHCO₃, NaHSO₄).
- Basic salt: Partial replacement of OH⁻ (e.g., Basic copper carbonate CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂).
- Double salts: Dissociate into multiple ions in solution (e.g., Potash alum K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O).
- Complex salts: Form coordination compounds (e.g., K₄[Fe(CN)₆]).
- Hydrated salts contain water of crystallisation (e.g., CuSO₄·5H₂O is blue; anhydrous is white).

6. Common Salts and Their Uses:
- Sodium chloride (NaCl): Table salt, raw material for NaOH, Cl₂, NaHCO₃.
- Sodium hydroxide (NaOH): Soap industry, paper, artificial silk, water purification.
- Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂): Disinfectant, bleach for cotton/linen.
- Baking soda (NaHCO₃): Antacid, fire extinguisher, baking powder ingredient.
- Washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O): Glass, soap, paper industry, water softening.
- Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O): Medical casts, toys, smoothing surfaces. On heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) at 373 K.

Metals and Non-Metals

This topic covers physical and chemical properties of elements, their extraction, and corrosion — a staple of defence examination GK.

1. Physical Properties of Metals:
- Solid at room temperature (except mercury, which is liquid). Good conductors of heat and electricity. Malleable (can be hammered into sheets) and ductile (drawn into wires). Sonorous (produce sound). Hard and dense with high melting/boiling points. Have metallic luster. Form basic oxides.

2. Physical Properties of Non-Metals:
- Can be solid, liquid (bromine), or gas. Brittle in solid form. Poor conductors (graphite is an exception). Non-lustrous (iodine is lustrous). Form acidic or neutral oxides. Low melting/boiling points generally.

3. Chemical Properties of Metals:
- React with oxygen: Metal + O₂ → Metal oxide (basic). E.g., 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O.
- React with water: Metal + H₂O → Metal hydroxide + H₂. Highly reactive metals (K, Na, Ca) react violently; Fe reacts with steam; Au and Pt do not react.
- React with dilute acids: Metal + Acid → Salt + H₂. Exception: Nitric acid (HNO₃) is a strong oxidising agent; with metals it produces NO₂ or NO instead of H₂.
- React with salts of less reactive metals (displacement): More reactive metal displaces less reactive metal from its salt solution. E.g., Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu.
- Electrochemical series order of reactivity (most to least): K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Ag > Au.

4. Extraction of Metals:
- Ore: Naturally occurring mineral from which metal can be extracted economically.
- Gangue: Earthy impurities mixed with ore.
- Steps: Crushing and grinding → Concentration (gravity, magnetic, Froth flotation) → Extraction of metal from concentrated ore → Refining (electrolytic, zone refining).
- Metals low in reactivity series occur free in nature (Au, Ag, Pt). Moderate reactivity metals occur as oxides/sulfides/carbonates. High reactivity metals occur as chlorides/sulfates.
- Metallurgy of iron: Haematite (Fe₂O₃) is the main ore. Smelted in a blast furnace with coke (reducing agent) and limestone (flux). Product is cast iron/pig iron.

5. Alloys:
- A homogeneous mixture of a metal with one or more other metals or non-metals.
- Brass: Copper + Zinc. Bronze: Copper + Tin. Solder: Lead + Tin (low melting point, used in electrical wiring). Steel: Iron + Carbon (+ Cr, Ni for stainless steel). AMU (Alnico): Al + Ni + Co. Duralumin: Al + Cu + Mg + Mn. Gun metal: Cu + Sn + Zn.

6. Corrosion:
- The gradual destruction of metals by chemical reaction with the environment (moisture, air, acids).
- Rusting of iron: 4Fe + 3O₂ + xH₂O → Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (hydrated iron oxide, reddish-brown).
- Prevention: Painting, greasing/oiling, galvanisation (coating with zinc), sacrificial protection, alloying, electroplating.

Carbon and Its Compounds (Organic Chemistry Basics)

Basic organic chemistry is included in NDA/CDS at a descriptive level. Focus on properties, functional groups, and common organic compounds.

1. Why Carbon Forms So Many Compounds:
- Catenation: Self-linking property of carbon to form long chains, branched chains, and closed rings due to strong C-C bond.
- Tetravalency: Carbon has 4 valence electrons; forms four covalent bonds with other atoms.
- Small size allows it to form strong bonds and stable compounds.

2. Types of Bonds in Carbon Compounds:
- Single (C-C), Double (C=C), Triple (C≡C).
- Saturated compounds: Only single bonds (alkanes). Unsaturated compounds: Contain double or triple bonds (alkenes, alkynes).

3. Nomenclature (IUPAC basics):
- Prefix (substituents) + Root (carbon chain length: 1=meth, 2=eth, 3=prop, 4=but, 5=pent, 6=hex, 7=hept, 8=oct) + Primary suffix (-ane/-ene/-yne) + Secondary suffix (functional group).
- Examples: CH₄ = Methane; C₂H₆ = Ethane; C₂H₄ = Ethene; C₂H₂ = Ethyne.

4. Homologous Series:
- A family of compounds with the same functional group, same general formula, and successive members differing by -CH₂.
- Alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ. Alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂. Alcohols: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH. Aldehydes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁CHO. Carboxylic acids: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH.
- Chemical properties similar within a series; physical properties change gradually.

5. Functional Groups (must memorise):
- -OH : Hydroxyl group → Alcohols (e.g., ethanol C₂H₅OH)
- -CHO : Aldehyde group (e.g., ethanal CH₃CHO)
- -COOH : Carboxyl group (e.g., ethanoic acid CH₃COOH, vinegar)
- -C=O : Keto group (e.g., propanone/acetone CH₃COCH₃)
- -X : Halogen → Haloalkanes (e.g., CH₃Cl)
- -O- : Ether linkage (e.g., dimethyl ether CH₃-O-CH₃)

6. Important Reactions of Carbon Compounds:
- Combustion: Organic compounds burn in oxygen to give CO₂, H₂O, and heat. C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O.
- Oxidation: Alcohols oxidised to carboxylic acids using alkaline KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇. Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid.
- Addition reactions: Hydrogenation of unsaturated hydrocarbons using Ni/Pt catalyst → saturated hydrocarbons. Used in vegetable oil → vegetable ghee (vanaspati ghee).
- Substitution reactions: Hydrogen atoms in alkanes replaced by halogens in presence of sunlight. E.g., CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (UV light).
- Esterification: Alcohol + Carboxylic acid → Ester + Water (conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst). Esters have sweet smell, used in perfumes.

7. Soap and Detergent:
- Soap: Sodium/potassium salt of long-chain carboxylic acids. Made by saponification (hydrolysis of fats/oils with NaOH).
- Micelle formation: Soap has a hydrophilic (ionic) head and hydrophobic (hydrocarbon) tail. In water, they form micelles trapping grease. Hard water (containing Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) forms scum with soap (insoluble calcium/magnesium soaps).
- Detergents: Sodium salt of sulphonated long-chain carboxylic acids. Work in hard water; do not form scum. More effective cleaning agents than soap.

Hydrogen and Important Gases

Hydrogen and several common gases are frequently asked in NDA/CDS General Knowledge through direct factual and application-based questions.

1. Hydrogen:
- Position: Placed in Group 1 but its properties resemble both halogens (Group 17) and alkali metals (Group 1). It can lose an electron like alkali metals (H → H⁺ + e⁻) and gain one like halogens (H + e⁻ → H⁻).
- Isotopes: Protium (¹H¹, most abundant, ~99.98%), Deuterium (¹H²/D, stable), Tritium (¹H³/T, radioactive, half-life 12.3 years).
- Preparation: Lab method — Zn + dilute H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂. Also: Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂; Steam passed over red-hot iron → Fe₃O₄ + H₂.
- Chemical性质: burns in oxygen to form water (explosive mixture — detonating gas 2:1 H₂:O₂); reduces metal oxides to metals; adds to unsaturated hydrocarbons (hydrogenation).
- Uses: Hydrogenation of oils, fuel (rocket fuel, atomic hydrogen torch), methanol production, hydrazine production.

2. Important Gases:
- Oxygen (O₂): Discovered by Priestley (1774). Colourless, odourless gas. Supports combustion but does not burn itself. 21% of atmosphere. Produced by photosynthesis. Used in welding, metallurgy, and medical emergencies.
- Nitrogen (N₂): 78% of atmosphere. Colourless, odourless, inert due to strong triple bond. Used in fertilizer production (Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃), liquid nitrogen for cryogenics, food packaging (prevents oxidation).
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂): Colourless, odourless, denser than air. Does not support combustion (used in fire extinguishers). Causes greenhouse effect. Fixed by plants via photosynthesis.
- Carbon monoxide (CO): Colourless, odourless, highly poisonous. Binds to haemoglobin 200 times more strongly than oxygen, causing asphyxiation. Produced by incomplete combustion. Known as the silent killer.
- Sulphur dioxide (SO₂): Pungent-smelling, colourless gas. Acid rain contributor. Used as bleaching agent and preservative.
- Ammonia (NH₃): Colourless, pungent-smelling gas. Highly soluble in water (forms NH₄OH). Prepared by Haber process. Used in fertilizers, explosives, cleaning agents. Turns red litmus blue.
- Chlorine (Cl₂): Greenish-yellow, pungent, poisonous gas. Used for water purification, bleach production (Cl₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O), PVC manufacturing.

Chemical Energetics and Reactions

This section covers reaction types, energy changes, and basic electrochemistry — all relevant for the NDA/CDS level.

1. Types of Chemical Reactions:
- Combination: Two or more substances combine to form one product. E.g., C + O₂ → CO₂.
- Decomposition: One compound breaks into two or more simpler substances. Requires energy (heat/light/electricity). E.g., 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂ (heat).
- Displacement: A more reactive element displaces a less reactive one from its compound. E.g., Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.
- Double displacement: Exchange of ions between two compounds. E.g., Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NaCl. Precipitation reactions are a subset.
- Oxidation-Reduction (Redox): Simultaneous oxidation and reduction. Oxidation = loss of electrons / gain of oxygen / loss of hydrogen. Reduction = gain of electrons / loss of oxygen / gain of hydrogen. E.g., CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (CuO reduced, H₂ oxidised).

2. Oxidation and Reducing Agents:
- Oxidising agent: Accepts electrons (gets reduced). Examples: KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, H₂O₂, conc. HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄, O₃, Cl₂.
- Reducing agent: Donates electrons (gets oxidised). Examples: H₂, CO, C, Zn, Na, KMnO₃ (intermediate), SO₂.

3. Energy Changes in Reactions:
- Exothermic reaction: Releases heat energy. ΔH is negative. E.g., Combustion of fuels, respiration, neutralisation, thermite reaction (Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃, extremely exothermic, used in welding railway tracks).
- Endothermic reaction: Absorbs heat energy. ΔH is positive. E.g., Photosynthesis, decomposition of CaCO₃, dissolution of NH₄Cl in water (feels cold).

4. Rate of Reaction and Catalysts:
- Factors affecting rate: Concentration, temperature, surface area, catalyst presence, nature of reactants.
- Catalyst: Substance that changes the rate of a reaction without being consumed. Positive catalyst increases rate; negative catalyst (inhibitor) decreases rate.
- Examples: Fe in Haber process (NH₃ production); V₂O₅ in Contact process (H₂SO₄ production); Pt in Ostwald process (HNO₃ production); Ni in hydrogenation of oils.

5. Nuclear Chemistry (Basic):
- Nuclear reactions involve changes in the nucleus, unlike chemical reactions which involve electrons.
- Radioactivity: Spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable nuclei. Three types: Alpha (α) — helium nucleus (He²⁺), beta (β) — high-speed electron, gamma (γ) — electromagnetic radiation (no mass, no charge, highest penetrating power).
- Half-life: Time taken for half of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay.
- Nuclear fission: Splitting of a heavy nucleus into lighter nuclei (Uranium-235 bombarded with neutrons). Releases enormous energy. Basis of nuclear reactors and atomic bombs.
- Nuclear fusion: Joining of light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus (e.g., ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n). Releases even more energy per unit mass. Powers the sun and stars. Hydrogen bomb basis.

Environmental Chemistry and Important Everyday Chemicals

Environmental chemistry is a high-yield area for NDA/CDS, especially questions on pollution, greenhouse gases, and harmful chemicals.

1. Air Pollution:
- Primary pollutants: Emitted directly (SO₂, NOₓ, CO, particulate matter, ozone in troposphere).
- Secondary pollutants: Formed in atmosphere (smog, sulfuric acid, peroxyacyl nitrate/PAN).
- Photochemical smog: Formed by action of sunlight on NOₓ and hydrocarbons. Causes eye irritation, respiratory problems. PAN is a major component.
- Industrial smog (London type): Sulfur oxides + fog + particulates. Acid rain (pH < 5.6) results from SO₂ and NOₓ reacting with water to form H₂SO₄ and HNO₃.
- Particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀): Fine inhalable particles causing lung and heart diseases.

2. Greenhouse Effect and Global Warming:
- Greenhouse gases trap infrared radiation: CO₂ (~60%), CH₄ (methane), N₂O, CFCs, water vapour.
- CO₂ increase mainly from fossil fuel combustion and deforestation.
- CH₄ sources: Wetlands, cattle, rice fields, landfills.
- Ozone layer depletion: CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) release Cl radicals in stratosphere that destroy ozone (O₃). Montreal Protocol (1987) aimed to phase out CFCs. Antarctic ozone hole is a major concern.

3. Water Pollution:
- Causes: Industrial effluents, agricultural runoff (pesticides, fertilisers → eutrophication), sewage discharge, thermal pollution, oil spills.
- BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand): Measure of organic pollution. High BOD indicates heavy organic contamination, reducing dissolved oxygen for aquatic life.
- Heavy metals (lead, mercury, cadmium, arsenic) cause severe health issues (Minamata disease from mercury, Itai-itai from cadmium).

4. Soil Pollution:
- Excessive use of pesticides, fertilizers, industrial waste dumping, plastic waste accumulation.

5. Important Chemicals and Their Uses:
- Ozone (O₃): Strong oxidising agent, used for water treatment and bleaching. Stratospheric ozone protects from UV radiation.
- Phosphorus: White phosphorus (highly reactive, stored in water, glows in dark — phosphorescence); Red phosphorus (stable, used in matchboxes).
- Sulphur: Used in vulcanisation of rubber, manufacture of H₂SO₄, gunpowder, fungicides.
- Phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄): Used in fertilizers, soft drinks, detergents.
- Nitric acid (HNO₃): "King of chemicals" — used in fertilizers (ammonium nitrate), explosives (TNT, nitroglycerin), metallurgy.
- Sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄): "King of chemicals" — used in batteries, fertilizers (superphosphate), petroleum refining, textile industry. Manufactured by Contact process (V₂O₅ catalyst).

6. Toxic Chemicals and Health Effects:
- Carbon monoxide (CO): Asphyxiant; binds to hemoglobin forming carboxyhemoglobin.
- Sulfur dioxide (SO₂): Respiratory irritant; causes asthma exacerbation.
- Lead (Pb): Neurotoxin; causes anaemia, kidney damage, developmental delays in children.
- Mercury (Hg): Damages nervous system; Minamata disease.
- Asbestos: Lung fibrosis, mesothelioma (cancer).
- PCBs (Polychlorinated biphenyls): Persistent organic pollutants, bioaccumulate.
- DDT: Persistent pesticide, banned in many countries; biomagnification in food chains.

7. Nuclear Weapons (Defence-relevant):
- Atomic bomb (fission): Uses U-235 or Pu-239. First dropped on Hiroshima (U-235) and Nagasaki (Pu-239) in 1945.
- Hydrogen bomb (fusion): Uses isotopes of hydrogen (deuterium and tritium). Much more powerful than atomic bomb. First tested by India in 1998 (Shakti). Pakistan tested in 1998. North Korea has conducted tests.
- Nuclear fallout: Radioactive debris spread over a wide area after a nuclear explosion.

Important facts

  • Lavoisier is called the Father of Chemistry.
  • Avogadro's number = 6.022 × 10²³ particles per mole.
  • STP: 0°C and 1 atm. Molar volume of any ideal gas at STP = 22.4 L.
  • Fluorine is the most electronegative element (4.0 on Pauling scale).
  • Francium is the most electropositive naturally occurring element.
  • Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe (~75% by mass).
  • Oxygen is the most abundant element in Earth's crust (~46.6% by mass).
  • Silicon is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust (~27.7%).
  • Iron (Fe) is the most abundant element in Earth's core.
  • Cesium (Cs) has the lowest ionisation energy among stable elements.
  • Helium has the lowest boiling point of all elements (~4 K).
  • Diamond is the hardest natural substance (10 on Mohs scale); talc is the softest (1).
  • Graphite is the only non-metal that conducts electricity.
  • The chemical name of vinegar is dilute acetic acid (CH₃COOH, ~5-8% solution).
  • Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃).
  • Washing soda is sodium carbonate decahydrate (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O).
  • Bleaching powder is calcium oxychloride (CaOCl₂).
  • Plaster of Paris is calcium sulfate hemihydrate (CaSO₄·½H₂O).
  • Gypsum is calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO₄·2H₂O).
  • Common salt (NaCl) is obtained from both rock salt deposits and seawater.
  • The contact process is used for manufacturing H₂SO₄; the Haber process for NH₃.
  • Thermite reaction: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + heat. Used for welding railway tracks.
  • Lightning fixes atmospheric nitrogen into NO, which eventually forms nitrates — a natural fertilisation process.
  • Chlorophyll is the green pigment in plants containing magnesium at its centre.
  • Haemoglobin contains iron; Vitamin B₁₂ contains cobalt.
  • Rusting requires both oxygen and moisture. Galvanisation prevents rusting by coating iron with zinc.
  • Ozone (O₃) is an allotrope of oxygen. It is diamagnetic and paramagnetic respectively (O₂ is paramagnetic).
  • White phosphorus is stored under water because it is pyrophoric (catches fire spontaneously in air).
  • The pH of human blood is tightly regulated around 7.35–7.45.
  • Neon signs emit reddish-orange light; sodium vapour lamps emit yellow light.
  • Milk of lime (suspension of Ca(OH)₂) is used to test for CO₂ (turns milky).
  • Dry ice is solid CO₂. It sublimes at -78.5°C and is used as a coolant.
  • Heavy water (D₂O) is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors.
  • The most abundant gas in the universe is hydrogen; in Earth's atmosphere it is nitrogen (78%).
  • Ammonia (NH₃) has the highest solubility in water among common gases.
  • Fulgurite is a glassy tube formed when lightning strikes sandy soil.

Formulas and relationships

Ideal Gas Equation: PV = nRT (P=pressure, V=volume, n=moles, R=0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹, T=temperature in Kelvin)
Molarity (M) = moles of solute / volume of solution in litres
Molality (m) = moles of solute / mass of solvent in kilograms
Mole fraction (χ_A) = moles of A / total moles of all components
Mass percentage = (mass of component / total mass of solution) × 100
pH = -log₁₀[H⁺]; pOH = -log₁₀[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C)
Oxidation number rules: Element in free state = 0; Sum of oxidation numbers in neutral compound = 0; For ion = charge on ion; O usually -2; H usually +1; F always -1
Half-life relationship (for first-order): t½ = 0.693 / λ, where λ is the decay constant
Relationship between Celsius and Kelvin: K = °C + 273.15
Number of particles: N = n × N_A, where N_A = 6.022 × 10²³
Molar mass = mass of given substance / number of moles
Percentage yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100
Density = mass / volume
Relative molecular mass = (mass of one molecule of substance) / (1/12 mass of one C-12 atom)

Common mistakes

  • Confusing atomic mass with atomic number — atomic number = number of protons, atomic mass ≈ protons + neutrons.
  • Thinking all non-metals are insulators — graphite (a form of carbon) conducts electricity.
  • Assuming hydrogen is placed only in Group 1 — its dual behaviour justifies its unique placement (sometimes shown separately).
  • Mistaking molecular mass and molar mass — they are numerically equal but molecular mass is dimensionless while molar mass has units of g/mol.
  • Thinking all acids contain oxygen — hydrochloric acid (HCl) and hydrobromic acid (HBr) are oxygen-free acids (hydracids).
  • Believing that pH 7 is always neutral — neutrality depends on temperature; at higher temperatures, neutral pH is slightly less than 7.
  • Assuming CO₂ is acidic — it is an acidic oxide (forms carbonic acid in water), not an acid itself.
  • Confusing isobars with isotopes — isotopes have same atomic number, different mass; isobars have same mass number, different atomic number.
  • Thinking noble gases never form compounds — xenon forms XeF₂, XeF₄, XeF₆, and XeO₃.
  • Believing that all combustion releases only CO₂ and H₂O — incomplete combustion produces toxic CO and soot (carbon particles).
  • Assuming that adding more reactant always increases product — limited by the limiting reagent.
  • Confusing the oxidising agent with the substance being oxidised — the oxidising agent itself gets reduced (gains electrons).
  • Thinking that H₂O₂ only acts as an oxidising agent — it can act as both oxidising and reducing agent depending on the reaction.
  • Mistaking Plaster of Paris setting as a simple drying process — it is actually a recrystallisation reaction where it reabsorbs water to form gypsum.
  • Assuming that all exothermic reactions occur spontaneously — spontaneity depends on both enthalpy and entropy (Gibbs free energy).
  • Forgetting that the reactivity series of metals determines displacement reactions — only a more reactive metal can displace a less reactive one.
