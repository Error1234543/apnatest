Optics — Complete Quick Revision for JEE Advanced

JEE AdvancedEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Refractive index (n): Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to speed in medium; n = c/v. It is dimensionless and always ≥ 1.
  • Critical angle: The angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the rarer medium is 90°. Given by sin C = 1/n (for denser-to-rarer boundary).
  • Total internal reflection (TIR): Phenomenon when light travels from denser to rarer medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle; the entire ray is reflected back into the denser medium.
  • Focal length of a mirror: Distance between pole and focus; f = R/2 where R is the radius of curvature.
  • Optical path: The product of refractive index and geometric distance; optical path = n·d. It represents the equivalent distance light would travel in vacuum in the same time.
  • Coherent sources: Two sources that emit waves with a constant phase difference and the same frequency. Required for sustained interference.
  • Fresnel's distance: The distance up to which ray optics is a good approximation; d_F = a²/λ where a is the aperture size.
  • Brewster's angle: The angle of incidence at which reflected light is completely polarized; tan θ_B = n (for air-to-medium interface). At this angle, reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular.

Key concepts

  • Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. Sign convention (Cartesian): distances measured against incident light direction are negative; f is negative for concave mirror, positive for convex mirror. Magnification m = -v/u = h_i/h_o. For real images m is negative (inverted); for virtual images m is positive (erect). Lateral magnification relates transverse dimensions while longitudinal magnification (for small objects along axis) is m_long = -m². JEE Advanced frequently combines mirror equations with lens equations in compound systems — always apply sign convention consistently before substituting values.
  • Thin lens formula: 1/v - 1/u = 1/f. Lens maker's formula: 1/f = (μ-1)[1/R₁ - 1/R₂] where μ is the refractive index of lens material relative to surrounding medium, and R₁, R₂ are radii of curvature of the two surfaces (signs per Cartesian convention). When a lens is immersed in a medium of refractive index μ_m, replace μ by μ_lens/μ_m. If μ_lens < μ_m, a converging lens becomes diverging and vice versa. Power of a lens P = 1/f (in metres) measured in Dioptres. For combination of thin lenses in contact: P_total = P₁ + P₂ + ... and 1/f_eq = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂. For separated lenses: 1/f_eq = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ - d/(f₁f₂).
  • Refraction at a single spherical surface: μ₂/v - μ₁/u = (μ₂ - μ₁)/R. This is the building block for deriving the lens maker's formula by applying it twice. Prism formula: Deviation δ = (i + e) - A where A is the prism angle, i is angle of incidence, e is angle of emergence. Minimum deviation occurs when i = e and the ray passes symmetrically: μ = sin[(A+δ_m)/2] / sin(A/2). For a thin prism (small A): δ ≈ (μ-1)A. In a compound prism system, two prisms can be combined to produce dispersion without deviation or deviation without dispersion by appropriate choice of materials and angles.
  • TIR condition: (1) Light must travel from denser to rarer medium. (2) Angle of incidence must exceed critical angle. Critical angle depends on the pair of media: sin C = μ_rare/μ_dense. Applications: Diamond's brilliance (C ≈ 24.4° for diamond-air), mirage formation (hot air near ground has lower n causing TIR), optical fibres (light trapped by successive TIR inside core of higher n surrounded by cladding of lower n), half-silvered mirrors, and prism binoculars. Apparent depth: For near-normal viewing, apparent depth d' = d/n (object in denser medium appears raised). For oblique viewing, the shift is more complex and involves Snell's law.
  • Simple microscope: A single convex lens used with the object within focal length. Maximum angular magnification M = 1 + D/f when image is at least distance of distinct vision D (usually 25 cm). When image is at infinity, M = D/f. Compound microscope: Two lenses — objective (short f_o) and eyepiece (short f_e). Tube length L = distance between focal points. Magnification M = (v_o/u_o)(1 + D/f_e) for image at D, or M = (v_o/u_o)(D/f_e) for image at infinity. For normal adjustment, L = v_o + u_e. Telescopes: Refracting telescope uses two convex lenses. Magnifying power M = f_o/f_e for normal adjustment (image at infinity). Resolving limit of a telescope: θ_min = 1.22λ/D where D is the aperture diameter. Larger aperture gives better resolution and brighter images.
  • Huygens' principle states that every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets spreading out in all directions with the speed of light in that medium. The new wavefront is the tangential envelope of these secondary wavelets. This principle is used to derive laws of reflection and refraction geometrically. A wavefront is the locus of all points oscillating in the same phase. For a point source in a homogeneous isotropic medium, wavefronts are spherical. For a linear source, they are cylindrical. For parallel rays, they are plane. In anisotropic media, wavefronts may not be spheres even for point sources.
  • Path difference Δ = d·sin θ ≈ d·y/D for small angles where d is slit separation, D is screen distance, y is position on screen. Constructive interference (bright fringes): Δ = nλ ⇒ y_n = nλD/d. Destructive interference (dark fringes): Δ = (2n-1)λ/2 ⇒ y_n = (2n-1)λD/(2d). Fringe width β = λD/d. Intensity at any point: I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂)cos φ where φ = 2πΔ/λ is the phase difference. For identical slits (I₁ = I₂ = I₀): I = 4I₀cos²(φ/2). Maximum intensity = 4I₀, minimum = 0. Angular fringe width = λ/d. Linear fringe width β = λD/d. Fringe width is independent of order n and slit width (as long as slits are narrow).
  • Light reflects from both surfaces of a thin film, creating interference. For a film of thickness t and refractive index μ illuminated at angle i (or normally): Path difference Δ = 2μt cos r ± λ/2 (the λ/2 term accounts for phase change on reflection from a denser medium — one phase reversal adds λ/2). For normal incidence, r = 0: Δ = 2μt ± λ/2. Constructive: 2μt = (2n-1)λ/2 (with one phase reversal). Destructive: 2μt = nλ (with one phase reversal). For soap bubbles and oil films, white light produces colored patterns because different wavelengths satisfy constructive/destructive conditions at different thicknesses. The condition depends critically on whether there are zero, one, or two phase reversals at the interfaces.
  • Single-slit diffraction: For slit width a, minima occur at a·sin θ = nλ (n = ±1, ±2, ...). Central maximum extends from -λ/a to +λ/a in sin θ. The angular width of central maximum = 2λ/a. Secondary maxima occur approximately at a·sin θ = (2n+1)λ/2 but are much weaker. Intensity distribution: I = I₀[sin(β/2)/(β/2)]² where β = 2πa·sin θ/λ. Width of nth secondary maximum ≈ half the width of central maximum. For diffraction grating with N slits separated by d: Principal maxima at d·sin θ = nλ. Resolving power R = nN where n is the order and N is the total number of slits. More slits give sharper, narrower principal maxima.
  • Unpolarized light vibrating in all planes perpendicular to propagation can be polarized by reflection, refraction, scattering, or passing through a polaroid. Malus' law: When plane-polarized light of intensity I₀ passes through an analyzer at angle θ, transmitted intensity I = I₀cos²θ. For unpolarized light incident on a polarizer, transmitted intensity is I₀/2. Brewster's law: At Brewster's angle θ_B, reflected light is completely polarized; tan θ_B = μ. At this angle, reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular: θ_B + r = 90°. Double refraction in calcite produces ordinary (o-ray, follows Snell's law) and extraordinary (e-ray, does not follow Snell's law) rays. Nicol prism and polaroid sheets are common polarizers.
  • For light, Doppler shift depends only on relative velocity (unlike sound which needs medium). For receding source: Δλ/λ ≈ v/c for v << c. Redshift (receding): λ_observed > λ_emitted. Blueshift (approaching): λ_observed < λ_emitted. For a star moving away at speed v, the fractional wavelength shift is Δλ/λ = v/c. This is used to determine recession velocities of galaxies (Hubble's law: v = H₀d). For transverse Doppler effect (relativistic), there is a second-order shift even for perpendicular motion. In JEE Advanced, Doppler problems often combine with interference — e.g., moving source or mirror changes path difference dynamically.

Important facts

  • In YDSE, if one slit is covered, intensity at the center drops to I₀ (from 4I₀), and the pattern becomes a single-slit diffraction pattern.
  • If the source slit in YDSE has finite width, visibility of fringes decreases. For complete disappearance, source width w = λD'/d where D' is distance from source to double slit.
  • Introducing a thin transparent sheet of thickness t and refractive index μ in front of one slit shifts the fringe pattern by y = (μ-1)tD/d toward that slit.
  • For a glass prism with refractive index μ and angle A, the condition for TIR inside the prism (no emergence) is A > 2C where C is the critical angle.
  • When a convex lens is cut into two halves and displaced perpendicular to the principal axis, each half forms its own image — useful in interferometry setups.
  • In a compound microscope, increasing the objective focal length decreases magnification; decreasing the eyepiece focal length increases magnification.
  • The resolving power of a telescope increases with larger aperture and shorter wavelength — hence space telescopes use UV/optical and large mirrors.
  • For a glass slab of thickness t, lateral shift s = t·sin(i-r)/cos r where r is the angle of refraction.
  • Newton's rings are formed due to interference between light reflected from the bottom of the lens and the top of the glass plate — fringes are concentric circles with a dark center.
  • Fresnel's biprism creates two virtual coherent sources from a single source — it is the practical method to obtain coherent sources for interference experiments.
  • The number of interference fringes that cross a point when a thin film thickness changes by Δt is n = 2μΔt/λ (for normal incidence with one phase reversal).
  • For diffraction, the first minimum in single-slit pattern occurs at sin θ = λ/a. If a < λ, no minimum exists and the slit acts as a point source.

Memory aids

  • Mirror formula mnemonic: 'One over V plus one over U equals one over F' — same form as lens formula but with different sign conventions (mirror: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f; lens: 1/v - 1/u = 1/f).
  • For prism minimum deviation: 'Mu equals sine of half sum divided by sine of half angle' — μ = sin[(A+δ_m)/2]/sin(A/2). Remember: at minimum deviation, the ray passes symmetrically.
  • Fringe width memory aid: 'Beta equals Lambda D over d' — β = λD/d. Wider fringes with longer wavelength, larger screen distance, or smaller slit separation.
  • Brewster's law: 'Tan theta naught equals mu' — θ_B is the angle where reflected light is totally polarized, and the reflected and refracted rays are at right angles.
  • Resolving power mnemonic: 'Rayleigh's criterion: 1.22 lambda over D' — larger aperture D means better resolution (smaller angular limit).
  • Phase change on reflection: 'From rare to dense, phase change takes the fence' — reflection from a denser medium causes a π phase shift (equivalent to λ/2 path change).
  • Thin film interference: 'Two times mu t cosine r' — path difference involves 2μt cos r, remember to add or subtract λ/2 based on number of phase reversals.

Exam tips

  • JEE Advanced frequently combines Ray Optics and Wave Optics in a single multi-concept question. Practice identifying which part of the problem requires mirror/lens equations versus interference/diffraction conditions.
  • For numerical problems involving YDSE with a glass slab or liquid introduced, write the additional path difference (μ-1)t clearly before equating to nλ for fringe shift calculations.
  • When dealing with multiple reflective/refractive surfaces (e.g., mirror+lens combinations), apply the formula sequentially, treating the image from the first surface as the object for the second — pay careful attention to whether the object is real or virtual for the second element.
  • In diffraction-grating problems, remember that the number of observable orders is limited by |sin θ| ≤ 1, so n_max ≤ d/λ. Always check this constraint.
  • For polarization problems involving multiple polaroids, apply Malus' law successively: I_after_each = I_before · cos²θ where θ is the angle between adjacent polarizer axes.
  • In TIR-based problems with prisms, draw the ray diagram carefully — the angle of incidence at the second surface depends on the prism angle and the refraction angle at the first surface.
  • For apparent depth/shift problems with multiple layers, calculate the apparent shift for each layer separately and sum them: total shift = Σt_i(1 - 1/μ_i).
  • When solving compound microscope or telescope problems, remember that the final image is usually formed at infinity for normal adjustment — this simplifies the calculation significantly.
  • Be alert to questions asking about the effect of changing parameters: doubling slit separation halves fringe width; doubling screen distance doubles fringe width; using a longer wavelength increases fringe width.
  • For matrix-match or multiple-correct questions on optics, verify each option independently against the fundamental formulas rather than trying to eliminate by comparison alone.

Common traps

  • Sign convention errors: Using mirror formula signs for lens problems or vice versa. Always write down the sign convention being used before substituting values. For mirrors, object distance u is typically negative; for lenses, u is negative by Cartesian convention.
  • Forgetting the λ/2 term in thin film interference: When only one reflection undergoes a phase change (air-film-glass or air-film-air with different indices), include λ/2 in the path difference. With zero or two phase reversals, do not include it.
  • Confusing fringe width with fringe spacing: Fringe width β is the distance between consecutive bright (or dark) fringes. Do not confuse with the position y_n of the nth fringe from the center.
  • Using μ instead of relative refractive index in lens maker's formula: The formula uses μ = μ_lens/μ_medium. If the lens is in water, use μ_glass/μ_water, not just μ_glass.
  • Assuming all maxima in diffraction are equally bright: In single-slit diffraction, the central maximum is 4 times brighter than the first secondary maximum and brightness decreases rapidly for higher orders.
  • Treating YDSE fringes as equally spaced without checking approximation: β = λD/d holds for small angles. For large angles or when precise positions are needed, use Δ = d sin θ exactly.
  • Incorrect application of Snell's law at curved surfaces: Remember that R is positive if the center of curvature is on the side of the outgoing ray, negative otherwise.
  • Forgetting that intensity is proportional to amplitude squared: When combining amplitudes, I ∝ A², so interfering amplitudes add algebraically before squaring.
  • Mixing up longitudinal and lateral magnification: Longitudinal magnification m_L = -m² (for small axial objects), not equal to lateral magnification m.
  • Assuming TIR occurs at any angle in a prism: TIR inside a prism requires the prism angle to be sufficiently large (A > 2C) and the incidence angle to be in a specific range for the ray to actually hit the second face at an angle exceeding C.
