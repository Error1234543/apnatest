JEE Advanced Physics — Waves and Thermodynamics: Quick Revision

JEE AdvancedEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Progressive wave: a disturbance that travels through a medium carrying energy from one point to another without permanent displacement of the medium's particles. Mathematically y(x,t) = A sin(kx ± ωt + φ).
  • Standing (stationary) wave: formed by superposition of two identical progressive waves traveling in opposite directions. Nodes are points of zero displacement; antinodes are points of maximum displacement. Form: y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt).
  • Longitudinal wave: particle displacement is parallel to wave propagation (e.g., sound in air). Characterized by compressions and rarefactions.
  • Transverse wave: particle displacement is perpendicular to wave propagation (e.g., string waves). Requires a medium with shear modulus — cannot propagate in fluids.
  • Fundamental frequency (first harmonic): the lowest natural frequency of vibration of a system. For a stretched string fixed at both ends: f₁ = v/(2L); for an open-open pipe: f₁ = v/(2L); for a closed-open pipe: f₁ = v/(4L).
  • Beats: periodic variation in intensity resulting from superposition of two waves of slightly different frequencies. Beat frequency = |f₁ − f₂|. Maximum amplitude = 2A, minimum amplitude = 0.
  • Doppler effect: apparent change in frequency perceived by an observer due to relative motion between source and observer. f' = f[(v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s)], where signs depend on direction of motion.
  • Degree of freedom (f): number of independent ways a molecule can store energy. Monatomic: f = 3 (translational only); diatomic at room temperature: f = 5 (3 trans. + 2 rot.); diatomic at high temperature: f = 7 (adds 2 vibrational).
  • Internal energy (U): total energy stored within a system due to molecular motion and interactions. For ideal gas: U = (f/2)nRT = (nRT)/(γ−1).
  • Heat capacity ratio (adiabatic index): γ = C_p/C_v. For monatomic gas γ = 5/3 ≈ 1.667; diatomic γ = 7/5 = 1.4; polyatomic γ = 4/3 ≈ 1.333.
  • Carnot engine: a theoretical reversible heat engine operating between two temperatures T_h and T_c. Efficiency η = 1 − T_c/T_h. It sets the maximum possible efficiency for any heat engine.
  • Thermal conductivity (k): material property defining rate of heat flow per unit area per unit temperature gradient. Fourier's law: dQ/dt = kA(ΔT/L). Unit: W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann law: power radiated per unit area by a black body is proportional to T⁴. P/A = εσT⁴, where σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ and ε is emissivity (ε = 1 for perfect black body).
  • Wien's displacement law: λ_max · T = b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K. Peak wavelength shifts inversely with temperature.

Key concepts

  • Speed of a wave on a stretched string: v = √(T/μ), where T = tension (N) and μ = linear mass density (kg/m). This depends ONLY on medium properties, not on frequency or amplitude.
  • Speed of sound in gas: v = √(γRT/M), where M is molar mass. Laplace correction: sound propagation is adiabatic, not isothermal, hence factor γ replaces 1.
  • Newton's formula gave v = √(P/ρ) (isothermal), which was ~15% low. Laplace corrected it using adiabatic condition: PV^γ = constant.
  • Harmonics of a string fixed at both ends: f_n = n·v/(2L) = n·f₁, where n = 1,2,3,... All harmonics present.
  • Harmonics of a pipe open at both ends: same as string — all harmonics f_n = n·v/(2L).
  • Harmonics of a pipe closed at one end: only odd harmonics f_n = n·v/(4L), n = 1,3,5,... Even harmonics are absent because boundary conditions require a node at closed end and antinode at open end.
  • End correction: open end of a pipe behaves as if it were slightly beyond the physical opening. Effective length L_eff = L + 0.6r for each open end. Closed pipe: L_eff = L + 0.6r; open pipe: L_eff = L + 1.2r.
  • Superposition principle: resultant displacement y = y₁ + y₂ + ... at any point. Used to derive standing waves, beats, and interference.
  • Energy in a progressive wave: average power P_avg = ½ρA²ω²v. Energy is proportional to square of amplitude AND square of frequency.
  • Intensity of sound: I = P/(4πr²) for point source. In dB: β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀), where I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² (threshold of hearing). Every 10 dB increase = 10× intensity.
  • Doppler effect formula (source and observer moving along line joining them): f' = f·(v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s). Sign convention: v_o positive when observer moves TOWARDS source; v_s positive when source moves TOWARDS observer.
  • When source moves faster than sound (v_s > v): shock wave / sonic boom forms a Mach cone. Mach angle: sin θ = v/v_s = 1/M, where M is Mach number.
  • First law of thermodynamics: ΔU = Q − W, where W is work done BY the system. Sign convention: Q positive when heat added, W positive when gas expands.
  • Work done in isobaric process: W = PΔV = nRΔT. Work done in isochoric process: W = 0. Work done in isothermal process: W = nRT ln(V_f/V_i) = nRT ln(P_i/P_f).
  • Work done in adiabatic process: W = (P_iV_i − P_fV_f)/(γ−1) = nR(T_i − T_f)/(γ−1). No heat exchange: Q = 0.
  • PV diagram: area under curve = work done. Clockwise cycle → net work done BY gas (heat engine). Anti-clockwise → net work done ON gas (refrigerator/heat pump).
  • Carnot cycle: four reversible processes — isothermal expansion (absorb Q_h at T_h), adiabatic expansion (cool to T_c), isothermal compression (reject Q_c at T_c), adiabatic compression (return to T_h).
  • Entropy change: ΔS = ∫dQ_rev/T. For irreversible processes, ΔS_universe > 0 (Second Law). Entropy is a state function; total entropy of universe never decreases.
  • Zeroth law of thermodynamics: if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. Defines temperature.
  • Coefficient of volume expansion: β = (1/V)(dV/dT). For isotropic solids: linear expansion α = β/3. Practical: L = L₀(1 + αΔT), V = V₀(1 + βΔT).
  • Specific heat capacity variation with temperature: C = dQ/(m·dT). For ideal gas: C_p − C_v = R (Mayer's relation). Ratio γ = C_p/C_v relates to degrees of freedom: γ = 1 + 2/f.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann law: net radiant power P_net = εσA(T⁴ − T₀⁴) where T₀ is surroundings temperature. For small ΔT = T − T₀, approximate Newton's law of cooling: P_net ≈ 4εσAT₀³·ΔT.
  • Newton's law of cooling: dT/dt = −k(T − T₀). For finite intervals: (T₁ − T₂)/Δt = k[(T₁ + T₂)/2 − T₀]. Useful for problems with moderate temperature differences.
  • Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation: at thermal equilibrium, emissivity = absorptivity (ε = α) at each wavelength. Good absorbers are good emitters.
  • Phonon: quantum of vibrational energy in a crystal lattice. Contributes to heat capacity at low temperatures (Debye T³ law).
  • Equipartition theorem: each degree of freedom contributes ½k_BT per molecule (½RT per mole) to internal energy. Valid when k_BT ≫ energy level spacing.

Important facts

  • Wave equation: ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²)·∂²y/∂t². Any function y = f(x ± vt) satisfies this and represents a traveling wave.
  • For transverse wave on string: v = √(T/μ). For longitudinal wave in solid: v = √(Y/ρ), where Y is Young's modulus. For sound in gas: v = √(γP/ρ) = √(γRT/M).
  • Fundamental frequency of sonometer wire: f = (1/2L)√(T/μ). Changing tension by factor 4 changes frequency by factor 2 (same as changing length by factor ½).
  • In stationary waves, adjacent nodes are λ/2 apart. Node to nearest antinode = λ/4. All particles between two nodes oscillate in phase; particles in adjacent segments are π out of phase.
  • Melde's experiment: frequency of tuning fork related to number of loops n by f = (n/2L)√(T/μ). With strings in series, tension is same but μ differs.
  • When a string fixed at one end is struck, only odd harmonics are excited: f, 3f, 5f, ...
  • Sound speed increases with temperature: v ∝ √T. At 0°C, v ≈ 331 m/s. v_T = v₀√(1 + T/273) where T is in °C.
  • Wind affects sound speed: effective speed = v ± v_wind (vector addition along propagation direction). Wind from source to observer increases effective speed.
  • In Doppler effect, if both source and observer move with same velocity (v_s = v_o), there is no frequency shift — only relative motion matters.
  • For expanding universe/red shift: f_observed = f_source·√[(1 − v/c)/(1 + v/c)] (relativistic Doppler). Redshift z = Δλ/λ = v/c for v ≪ c.
  • Adiabatic bulk modulus: B_ad = γP. Isothermal bulk modulus: B_iso = P. Hence sound speed v = √(B_ad/ρ) = √(γP/ρ).
  • Latent heat: energy required for phase change at constant temperature. Q = mL, where L_f = fusion, L_v = vaporization. For water: L_f = 3.33 × 10⁵ J/kg, L_v = 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg.
  • Triple point of water: T = 273.16 K, P = 611.657 Pa. All three phases coexist.
  • Clausius statement (2nd Law): heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot body without external work.
  • Kelvin-Planck statement (2nd Law): it is impossible to construct a heat engine that operates in a cycle and produces no effect other than absorbing heat from a reservoir and delivering an equivalent amount of work.
  • Refrigerator COP: COP = Q_c/W = T_c/(T_h − T_c) for Carnot refrigerator. Must be > 1 for practical devices.
  • Heat engine efficiency: η = W/Q_h = 1 − Q_c/Q_h = 1 − T_c/T_h (Carnot). Always η < 1 because T_c > 0.
  • Joule's law of heating: H = I²Rt. Electrical energy completely converts to heat.
  • Thermal resistance: R_th = L/(kA). Heat current analogous to electric current: ΔT = Q̇·R_th. Series: R_total = ΣR_i. Parallel: 1/R_total = Σ(1/R_i).
  • Solar constant (intensity at Earth): ≈ 1361 W/m². Solar surface temperature ≈ 5778 K from Wien's law using λ_max ≈ 500 nm.
  • Black body spectrum: peaks at λ_max = b/T. Total power ∝ T⁴. As T increases, peak shifts to shorter wavelengths (blue) and total power increases rapidly.
  • Gases: C_p − C_v = R exactly for ideal gases. Real gases deviate slightly; correction uses van der Waals equation.
  • At very high temperatures, diatomic molecules activate vibrational modes: f increases from 5 to 7, γ drops from 1.4 to 9/7 ≈ 1.286.
  • Phase change on PV diagram: horizontal line during vaporization (constant P, T while V changes). Critical point: above which liquid and gas phases become indistinguishable.
  • Carnot theorem: no engine operating between two given reservoirs can be more efficient than a Carnot engine. All reversible engines between same reservoirs have same efficiency.
  • Entropy of free expansion of ideal gas: ΔS = nR ln(V_f/V_i) > 0. Irreversible process — entropy of isolated system increases.

Memory aids

  • String wave speed mnemonic: "Tension pulls, mass resists" → v = √(T/μ). Higher tension = faster wave; heavier string = slower wave.
  • Sound in gas: "Heavy gas slows sound, hot gas speeds it" → v = √(γRT/M). Lighter molar mass M and higher T both increase v.
  • Pipe harmonics: "Open pipe sings all notes, closed pipe sings only odd" → Open: f, 2f, 3f,... | Closed: f, 3f, 5f,...
  • γ values: "Mono 5/3, Di 7/5, Poly 4/3" — remember numerator is (f+2), denominator is f. Monatomic f=3→5/3, Diatomic f=5→7/5.
  • First law sign convention: "U equals Heat IN minus Work OUT" → ΔU = Q − W. If you remember the physics (energy in stays, energy out leaves), you never need to memorize.
  • Adiabatic vs Isothermal on PV diagram: Adiabatic is steeper. Why? Because in adiabatic expansion, both P and T drop, so P falls faster than in isothermal (where T is constant). Slope ratio: adiabatic/isothermal = γ.
  • Carnot efficiency trick: η = 1 − T_c/T_h. To maximize η, either increase T_h or decrease T_c. Decreasing T_c is more effective per degree (calculus proof).
  • Beats: "Two close frequencies make the volume pulse" — imagine two singers slightly off-pitch, the combined sound waxes and wanes.
  • Doppler sign rule: "Observer towards source → + in numerator. Source towards observer → − in denominator." Both make f' larger.
  • Entropy mnemonic: "Disorder always grows in isolated systems" — think of a messy room that never cleans itself without external effort.
  • Standing wave energy: "Nodes don't move (KE=0, max PE), antinodes move most (max KE, min PE)" — energy sloshes between kinetic and potential.
  • PV work = area under curve: visualize walking on the PV diagram — the ground beneath your feet is the work.
  • Heat capacity: C_p > C_v always. Reason: at constant pressure, some heat goes into doing expansion work, so more heat is needed for same ΔT.
  • Newton's law of cooling shortcut: "Cooling rate proportional to excess temp" — works well when ΔT is moderate (not too large, not near equilibrium).
  • Thermal resistance analogy: "Heat flows like current, ΔT like voltage, R_th like electrical resistance" — all series/parallel rules apply identically.

Exam tips

  • JEE Advanced frequently combines wave and thermo concepts in single problems — e.g., finding frequency of a sonometer wire whose tension changes due to thermal expansion of the support. Always check for coupled effects.
  • For standing wave problems, draw the mode shape first. Mark nodes and antinodes explicitly. This prevents errors in identifying allowed wavelengths and frequencies.
  • In Doppler problems with moving reflected surfaces, treat the wall as both observer (receiving shifted frequency) AND source (re-emitting at that shifted frequency). Apply Doppler twice.
  • When a gas undergoes multiple processes in a cycle, compute work and heat for EACH process separately using the appropriate formula, then sum algebraically. Don't assume a single formula applies to the whole cycle.
  • For adiabatic questions, memorize TV^(γ−1) = constant and TγP^(1−γ) = constant in addition to PV^γ = constant. Problems often give T and V or T and P data.
  • Entropic arguments: if asked whether a process is reversible, check ΔS_total. If ΔS_universe = 0, reversible; if ΔS_universe > 0, irreversible. Never rely solely on intuition.
  • For heat transfer through composite slabs: thermal resistances add like electrical resistors. Series: R_eq = ΣL_i/(k_i A_i). Parallel (same ΔT, different paths): conductances add.
  • In Carnot engine problems, remember: Q_h/T_h = Q_c/T_c (for reversible cycle). This entropy equality is often more useful than computing work directly.
  • Beats problems with sonometer: when tension changes by small amount ΔT, fractional frequency change Δf/f = ½·ΔT/T. Use this linear approximation for small changes — exact calculation is unnecessary.
  • For JEE Advanced numerical answer type (NAT): retain at least 3 significant figures. Intermediate rounding errors compound significantly in multi-step thermo problems.
  • Multi-correct questions on thermodynamics often test subtle distinctions: e.g., 'Which statements are true about an adiabatic free expansion?' Remember: Q = 0, W = 0, ΔU = 0, but ΔS > 0 (irreversible!).
  • When a question mentions 'rapidly compressed' or 'suddenly expanded', immediately recognize it as adiabatic (no time for heat exchange). When it says 'slowly', consider isothermal.
  • For matrix-match type questions on heat engines: match each process (isobaric/isochoric/isothermal/adiabatic) with its correct features (Q sign, W sign, ΔU sign, PV relation). Create a quick reference table in your mind.
  • In wave on string problems with variable tension (e.g., horizontal string with hanging mass), remember T varies along the string if the string has mass. Usually simplified: T = mg at the hanging end.
  • Remember: in JEE Advanced, 'find the ratio' or 'express in terms of given parameters' often means the final answer should be clean and parameter-free. If you get a messy expression, recheck your approach before submitting.

Common traps

  • Confusing v = √(T/μ) for string waves with v = √(γP/ρ) for sound. The first is for transverse waves on a string; the second is for longitudinal sound waves in gas. Different physics entirely.
  • Using v = fλ with the wrong v. The wave speed v in v = fλ must be the SPEED OF THE WAVE IN THAT MEDIUM, not the speed of the source or observer. Doppler changes frequency, not wave speed in the medium.
  • Forgetting end correction in pipe problems. An open pipe of length L has effective length L + 0.6r per open end. Missing this gives wrong harmonic frequencies.
  • Treating all processes with W = PΔV. This is ONLY valid for isobaric processes. For isothermal: W = nRT ln(V_f/V_i). For adiabatic: W = (P_iV_i − P_fV_f)/(γ−1). For isochoric: W = 0.
  • Sign errors in First Law. Common mistake: writing ΔU = Q + W instead of ΔU = Q − W. Check consistency: if gas expands (W > 0) and no heat enters, internal energy must decrease → ΔU should be negative, which requires ΔU = Q − W.
  • Assuming adiabatic means isothermal. They are opposites: adiabatic = no heat exchange (Q = 0, T changes); isothermal = constant temperature (T constant, Q ≠ 0).
  • Forgetting that in a closed pipe only odd harmonics exist. If a problem states a pipe is 'closed at one end', frequencies are f, 3f, 5f — NOT f, 2f, 3f.
  • Applying Wien's law or Stefan-Boltzmann law to non-black bodies without emissivity factor ε. Real objects: P = εσAT⁴ with 0 < ε ≤ 1.
  • Using Celsius temperatures in Carnot efficiency η = 1 − T_c/T_h. Temperatures MUST be in Kelvin. Using °C gives completely wrong answers.
  • Thinking Doppler effect changes the wavelength emitted by the source. The source always emits at its natural frequency. Only the wavelength RECEIVED by a moving observer changes.
  • Assuming sound travels instantaneously. Sound speed (~343 m/s in air) is finite — distance = v_sound × time for echo/sonar problems.
  • Forgetting that beat frequency is |f₁ − f₂|, not (f₁ + f₂)/2. Beats come from the envelope of the sum, which oscillates at the difference frequency.
  • In standing waves, confusing node separation (λ/2) with node-to-antinode distance (λ/4). Both distances frequently appear in problems.
  • Assuming γ is the same for all temperatures. For diatomic gases, γ changes from 7/5 to 9/7 when vibrational modes activate at high T.
  • For PV diagrams: clockwise cycles produce positive work (engine), anti-clockwise cycles produce negative work (refrigerator/compressor). Getting this backwards flips all efficiency/COP calculations.
  • Using Q = mcΔT for phase changes. During melting/boiling, temperature is constant — use Q = mL instead. Combining both formulas incorrectly is a common error.
  • For adiabatic processes, using PV = constant instead of PV^γ = constant. The exponent γ is crucial — omitting it or using 1 instead is a fatal error.
  • In Doppler problems with reflecting surfaces (wall), treating the reflection as just another source without accounting for the double shift: first source-to-wall, then wall-to-observer.
  • Forgetting that thermal conductivity k is a material property independent of geometry, while thermal resistance R_th = L/(kA) depends on both geometry and material.
  • Assuming all gases behave ideally. At high pressure/low temperature, real gas deviations matter. JEE Advanced occasionally tests understanding of van der Waals corrections qualitatively.
