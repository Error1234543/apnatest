JEE Advanced Physics — Waves and Thermodynamics: Complete Formula Sheet

JEE AdvancedEnglishFormula Sheet
y(x,t) = A cos(kx - ωt + φ)

Symbols: y = displacement; A = amplitude; k = wave number = 2π/λ; ω = angular frequency = 2πf; φ = initial phase constant

Unit: SI: y, A in meters (m); k in rad/m; ω in rad/s

When to use: Describes a sinusoidal progressive (traveling) wave moving in +x direction. Used for transverse waves on strings, sound waves, electromagnetic waves.

Conditions: Valid for linear, non-dispersive media in the small-amplitude limit.

Common mistake: Confusing sign convention: (kx - ωt) is wave moving in +x; (kx + ωt) moves in -x. Also forgetting φ can shift the wave horizontally.

v = fλ = ω/k = √(T/μ)

Symbols: v = wave speed; f = frequency; λ = wavelength; T = tension in string; μ = linear mass density = m/L

Unit: v in m/s; T in N; μ in kg/m

When to use: Relates wave speed to frequency and wavelength. The string formula gives speed of transverse waves on a stretched string.

Conditions: String must be ideal (flexible, uniform). For sound in gas, v = √(γP/ρ) or v = √(γRT/M).

Common mistake: Using v = √(T/μ) for sound waves — that formula is ONLY for transverse waves on strings. Sound speed depends on γ, P, ρ or T and M of gas.

v_sound = √(γRT/M) = √(γP/ρ)

Symbols: γ = Cp/Cv (adiabatic index); R = universal gas constant = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T = absolute temperature (K); M = molar mass (kg/mol); P = pressure; ρ = density

Unit: v in m/s; T in K; M in kg/mol

When to use: Speed of sound in an ideal gas. Laplace's correction uses adiabatic rather than isothermal assumption.

Conditions: Ideal gas behavior. For diatomic gases like air, γ ≈ 1.4.

Common mistake: Forgetting to convert M from g/mol to kg/mol. Also confusing isothermal speed √(P/ρ) with adiabatic speed √(γP/ρ).

I = P/A = ½ρvω²A²

Symbols: I = intensity (power per unit area); P = power of source; A = area; ρ = medium density; v = wave speed; ω = angular frequency; A = amplitude (note: same symbol as area — context clarifies)

Unit: I in W/m²; ρ in kg/m³; v in m/s; ω in rad/s; amplitude in m

When to use: Intensity of a mechanical wave. Intensity is proportional to square of amplitude and square of frequency.

Conditions: Plane wave or spherical wave at large distance where curvature is negligible over the detector area.

Common mistake: Using amplitude A for both area and displacement amplitude in the same expression. Also forgetting I ∝ A²ω² — doubling amplitude quadruples intensity.

f_n = n v/(2L), n = 1, 2, 3, ...

Symbols: f_n = frequency of nth harmonic; v = wave speed on string; L = length of string; n = harmonic number

Unit: f in Hz; v in m/s; L in m

When to use: Natural frequencies (harmonics) of a string fixed at both ends. Fundamental (n=1): f₁ = v/(2L).

Conditions: Both ends of the string are fixed (nodes at both ends).

Common mistake: Applying this to a pipe closed at one end — that case has only odd harmonics: f_n = nv/(4L), n = 1,3,5...

f_n = nv/(4L), n = 1, 3, 5, ...

Symbols: f_n = frequency of nth allowed harmonic; v = speed of sound in the pipe; L = length of pipe; n = odd integer

Unit: f in Hz; v in m/s; L in m

When to use: Resonance frequencies of an air column closed at one end and open at the other. Only odd harmonics exist.

Conditions: One end closed (node), one end open (antinode). End correction may apply: effective length = L + 0.6r where r is pipe radius.

Common mistake: Forgetting end correction for real pipes. Also using even n values — only odd harmonics are present.

f_beats = |f₁ - f₂|

Symbols: f_beats = beat frequency; f₁, f₂ = frequencies of two superposing waves

Unit: f_beats in Hz

When to use: Number of intensity maxima heard per second when two waves of slightly different frequencies superpose.

Conditions: Frequencies must be close enough that the ear can resolve individual beats (typically within ~10 Hz for humans).

Common mistake: Calculating beat frequency between two notes that are far apart — beats become imperceptible. Also confusing beat period (1/f_beats) with beat frequency.

f_observed = f_source × (v ± v_observer)/(v ∓ v_source)

Symbols: f_observed = frequency heard by observer; f_source = emitted frequency; v = speed of sound in medium; v_observer = speed of observer; v_source = speed of source

Unit: All speeds in m/s; frequency in Hz

When to use: Doppler effect for sound when source and/or observer move along the line joining them.

Conditions: Medium is at rest. Velocities are measured relative to the medium. Sign convention: numerator + if observer moves toward source, - if away; denominator - if source moves toward observer, + if away.

Common mistake: Mixing up the sign convention. Remember: toward each other always increases observed frequency. When source speed equals sound speed, a shock wave (sonic boom) forms and the formula breaks down.

Δφ = (2π/λ) × Δx (phase difference from path difference)

Symbols: Δφ = phase difference in radians; λ = wavelength; Δx = path difference

Unit: Δφ in rad; λ and Δx in same length unit

When to use: Converting spatial path difference into phase difference for interference calculations.

Conditions: Coherent sources (constant phase relationship).

Common mistake: Using degrees instead of radians without converting. Also forgetting that a path difference of λ gives phase difference of 2π (constructive), and λ/2 gives π (destructive).

I_total = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cos(Δφ)

Symbols: I_total = resultant intensity; I₁, I₂ = individual intensities; Δφ = phase difference between the two waves at the point of interference

Unit: I in W/m²

When to use: Superposition principle for intensities of two coherent waves. Maximum when cos(Δφ)=1, minimum when cos(Δφ)=-1.

Conditions: Coherent sources required. For incoherent sources, I_total = I₁ + I₂ (no interference term).

Common mistake: Adding amplitudes directly instead of intensities, or applying the interference formula to incoherent sources. For equal intensities I₁ = I₂ = I₀: I_max = 4I₀, I_min = 0.

T_S = T_C + 273.15

Symbols: T_S = temperature in Kelvin; T_C = temperature in Celsius

Unit: T_S in K; T_C in °C

When to use: Converting between Celsius and Kelvin scales. Essential for all thermodynamics calculations.

Conditions: Exact conversion (273.15, not 273, for precision). In JEE problems 273 is often used as approximation.

Common mistake: Using 273 instead of 273.15 when high precision is needed. Forgetting to convert to Kelvin before using gas laws.

PV = nRT

Symbols: P = pressure; V = volume; n = number of moles; R = universal gas constant = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T = absolute temperature (K)

Unit: P in Pa; V in m³; n in mol; T in K

When to use: Ideal gas equation — the fundamental equation of state for an ideal gas.

Conditions: Ideal gas: negligible intermolecular forces, point particles. Good approximation at low pressure and high temperature.

Common mistake: Using P in atm or V in liters without converting to SI (Pa and m³) when combining with other SI equations. Also using R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K) with SI pressure units creates inconsistency.

W = ∫ P dV (work done BY the gas)

Symbols: W = work done by the gas; P = pressure; V = volume

Unit: W in Joules (J) when P in Pa and V in m³

When to use: Work done in any quasi-static thermodynamic process. Graphically, W = area under the P-V curve.

Conditions: Quasi-static process (system remains in equilibrium throughout). Sign convention: positive when gas expands (dV > 0).

Common mistake: Sign errors: work done BY the gas is positive during expansion, but work done ON the gas is negative. In physics convention, ΔU = Q - W, where W is work done BY the gas.

ΔU = Q - W (First Law of Thermodynamics)

Symbols: ΔU = change in internal energy; Q = heat added to the system; W = work done BY the system

Unit: All in Joules (J)

When to use: Energy conservation for thermodynamic systems. If W is defined as work done ON the gas: ΔU = Q + W.

Conditions: Applies to any process (reversible or irreversible). ΔU depends only on initial and final states (state function).

Common mistake: Confusing sign conventions between chemistry (ΔU = Q + W, W = work on system) and physics (ΔU = Q - W, W = work by system). JEE uses physics convention: ΔU = Q - W.

ΔU = nCvΔT

Symbols: ΔU = change in internal energy; n = number of moles; Cv = molar heat capacity at constant volume; ΔT = change in temperature

Unit: ΔU in J; Cv in J/(mol·K); ΔT in K

When to use: Change in internal energy of an ideal gas — depends ONLY on temperature change, regardless of the process.

Conditions: Valid for ideal gases only. Cv is constant for a given gas over moderate temperature ranges.

Common mistake: Using this for real gases or applying it with wrong Cv value. Remember: for monatomic gas Cv = 3R/2, diatomic = 5R/2 (at room temp), polyatomic = 3R (non-linear).

Q = nCΔT

Symbols: Q = heat exchanged; n = moles; C = molar heat capacity for the specific process; ΔT = temperature change

Unit: Q in J; C in J/(mol·K)

When to use: Heat involved in a process with constant molar heat capacity C.

Conditions: C must match the process: use Cv for isochoric, Cp for isobaric. C is not defined for adiabatic (Q = 0).

Common mistake: Using Cp for an isochoric process or vice versa. In an adiabatic process Q = 0 by definition, so this formula doesn't apply.

Cp - Cv = R (Mayer's relation)

Symbols: Cp = molar heat capacity at constant pressure; Cv = molar heat capacity at constant volume; R = universal gas constant

Unit: Both Cp and Cv in J/(mol·K); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)

When to use: Relates the two molar heat capacities of an ideal gas. Derived from first law applied to isobaric heating.

Conditions: Valid ONLY for ideal gases.

Common mistake: Trying to apply Mayer's relation to real gases or solids/liquids. Also confusing Cp - Cv = R with Cp/Cv = γ.

γ = Cp/Cv

Symbols: γ = adiabatic index (ratio of specific heats); Cp = molar heat capacity at constant pressure; Cv = molar heat capacity at constant volume

Unit: Dimensionless

When to use: Key parameter in adiabatic processes and speed of sound calculations.

Conditions: For ideal gases: monatomic γ = 5/3 ≈ 1.67; diatomic γ = 7/5 = 1.4; polyatomic (non-linear) γ = 4/3 ≈ 1.33.

Common mistake: Using wrong γ value. At high temperatures, vibrational modes activate and γ decreases. Also not confusing Cv = R/(γ-1).

PV^γ = constant (Adiabatic process)

Symbols: P = pressure; V = volume; γ = Cp/Cv

Unit: No specific units — it's a relation between P and V

When to use: Equation for a quasi-static adiabatic process (no heat exchange, Q = 0).

Conditions: Adiabatic: thermally insulated system. Quasi-static: process slow enough to maintain equilibrium. Ideal gas.

Common mistake: Applying PV^γ = const to isothermal processes (should use PV = const). Also confusing with PV^n = const where n is polytropic index (general case).

TV^(γ-1) = constant and P^(1-γ) T^γ = constant

Symbols: T = temperature; V = volume; P = pressure; γ = Cp/Cv

Unit: No specific units

When to use: Alternative forms of the adiabatic condition, useful when different pairs of variables are known.

Conditions: Same as PV^γ = const — adiabatic, quasi-static, ideal gas.

Common mistake: Memorizing only PV^γ = const and struggling when asked to find T vs V relation. Deriving from PV = nRT combined with PV^γ = const gives TV^(γ-1) = const.

W_adiabatic = (P₁V₁ - P₂V₂)/(γ - 1) = nCv(T₁ - T₂)

Symbols: W = work done by gas; P₁,V₁,T₁ = initial state; P₂,V₂,T₂ = final state; γ = Cp/Cv; Cv = molar heat capacity at constant volume

Unit: W in Joules

When to use: Work done in a quasi-static adiabatic process. Since Q = 0, ΔU = -W, so W = -nCvΔT = nCv(T₁ - T₂).

Conditions: Adiabatic (Q = 0), quasi-static, ideal gas.

Common mistake: Sign error: if gas expands adiabatically, T₂ < T₁ and W > 0 (gas does work at expense of internal energy). Using Cp instead of Cv in this formula.

W_isothermal = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = nRT ln(P₁/P₂)

Symbols: W = work done by gas; n = moles; R = gas constant; T = constant temperature; V₁,V₂ = initial and final volumes; P₁,P₂ = initial and final pressures

Unit: W in Joules

When to use: Work done in a quasi-static isothermal process of an ideal gas. Since ΔU = 0 (T constant), Q = W.

Conditions: Isothermal (T = constant), quasi-static, ideal gas.

Common mistake: Using ln(V₁/V₂) instead of ln(V₂/V₁) — sign matters for expansion vs compression. For expansion V₂ > V₁ so W > 0.

η = W/Q₁ = 1 - Q₂/Q₁ = 1 - T₂/T₁ (Carnot engine)

Symbols: η = thermal efficiency; W = work output per cycle; Q₁ = heat absorbed from hot reservoir; Q₂ = heat rejected to cold reservoir; T₁ = hot reservoir temperature; T₂ = cold reservoir temperature

Unit: η is dimensionless (ratio); T in Kelvin

When to use: Efficiency of a Carnot (reversible) heat engine — the maximum possible efficiency between two temperatures.

Conditions: Carnot cycle: two isothermal + two adiabatic processes. Reversible. T₁ and T₂ must be absolute temperatures.

Common mistake: Using Celsius instead of Kelvin for T₁ and T₂. Also misunderstanding that η < 1 always — no engine can be 100% efficient (Second Law).

COP_heating = Q₁/W = Q₁/(Q₁ - Q₂) (Heat pump / Refrigerator)

Symbols: COP = coefficient of performance; Q₁ = heat delivered to hot reservoir; Q₂ = heat extracted from cold reservoir; W = work input

Unit: COP is dimensionless

When to use: Coefficient of performance for a refrigerator or heat pump. For a Carnot refrigerator: COP = T₂/(T₁ - T₂).

Conditions: Refrigerator moves heat from cold to hot reservoir using external work.

Common mistake: Confusing COP of refrigerator with efficiency of heat engine. COP can be greater than 1 (unlike engine efficiency). For refrigerator: COP = T_cold/(T_hot - T_cold).

ΔS = Q_rev/T (Entropy change for reversible process)

Symbols: ΔS = change in entropy; Q_rev = heat transferred reversibly; T = absolute temperature at which transfer occurs

Unit: ΔS in J/K

When to use: Entropy change for a reversible isothermal process. For finite ΔT: ΔS = ∫dQ_rev/T.

Conditions: T must be in Kelvin. For irreversible processes, ΔS_system > Q/T (Clausius inequality). Total entropy of universe always increases.

Common mistake: Applying ΔS = Q/T for irreversible processes without care — this gives ΔS_surroundings but not ΔS_system. For the system, must use a reversible path between same states.

ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings ≥ 0

Symbols: ΔS_universe = total entropy change; ≥ 0 because entropy never decreases for an isolated system

Unit: ΔS in J/K

When to use: Second law of thermodynamics in entropy form. Equality holds for reversible processes, strict inequality for irreversible.

Conditions: Applies to the universe (system + surroundings). For system alone, entropy can decrease if surroundings increase more.

Common mistake: Thinking entropy of the system always increases — it can decrease (e.g., water freezing), but total entropy of universe must increase.

v_rms = √(3RT/M) = √(3k_BT/m)

Symbols: v_rms = root mean square speed; R = gas constant; T = temperature; M = molar mass; k_B = Boltzmann constant = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K; m = mass of one molecule

Unit: v_rms in m/s; M in kg/mol; m in kg

When to use: RMS speed of gas molecules — related to average kinetic energy.

Conditions: Ideal gas. Derived from ½mv_rms² = 3/2 k_BT.

Common mistake: Confusing v_rms with v_avg (mean speed) = √(8RT/πM) or v_mp (most probable speed) = √(2RT/M). Order: v_mp < v_avg < v_rms.

KE_avg per molecule = 3/2 k_BT (monatomic)

Symbols: KE_avg = average kinetic energy per molecule; k_B = Boltzmann constant; T = absolute temperature

Unit: KE in Joules

When to use: Average translational kinetic energy of a gas molecule depends only on temperature, not on the type of gas.

Conditions: Ideal gas. For diatomic at room temp: KE_total = 5/2 k_BT (translational + rotational). At high temp, vibrational modes add 2×1/2 k_BT = k_BT.

Common mistake: Using 3/2 k_BT for diatomic gases at room temperature — missing rotational contribution. The f/2 k_BT rule applies where f = degrees of freedom.

KE_avg = f/2 × k_BT (per molecule, f = degrees of freedom)

Symbols: f = number of degrees of freedom; k_B = Boltzmann constant; T = temperature

Unit: KE in Joules

When to use: Equipartition theorem: each quadratic degree of freedom contributes ½k_BT to average energy per molecule.

Conditions: Classical regime (k_BT ≫ energy level spacing). At very low T, quantum effects freeze out some degrees of freedom.

Common mistake: Counting vibrational DOF as 1 instead of 2 (kinetic + potential). For diatomic at room temp: f = 5 (3 trans + 2 rot). At high temp: f = 7 (add 2 for vibration).

f = 3 (monatomic), f = 5 (diatomic, room temp), f = 6 (diatomic, high temp), f = 6/9 (linear/non-linear polyatomic)

Symbols: f = degrees of freedom contributing to energy

Unit: Dimensionless count

When to use: Degrees of freedom for common gas molecules, needed for Cp, Cv, γ calculations.

Conditions: Room temperature for diatomic: rotation active, vibration frozen. High temperature: vibration also active.

Common mistake: Forgetting that CO₂ (linear triatomic) has f = 5 at room temp (3 trans + 2 rot) and f = 7 at high temp. Non-linear polyatomic (e.g., H₂O): f = 6 at room temp.

Cv = fR/2, Cp = (f+2)R/2, γ = (f+2)/f

Symbols: Cv = molar heat capacity at constant volume; Cp = molar heat capacity at constant pressure; f = degrees of freedom; R = gas constant

Unit: Cv, Cp in J/(mol·K); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)

When to use: Derived from equipartition theorem. Monatomic: Cv = 3R/2, Cp = 5R/2, γ = 5/3. Diatomic (room temp): Cv = 5R/2, Cp = 7R/2, γ = 7/5.

Conditions: Ideal gas. Classical equipartition valid.

Common mistake: Using γ = Cp/Cv but then plugging in wrong Cv or Cp values. Always verify: Cp - Cv = R should hold.

λ_mean_free_path = 1/(√2 π d² n)

Symbols: λ = mean free path; d = molecular diameter; n = number density = N/V

Unit: λ in meters; d in meters; n in m⁻³

When to use: Average distance a molecule travels between successive collisions in a gas.

Conditions: Hard-sphere model of molecules. Valid for dilute gases.

Common mistake: Forgetting the √2 factor (from relative velocity of molecules). Also confusing number density n = N/V with molar density.

v_avg = √(8RT/πM), v_mp = √(2RT/M)

Symbols: v_avg = average (mean) speed; v_mp = most probable speed; R = gas constant; T = temperature; M = molar mass

Unit: Speeds in m/s

When to use: Alternative speed measures from Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution. v_mp is the peak of the distribution curve.

Conditions: Ideal gas in thermal equilibrium.

Common mistake: Mixing up the three speeds: v_mp : v_avg : v_rms = √2 : √(8/π) : √3 ≈ 1 : 1.128 : 1.225.

Q = mL (Latent heat)

Symbols: Q = heat absorbed or released; m = mass; L = specific latent heat (fusion or vaporization)

Unit: Q in J; m in kg; L in J/kg

When to use: Heat required for phase change at constant temperature (melting, boiling, sublimation).

Conditions: Temperature remains constant during phase change. L_fusion for melting/freezing, L_vaporization for boiling/condensation.

Common mistake: Applying Q = mL when temperature is changing — use Q = mcΔT instead. Also confusing specific latent heat (per kg) with molar latent heat.

Q = mcΔT (Sensible heat)

Symbols: Q = heat exchanged; m = mass; c = specific heat capacity; ΔT = temperature change

Unit: Q in J; m in kg; c in J/(kg·K); ΔT in K or °C

When to use: Heat required to change temperature without phase change.

Conditions: No phase change occurs. c is approximately constant over moderate temperature ranges.

Common mistake: Not distinguishing specific heat (per kg) from molar heat capacity (per mol). For water, c = 4186 J/(kg·K) = 4.186 J/(g·K).

Thermal conductor: Q/t = kAΔT/L (Fourier's law)

Symbols: Q/t = heat current (rate of heat flow); k = thermal conductivity; A = cross-sectional area; ΔT = temperature difference; L = length/thickness

Unit: Q/t in W; k in W/(m·K); A in m²; L in m; ΔT in K

When to use: Steady-state heat conduction through a material.

Conditions: Steady state (temperature profile doesn't change with time). One-dimensional flow.

Common mistake: Forgetting that for composite rods in series, the rate Q/t is the same through each, but temperature drops add. For parallel, temperature drop is same but heat currents add.

Newton's law of cooling: dT/dt = -k(T - T_env)

Symbols: dT/dt = rate of temperature change; k = cooling constant (depends on surface, environment); T = object temperature; T_env = ambient temperature

Unit: dT/dt in K/s; T and T_env in K or °C

When to use: Rate of cooling is proportional to temperature difference with surroundings. Approximation of radiative cooling for small ΔT.

Conditions: Valid when (T - T_env) is small compared to T_env. For large ΔT, Stefan-Boltzmann law applies.

Common mistake: Applying Newton's cooling law when temperature difference is large — Stefan-Boltzmann (P ∝ T⁴ - T_env⁴) is more accurate.

P = σAT⁴ (Stefan-Boltzmann law)

Symbols: P = power radiated; σ = Stefan-Boltzmann constant = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴); A = surface area; T = absolute temperature

Unit: P in Watts; A in m²; T in K

When to use: Total power radiated by a blackbody. Net radiative power: P_net = εσA(T⁴ - T_env⁴).

Conditions: Blackbody (ε = 1). For real bodies, multiply by emissivity ε (0 ≤ ε ≤ 1).

Common mistake: Forgetting emissivity for real objects. Also not subtracting T_env⁴ for net power radiated. Power radiated ∝ T⁴, not T.

λ_max T = b = 2.898×10⁻³ m·K (Wien's displacement law)

Symbols: λ_max = wavelength at which emission is maximum; T = absolute temperature; b = Wien's displacement constant

Unit: λ_max in meters; T in K

When to use: Relates peak emission wavelength to temperature. Hotter objects emit at shorter wavelengths (bluer).

Conditions: Blackbody radiation spectrum.

Common mistake: Confusing Wien's law with Stefan-Boltzmann law. Wien's law gives λ_max; Stefan-Boltzmann gives total power.

Work in isobaric: W = PΔV; Work in isochoric: W = 0; Work in cyclic: W = area enclosed in P-V diagram

Symbols: W = work; P = pressure (constant in isobaric); ΔV = change in volume; for cyclic processes, W = ∮P dV = area inside the loop

Unit: W in Joules

When to use: Special case work formulas. Clockwise cycle → positive net work (engine); counterclockwise → negative net work (refrigerator).

Conditions: Quasi-static processes for the integral form to be valid.

Common mistake: Forgetting that work in a cyclic process equals the area enclosed — not zero just because ΔV = 0 over the full cycle. Direction of traversal matters.

v_wave = √(Y/ρ) (Longitudinal wave in solid rod)

Symbols: v = wave speed; Y = Young's modulus of the material; ρ = density

Unit: v in m/s; Y in Pa; ρ in kg/m³

When to use: Speed of longitudinal (sound) waves traveling through a solid rod.

Conditions: Solid rod where lateral dimensions are much smaller than wavelength.

Common mistake: Using this for sound in fluids — fluids use bulk modulus B: v = √(B/ρ). Solids use Young's modulus for rods, but bulk/shear moduli for bulk solids.
