K_max = hν − φ₀

Symbols: K_max = maximum kinetic energy of emitted photoelectron; h = Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s); ν = frequency of incident light; φ₀ = work function of the metal surface

Unit: K_max and φ₀ in Joules (or eV); h in J·s; ν in Hz

When to use: To find the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons ejected from a metal surface by incident electromagnetic radiation of known frequency.

Conditions: Applicable only when ν ≥ ν₀ (threshold frequency). If ν < ν₀, no emission occurs regardless of intensity.

Common mistake: Using intensity instead of frequency to determine electron energy; intensity affects current, not K_max.

eV₀ = hν − φ₀ ⟹ V₀ = (h/e)ν − φ₀/e

Symbols: V₀ = stopping (cut-off) potential in volts; e = elementary charge (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C); φ₀ = work function; ν = incident light frequency

Unit: V₀ in volts (V); φ₀ in joules or eV (if eV is used, V₀ directly equals numerical value)

When to use: To relate the stopping potential to the frequency of incident radiation; slope of V₀ vs ν graph gives h/e.

Conditions: Valid only above threshold frequency. φ₀ must be expressed in joules when dividing by e in coulombs.

Common mistake: Forgetting that V₀ is independent of light intensity; confusing work function (J) with threshold frequency (Hz).

ν₀ = φ₀/h ⟹ λ₀ = hc/φ₀

Symbols: ν₀ = threshold frequency (minimum frequency for emission); φ₀ = work function; λ₀ = threshold wavelength; h = Planck's constant; c = speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s)

Unit: ν₀ in Hz; λ₀ in meters (convert to nm: multiply by 10⁹)

When to use: To find the minimum frequency or maximum wavelength capable of ejecting electrons from a given metal.

Conditions: At threshold, K_max = 0, so hν₀ = φ₀.

Common mistake: Confusing threshold wavelength with the de Broglie wavelength; using wrong unit for φ₀ (must be in J if using SI h).

I = n_e · e · A · v_d or I_photo ∝ Intensity

Symbols: I_photo = photoelectric current; n_e = number of photoelectrons per unit volume; A = area of plate; v_d = drift velocity of electrons; Intensity = power per unit area of incident light

Unit: I in amperes (A); Intensity in W/m²

When to use: Photoelectric current is directly proportional to light intensity (number of photons), NOT to frequency. Used in solar cells, photomultiplier tubes.

Conditions: Frequency must exceed threshold for any current to flow.

Common mistake: Thinking increasing intensity increases K_max; intensity only increases the NUMBER of emitted electrons, not their energy.

E_n = −13.6 Z²/n² eV ⟹ ΔE = 13.6 Z²(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²) eV

Symbols: E_n = energy of nth orbit; Z = atomic number (Z=1 for H); n = principal quantum number (1,2,3,...); ΔE = energy absorbed/emitted during transition

Unit: E_n in eV (or convert to J: 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J)

When to use: To calculate energy levels and transition energies in hydrogen-like atoms (H, He⁺, Li²⁺).

Conditions: Bohr model valid for one-electron systems only. n ≥ 1 integer.

Common mistake: Applying E_n formula to multi-electron atoms without modification; sign error in ΔE (negative = emission, positive = absorption).

r_n = 0.529 n²/Z Å ⟹ r_n = n²a₀/Z where a₀ = 0.529 Å

Symbols: r_n = radius of nth orbit; a₀ = Bohr radius (0.529 × 10⁻¹⁰ m); n = principal quantum number; Z = atomic number

Unit: r_n in angstrom (Å) or meters (multiply by 10⁻¹⁰)

When to use: To find the radius of an electron's orbit in hydrogen-like atoms; radius scales as n²/Z.

Conditions: Valid only for one-electron atoms under Bohr model assumptions.

Common mistake: Forgetting Z in denominator for ions; using n² instead of n²/Z dependence.

v_n = 2.188 × 10⁶ Z/n m/s ⟹ v_n = (Z/n) · c/137

Symbols: v_n = orbital speed of electron in nth orbit; Z = atomic number; n = principal quantum number; c = speed of light

Unit: v_n in m/s

When to use: To compute electron velocity in hydrogen-like atoms; v ∝ Z/n.

Conditions: Non-relativistic approximation; valid when v ≪ c.

Common mistake: Using classical Coulomb formula without quantization; forgetting Z dependency for ions.

p_n = mv_n = nh/(2πr_n) ⟹ mvr = nh/(2π) (Bohr's quantisation condition)

Symbols: p = linear momentum; m = electron mass (9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg); v = orbital speed; n = quantum number; h = Planck's constant; r = orbit radius

Unit: Left side: kg·m/s; Right side: J·s = kg·m²/s

When to use: Fundamental quantisation postulate — angular momentum is an integral multiple of h/2π. Used to derive all other Bohr results.

Conditions: Only applies to stationary states; not valid during transitions.

Common mistake: Writing mvr = nh instead of nh/(2π); confusing linear and angular momentum.

E_total = −KE = ½ PE ⟹ KE = |E_n| PE = 2E_n

Symbols: E_total = total energy (negative); KE = kinetic energy (always positive); PE = potential energy (negative, twice total energy)

Unit: All in eV or J

When to use: Virial theorem relations in Coulomb field: KE = −E_total, PE = 2E_total. Quick way to find KE and PE from energy level.

Conditions: True for any 1/r potential (Coulomb).

Common mistake: Taking PE as positive; forgetting that total energy is negative.

1/λ = R_H Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) (Rydberg formula, n₂ > n₁)

Symbols: λ = wavelength of emitted/absorbed photon; R_H = Rydberg constant (1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹); Z = atomic number; n₁, n₂ = lower and upper principal quantum numbers

Unit: λ in meters (or convert to nm by × 10⁹); R_H in m⁻¹

When to use: To find wavelengths of spectral lines in hydrogen-like atoms; specific series: Lyman(n₁=1), Balmer(n₁=2), Paschen(n₁=3), Brackett(n₁=4), Pfund(n₁=5).

Conditions: n₁ and n₂ are integers, n₂ > n₁. For emission, electron drops from n₂ to n₁.

Common mistake: Reversing n₁ and n₂ (gives negative wavelength); applying to multi-electron atoms directly.

λ_photon = hc/ΔE = 1240 eV·nm / ΔE(eV)

Symbols: λ = wavelength of photon emitted/absorbed; h = Planck's constant; c = speed of light; ΔE = energy difference between two levels

Unit: λ in nm when ΔE is in eV (using hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm shortcut)

When to use: Quick conversion between energy difference and photon wavelength; extremely useful in JEE problems.

Conditions: Energy must be in eV for the 1240 shortcut; otherwise use hc = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸ J·m.

Common mistake: Using 1240 without converting eV first; forgetting it gives nm not m.

Larmor formula (classical): P = μ₀q²a²/(6πc)

Symbols: P = power radiated; μ₀ = permeability of free space; q = charge; a = acceleration; c = speed of light

Unit: P in watts (W)

When to use: Classical prediction that led to Bohr model — an accelerating electron should continuously radiate, but Bohr postulated discrete orbits with NO radiation.

Conditions: Classical electrodynamics result; not applicable to quantised orbits.

Common mistake: Using this for Bohr orbits — it contradicts Bohr's second postulate.

λ_dB = h/p = h/(mv) = h/√(2mK) (for a charged particle accelerated through V: λ = h/√(2mqV))

Symbols: λ_dB = de Broglie wavelength; h = Planck's constant; p = momentum; m = mass; v = velocity; K = kinetic energy; q = charge; V = accelerating potential

Unit: λ in meters; use h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s; m in kg; V in volts

When to use: To find the wave nature associated with matter particles — electrons, protons, α-particles, etc.

Conditions: Non-relativistic form. For relativistic particles, use λ = h/(γmv).

Common mistake: Using λ = h/mv when the particle is accelerated through potential V without substituting v = √(2qV/m); forgetting that λ depends on both mass and charge for same V.

λ = h/√(2mK) = hc/√(2mc²·K) (relativistic correction when K comparable to mc²)

Symbols: λ = de Broglie wavelength; h = Planck's constant; m = rest mass; K = kinetic energy; c = speed of light; mc² = rest energy

Unit: Use consistent units: if mc² in eV and K in eV, hc in eV·m

When to use: When particle kinetic energy is a significant fraction of its rest mass energy (e.g., high-energy electrons), use relativistic de Broglie wavelength.

Conditions: Valid when K ≳ 0.01 mc². For K ≪ mc², use non-relativistic form.

Common mistake: Applying non-relativistic formula when K is large (gives wrong answer by ~10% or more).

λ = 1.227/√V nm (for electron, V in volts)

Symbols: λ = de Broglie wavelength of electron; V = accelerating potential in volts

Unit: λ in nanometers (nm)

When to use: Quick formula for electron wavelength accelerated through potential V — very commonly used in JEE.

Conditions: Non-relativistic approximation; valid for V up to ~10⁴ V.

Common mistake: Using this formula for protons or α-particles — they have different masses, so λ differs by √(m_p/m_e) factor.

K.E._photon = hν = hc/λ = hc/λ_limit (for bremsstrahlung X-ray cutoff)

Symbols: K.E._photon = energy of emitted X-ray photon; h = Planck's constant; ν = frequency; λ = wavelength; λ_limit = minimum (cutoff) wavelength

Unit: K.E. in eV or J; λ in m or nm

When to use: In X-ray tubes, maximum photon energy occurs when ALL kinetic energy of incident electron is converted to a single photon: eV = hc/λ_min.

Conditions: λ_min corresponds to the most energetic photon possible.

Common mistake: Confusing λ_min with characteristic X-ray wavelengths; thinking λ_min depends on target material (it does NOT, only on V).

λ_Kα = 4/(3R_H(Z−1)²) (Moseley's law for Kα line)

Symbols: λ_Kα = wavelength of Kα X-ray characteristic line; R_H = Rydberg constant; Z = atomic number of target material

Unit: λ in meters (after substituting R_H in m⁻¹)

When to use: To find the wavelength of characteristic Kα X-ray for an element of atomic number Z. The (Z−1) accounts for shielding by the remaining K-shell electron.

Conditions: Empirical formula; valid for Kα (transition n=2 → n=1). Different shielding constants for other lines.

Common mistake: Using Z instead of (Z−1); applying to L-series without adjusting shielding constant.

N = N₀ e^(−λt) ⟹ t_{1/2} = ln 2/λ ≈ 0.693/λ ⟹ τ = 1/λ

Symbols: N = number of nuclei remaining at time t; N₀ = initial number; λ = decay constant; t_{1/2} = half-life; τ = mean lifetime

Unit: λ in s⁻¹; t, t_{1/2}, τ in seconds (or consistent time unit)

When to use: Law of radioactive decay — to find remaining nuclei, activity, or time elapsed given any two quantities.

Conditions: Decay constant λ is characteristic of the nuclide; independent of physical/chemical conditions.

Common mistake: Using N = N₀(1/2)^t without ensuring t is in units of half-lives; confusing mean life τ with half-life.

A = λN = A₀ e^(−λt) ⟹ A = A₀(1/2)^(t/t_{1/2})

Symbols: A = activity at time t (decays/s = Bq); A₀ = initial activity; λ = decay constant; N = number of nuclei; t_{1/2} = half-life

Unit: A in becquerel (Bq) or curie (Ci); 1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq

When to use: Activity is the rate of disintegration; decays exponentially with same time constant as number of nuclei.

Conditions: Same exponential law as N(t); A₀ = λN₀.

Common mistake: Using different half-lives for N and A — they are the SAME because A ∝ N.

Average life τ = t_{1/2}/ln 2 ≈ 1.443 t_{1/2}

Symbols: τ = mean lifetime (average life); t_{1/2} = half-life

Unit: Same time unit as t_{1/2}

When to use: To convert between half-life and average life; mean life is the time after which N = N₀/e.

Conditions: Always true for exponential decay.

Common mistake: Confusing mean life with half-life; using τ = t_{1/2}.

Number of daughter nuclei: N_D = N₀(1 − e^(−λt))

Symbols: N_D = number of daughter nuclei formed; N₀ = initial parent nuclei; λ = decay constant; t = time elapsed

Unit: Same as N₀

When to use: When a parent nuclide decays to a stable daughter, this gives the accumulated daughter count at time t.

Conditions: Daughter is assumed stable. If daughter is also radioactive, use Bateman equations.

Common mistake: Forgetting that N_parent + N_daughter = N₀ only if daughter is stable.

Binding Energy BE = [Z·m_p + (A−Z)·m_n − M_nuc]·c² = Δm · c²

Symbols: BE = binding energy of nucleus; Z = atomic number; m_p = proton mass (1.007276 u); A = mass number; m_n = neutron mass (1.008665 u); M_nuc = actual nuclear mass; Δm = mass defect

Unit: BE in MeV when masses in atomic mass units (u); 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c²

When to use: To find how tightly nucleons are bound; BE/A curve determines energy release in fusion and fission.

Conditions: Use nuclear mass, not atomic mass. If given atomic mass M_atom, subtract Z·m_e: M_nuc = M_atom − Z·m_e.

Common mistake: Using atomic mass directly without subtracting electron masses; forgetting 1 u = 931.5 MeV.

BE/A vs A curve: peaks at Fe-56 (≈ 8.8 MeV/nucleon)

Symbols: BE/A = binding energy per nucleon; A = mass number; Fe-56 has maximum BE/A ≈ 8.79 MeV

Unit: MeV per nucleon

When to use: Fusion releases energy when light nuclei combine (A < 56, BE/A increases). Fission releases energy when heavy nuclei split (A > 56, BE/A increases). Iron-56 is most stable.

Conditions: General trend; exact values vary slightly.

Common mistake: Thinking all fusion/fission releases energy — only fusion for A<56 and fission for A>56.

α-decay: ^A_Z X → ^{A−4}_{Z−2}Y + ^4_2He + Q where Q = [M_X − M_Y − M_He]c²

Symbols: Q = disintegration energy (released as kinetic energy of products); M_X, M_Y, M_He = nuclear masses

Unit: Q in MeV

When to use: Alpha emission reduces A by 4 and Z by 2. Q-value determines if decay is energetically possible (Q > 0).

Conditions: Q must be positive for spontaneous decay. α-particle carries away most kinetic energy (≈ Q·(A−4)/A).

Common mistake: Forgetting kinetic energy sharing: T_α ≈ Q·(A−4)/A, T_recoil ≈ Q·4/A.

β⁻-decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄_e ⟹ ^A_Z X → ^A_{Z+1}Y + e⁻ + ν̄_e + Q

Symbols: ν̄_e = antineutrino; Q = disintegration energy shared among e⁻, ν̄_e, and recoil nucleus; continuous energy spectrum for electron

Unit: Q in MeV

When to use: Beta-minus decay converts a neutron to a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. Electron energy is NOT fixed — shared with neutrino.

Conditions: Q > 0 required. Max electron energy E_max ≈ Q (when neutrino gets negligible energy).

Common mistake: Thinking β-decay emits electrons from the orbitals; it comes from the nucleus. Forgetting the antineutrino.

β⁺-decay: p → n + e⁺ + ν_e ⟹ ^A_Z X → ^A_{Z−1}Y + e⁺ + ν_e + Q

Symbols: ν_e = neutrino; e⁺ = positron; Q = disintegration energy

Unit: Q in MeV; mass condition: M_parent > M_daughter + 2m_e

When to use: Positron emission converts a proton to a neutron. Requires parent-daughter mass difference > 2m_e c² ≈ 1.022 MeV.

Conditions: M(X) − M(Y) > 2m_e for β⁺ decay to occur (unlike β⁻ which only needs M(X) > M(Y)).

Common mistake: Using same mass condition as β⁻; forgetting the 2m_e requirement for positron emission.

Q-value general: Q = (Σm_reactants − Σm_products) c²

Symbols: Q = energy released (positive = exothermic/exoergic); Σm = sum of rest masses before and after reaction

Unit: Q in MeV if masses in u (1 u = 931.5 MeV)

When to use: To determine if a nuclear reaction is energetically possible (Q > 0) and how much energy is released.

Conditions: Use nuclear masses or atomic masses consistently (accounting for electrons).

Common mistake: Sign error: Q = (initial − final)c², not (final − initial).

Neutron-to-proton ratio stability: N/Z ≈ 1 for light nuclei (A < 20), N/Z ≈ 1.5 for heavy nuclei (A ≈ 238)

Symbols: N = number of neutrons; Z = number of protons; A = N + Z

Unit: Dimensionless ratio

When to use: To predict whether a nuclide is stable or what decay mode it undergoes. Neutron-rich → β⁻; proton-rich → β⁺/EC.

Conditions: Valley of stability curve; deviations cause radioactive decay.

Common mistake: Assuming N=Z always stable; heavy nuclei NEED extra neutrons for stability.

E = γmc² where γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)

Symbols: E = total relativistic energy; m = rest mass; v = particle speed; c = speed of light; γ = Lorentz factor

Unit: E in J or eV

When to use: Total energy of a relativistic particle; includes rest mass energy mc² and kinetic energy.

Conditions: v can be any value up to c. At v=0, E = mc² (rest energy).

Common mistake: Forgetting rest energy; using E = ½mv² at high speeds.

K = (γ − 1)mc² ⟹ total E = K + mc²

Symbols: K = relativistic kinetic energy; γ = Lorentz factor; mc² = rest energy

Unit: K and mc² in same energy units (eV, J)

When to use: Relativistic KE differs from classical ½mv² when v is comparable to c. Important for high-energy particle physics.

Conditions: At low v (v≪c), K ≈ ½mv² (recovered from binomial expansion of γ).

Common mistake: Using ½mv² for relativistic particles; confusing total energy with kinetic energy.

E² = (pc)² + (m₀c²)²

Symbols: E = total energy; p = relativistic momentum; m₀ = rest mass; c = speed of light

Unit: E and pc in same energy units

When to use: Energy-momentum relation valid for both massive and massless particles. For photons (m₀=0): E = pc.

Conditions: Universal relation; applies to all particles.

Common mistake: Applying classical p = mv for relativistic speeds; forgetting it works for photons too.

Photon momentum: p = E/c = h/λ = hν/c

Symbols: p = photon momentum; E = photon energy; h = Planck's constant; λ = wavelength; ν = frequency

Unit: p in kg·m/s or eV/c

When to use: Photons carry momentum despite having zero rest mass. Explains Compton scattering and radiation pressure.

Conditions: Valid for all electromagnetic radiation (photons).

Common mistake: Thinking photons have no momentum because they have no mass.

Compton shift: Δλ = λ' − λ = (h/m_e c)(1 − cos θ) = λ_C(1 − cos θ)

Symbols: λ' = scattered photon wavelength; λ = incident photon wavelength; θ = scattering angle of photon; λ_C = Compton wavelength of electron = h/(m_e c) ≈ 2.426 × 10⁻¹² m = 0.02426 Å

Unit: Δλ and λ_C in meters or angstroms

When to use: To find wavelength change when X-ray/gamma photon scatters off a free electron. Maximum shift at θ = 180° (backscattering): Δλ_max = 2λ_C.

Conditions: Electron initially at rest and effectively free (binding energy ≪ photon energy).

Common mistake: Thinking Δλ depends on target material — it depends only on scattering angle θ.

Photon energy after Compton scattering: E' = E/[1 + (E/m_e c²)(1 − cos θ)]

Symbols: E' = energy of scattered photon; E = incident photon energy; m_e c² = electron rest energy = 0.511 MeV; θ = scattering angle

Unit: E and E' in same energy units (eV, keV, MeV)

When to use: Direct calculation of scattered photon energy without finding λ first. Useful for high-energy photons.

Conditions: Same as Compton shift formula.

Common mistake: Using non-relativistic energy relations for the scattered photon.

Pair production: γ → e⁺ + e⁻ (requires nucleus nearby for momentum conservation; E_γ ≥ 2m_e c² = 1.022 MeV)

Symbols: E_γ = photon energy; m_e c² = electron rest energy = 0.511 MeV; 2m_e c² = threshold energy

Unit: E_γ in MeV

When to use: High-energy photon converts into electron-positron pair near a nucleus. Threshold: photon energy must be at least 1.022 MeV.

Conditions: Cannot occur in free space (conservation of momentum requires a third body like a nucleus). Excess energy becomes KE of e⁺ and e⁻.

Common mistake: Forgetting the 1.022 MeV threshold; thinking pair production occurs without a nucleus.

Photodisintegration (γ-process): γ + nucleus → nucleus' + nucleon(s)

Symbols: γ = high-energy photon; threshold energy = binding energy of the removed nucleon

Unit: Threshold in MeV

When to use: Very high-energy photon can knock out a nucleon from a nucleus (e.g., γ + ²H → p + n). Threshold ≈ binding energy of deuteron ≈ 2.2 MeV.

Conditions: Photon energy must exceed the binding energy of the nucleon.

Common mistake: Using binding energy of whole nucleus instead of the specific nucleon being removed.

Nuclear radius: R = R₀ A^{1/3} where R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m

Symbols: R = nuclear radius; A = mass number; R₀ = empirical constant (~1.2 fm)

Unit: R in femtometers (fm) or meters

When to use: To estimate nuclear size; nuclear density is approximately constant for all nuclei.

Conditions: Approximate formula; holds well for medium and heavy nuclei.

Common mistake: Using R = R₀A instead of R₀A^{1/3}; confusing nuclear radius with atomic radius.

Nuclear density: ρ ≈ constant ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³

Symbols: ρ = nuclear density; M_nuc ≈ A·m_n; V = (4/3)πR³ = (4/3)π(R₀A^{1/3})³

Unit: kg/m³

When to use: Nuclear density is nearly independent of A — all nuclei have roughly the same density. Important concept for JEE.

Conditions: Derived assuming uniform sphere and R = R₀A^{1/3}.

Common mistake: Thinking larger nuclei are denser; density is essentially the same for all nuclei.

Secular equilibrium: λ₁N₁ = λ₂N₂ ⟹ A₁ = A₂

Symbols: λ₁, N₁ = decay constant and number of parent; λ₂, N₂ = decay constant and number of daughter; A = activity

Unit: Activities in same units (Bq, Ci)

When to use: When parent half-life ≫ daughter half-life, after sufficient time both decay at same rate (activities equal).

Conditions: Parent half-life must be much longer than daughter half-life.

Common mistake: Applying to transient equilibrium where t_{1/2,parent} is only slightly longer.

Transient equilibrium: N₂/N₁ = λ₁/(λ₂ − λ₁) when t_{1/2,parent} > t_{1/2,daughter}

Symbols: N₁, N₂ = parent and daughter populations; λ₁, λ₂ = decay constants

Unit: Ratio is dimensionless

When to use: When parent half-life is moderately longer than daughter, daughter activity exceeds parent temporarily before both decay together.

Conditions: λ₂ > λ₁ (daughter decays faster).

Common mistake: Using secular equilibrium formula when half-lives are comparable.

Effective half-life (biological + physical): 1/T_eff = 1/T_physical + 1/T_biological

Symbols: T_eff = effective half-life; T_physical = physical half-life; T_biological = biological half-life

Unit: Time in same units

When to use: In medical physics/radiotherapy, radioactive material is also eliminated from body biologically.

Conditions: Both processes are exponential.

Common mistake: Adding half-lives directly instead of reciprocals.

Photoelectric effect yield (quantum efficiency): η = N_e/N_photons

Symbols: η = quantum efficiency (fraction of photons that eject an electron); N_e = number of photoelectrons; N_photons = number of incident photons

Unit: Dimensionless (0 to 1 or percentage)

When to use: Not all incident photons eject electrons; η depends on material and photon energy.

Conditions: Typically η < 1 even above threshold.

Common mistake: Assuming every photon above threshold ejects one electron.

Photon flux: Φ = I/(hν) (photons per unit area per second)

Symbols: Φ = photon flux; I = intensity (power per unit area); hν = energy per photon

Unit: Φ in photons·m⁻²·s⁻¹

When to use: To find number of photons arriving per second per unit area from a light source of known intensity.

Conditions: Monochromatic source assumed.

Common mistake: Using total power instead of intensity (power/area).

de Broglie hypothesis: λ = h/p (universal wave-particle duality)

Symbols: λ = wavelength associated with any moving particle; h = Planck's constant; p = momentum of particle

Unit: λ in meters; p in kg·m/s

When to use: Foundation of quantum mechanics; all matter has wave properties. Verified by Davisson-Germer experiment (electron diffraction).

Conditions: Applicable to all matter; most noticeable for microscopic particles (electrons, neutrons).

Common mistake: Thinking only light has wave-particle duality; matter waves apply to ALL particles.

Davisson-Germer verification: constructive interference 2d sinθ = nλ (Bragg's law for crystal diffraction)

Symbols: d = interplanar spacing in nickel crystal; θ = glancing angle; n = order of diffraction; λ = de Broglie wavelength

Unit: d and λ in meters; θ in degrees or radians

When to use: Experimental confirmation of de Broglie hypothesis: electrons diffracted by crystal planes produce interference pattern matching Bragg's law.

Conditions: Crystal acts as diffraction grating for matter waves.

Common mistake: Confusing Bragg angle definition; using 2d sinθ = nλ for normal incidence instead of glancing angle.

Uncertainty principle: Δx · Δp ≥ ℏ/2 (ℏ = h/2π)

Symbols: Δx = uncertainty in position; Δp = uncertainty in momentum; ℏ = reduced Planck's constant = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s

Unit: Δx in m; Δp in kg·m/s; product in J·s

When to use: Fundamental limit on simultaneous measurement precision; explains why electrons don't spiral into nucleus.

Conditions: Minimum uncertainty state achieves equality; general case uses ≥.

Common mistake: Writing Δx·Δp ≥ h instead of ℏ/2; confusing with energy-time uncertainty.

Energy-time uncertainty: ΔE · Δt ≥ ℏ/2

Symbols: ΔE = uncertainty in energy; Δt = uncertainty in time (lifetime of state); ℏ = h/2π

Unit: ΔE in J or eV; Δt in s

When to use: Short-lived excited states have energy uncertainty (natural line width); virtual particles can exist for Δt ≈ ℏ/ΔE.

Conditions: Δt is the lifetime of the state, not measurement time.

Common mistake: Applying as if ΔE and Δt are measurement errors rather than intrinsic quantum uncertainty.

Nuclear binding energy from semi-empirical formula (Weizsäcker): BE = a_v A − a_s A^{2/3} − a_c Z²/A^{1/3} − a_a (A−2Z)²/A ± δ(A,Z)

Symbols: a_v ≈ 15.5 MeV (volume); a_s ≈ 16.8 MeV (surface); a_c ≈ 0.72 MeV (Coulomb); a_a ≈ 23 MeV (asymmetry); δ = pairing term (+11/A^{1/2} even-even, −11/A^{1/2} odd-odd, 0 odd-A)

Unit: BE in MeV

When to use: To estimate binding energy of a nucleus when experimental mass data is unavailable. Explains nuclear stability trends.

Conditions: Empirical formula; good approximation for medium and heavy nuclei.

Common mistake: Forgetting the pairing term δ; wrong sign for even-even vs odd-odd nuclei.
