1. Simple Harmonic Motion and Wave Fundamentals

• Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) is the baseline for all wave analysis. A particle executes SHM when its acceleration is proportional to displacement from mean position and directed opposite: a = −ω²x. The displacement can be written as x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), velocity v(t) = −Aω sin(ωt + φ), acceleration a(t) = −Aω² cos(ωt + φ). Energy in SHM is conserved: total E = ½kA², where k = mω². Kinetic energy K = ½mω²(A² − x²), potential energy U = ½kx².

• A progressive (traveling) wave is described by y(x,t) = A cos(kx − ωt + φ) or equivalently y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ). Here k = 2π/λ is the angular wave number, ω = 2πν = 2π/T is angular frequency, v = ω/k = λν is the wave speed. The phase velocity relates medium properties: for a stretched string v = √(T/μ), where T is tension and μ is linear mass density; for sound in a gas v = √(γP/ρ) = √(γRT/M).

• Transverse waves displace particles perpendicular to propagation; longitudinal waves displace parallel. Sound in air is longitudinal. In solids both transverse and longitudinal waves can propagate; in fluids only longitudinal. Wave intensity I = ½ρvω²A² for a plane wave, and power transmitted P = I × area.

• The principle of superposition states that net displacement at any point equals the vector sum of individual displacements. This underlies interference, beats, and standing waves. Boundary conditions determine whether reflections involve phase reversal: fixed end → inverted reflection (phase change π), free end → non-inverted reflection.

2. Standing Waves and Resonance in Strings and Pipes

• Standing waves arise from superposition of two identical progressive waves traveling in opposite directions. For a string fixed at both ends, allowable wavelengths satisfy L = nλ/2, so λₙ = 2L/n, n = 1,2,3... Frequencies are νₙ = nv/(2L) = n√(T/μ)/(2L). The fundamental (n=1) is the first harmonic; higher modes are overtones.

• Boundary nodes and antinodes: at a fixed end displacement is always zero (node); at a free end displacement is maximum (antinode). For strings both ends fixed → nodes at ends. For open organ pipe both ends open → antinodes at ends. For closed organ pipe one end closed → node at closed end, antinode near open end (end correction e ≈ 0.6r applies).

• Closed pipe resonances: only odd harmonics exist. Length condition L = (2n−1)λ/4, n = 1,2,3..., so νₙ = (2n−1)v/(4L). Open pipe: all integer harmonics νₙ = nv/(2L).

• End correction matters in precision problems. Effective length L_eff = L + 0.6r for each open end. For a closed pipe L_eff = L + 0.6r. Neglecting end correction gives noticeably wrong frequencies for narrow pipes.

• Resonance occurs when driving frequency matches a natural frequency of the system. At resonance amplitude grows large; energy transfer is maximal. In Kundt's tube experiments, resonance length changes reveal ratio of sound speeds in different gases.

3. Beats, Doppler Effect, and Sound Intensity Level

• Beats occur when two sources of slightly different frequencies ν₁ and ν₂ superpose. Resultant intensity varies periodically with beat frequency ν_beat = |ν₁ − ν₂|. Displacement equation: y = 2A cos(2π((ν₁−ν₂)/2)t) · sin(2π((ν₁+ν₂)/2)t). Envelope modulates at half the difference, intensity peaks twice per envelope cycle, giving beat frequency equal to frequency difference.

• Doppler effect shifts observed frequency when source and observer move relative to medium. General form: ν' = ν (v ± v_o)/(v ∓ v_s), where signs depend on direction convention. Positive v_o when observer moves toward source; positive v_s when source moves toward observer. Careful sign choice is critical: if both approach, numerator increases and denominator decreases, maximizing shift.

• Moving source toward stationary observer: ν' = ν·v/(v−v_s). Moving source away: ν' = ν·v/(v+v_s). If v_s approaches v, denominator → 0, frequency diverges (shock wave / sonic boom regime).

• Sound intensity level in decibels: β = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀), where I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² is threshold of hearing. Doubling intensity adds ≈3 dB; tenfold increase adds 10 dB. Intensity follows inverse-square law for point source: I = P/(4πr²).

• Common trap: mixing up signs in Doppler formula. Remember rule-of-thumb: approaching increases apparent frequency, receding decreases it. Always check whether velocities are with respect to the medium (air), not each other.

4. Kinetic Theory of Gases

• Ideal gas law: PV = nRT = Nk_BT, where N is molecule count, k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K. Mean translational kinetic energy per molecule: ⟨KE⟩ = 3/2 k_BT. This depends only on temperature, not on gas identity.

• Root-mean-square speed: v_rms = √(3k_BT/m) = √(3RT/M), where M is molar mass. Most probable speed v_p = √(2k_BT/m) = √(2RT/M). Average speed v_avg = √(8k_BT/πm) = √(8RT/πM). Ordering: v_p < v_avg < v_rms.

• Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution describes speed spread. Graph shifts right and flattens with increasing T. Number of molecules between v and v+dv: dN/dv = 4πN(m/2πk_BT)^(3/2) v² exp(−mv²/2k_BT).

• Degrees of freedom f: monoatomic = 3 (translation only), diatomic at room temp = 5 (3 trans + 2 rot), polyatomic non-linear = 6. At high T vibrational modes activate, adding 2 per vibrational mode.

• Equipartition theorem: each quadratic degree of freedom contributes ½k_BT per molecule, or ½R per mole. Internal energy U = f/2 nRT. Molar heat capacities: C_V = f/2 R, C_P = (f/2 + 1)R, γ = C_P/C_V = 1 + 2/f.

5. Thermodynamic Processes and Work Calculations

• Isothermal process (T constant): ΔU = 0 for ideal gas, so Q = W. For reversible expansion/compression from V₁ to V₂: W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = nRT ln(P₁/P₂). On PV diagram curve is hyperbola PV = constant.

• Adiabatic process (Q = 0): PV^γ = constant, TV^(γ−1) = constant, P^(1−γ)T^γ = constant. Work done W = (P₁V₁ − P₂V₂)/(γ−1) = nR(T₁−T₂)/(γ−1). For irreversible adiabatic against constant external pressure, use ΔU = −W_ext with appropriate work expression.

• Isobaric (P constant): W = PΔV = nRΔT, Q = nC_PΔT, ΔU = nC_VΔT.

• Isochoric (V constant): W = 0, Q = ΔU = nC_VΔT.

• Cyclic process: net ΔU = 0, net Q = net W. Area enclosed in PV diagram equals work magnitude. Clockwise cycle → engine (positive work output); counter-clockwise → refrigerator/heat pump.

• Sign conventions vary by textbook. JEE Advanced typically uses physics convention: work done BY gas is positive, work done ON gas is negative. Internal energy change ΔU = Q − W (where W is work by system). Verify context in question.

6. First Law of Thermodynamics and Heat Engines

• First law: ΔU = Q − W (physics sign convention). Energy conservation for thermodynamic systems. U is state function; Q and W are path functions.

• Heat engine efficiency: η = W/Q_H = 1 − Q_C/Q_H = 1 − T_C/T_H for Carnot engine operating between reservoirs at temperatures T_H (hot) and T_C (cold). Temperatures must be absolute (Kelvin).

• Carnot engine is ideal reversible engine. No real engine can exceed Carnot efficiency between same two temperatures. Efficiency depends only on reservoir temperatures, not working substance.

• Coefficient of performance (COP): for refrigerator COP_R = Q_C/W = T_C/(T_H − T_C); for heat pump COP_HP = Q_H/W = T_H/(T_H − T_C).

• Real engines have irreversibilities (friction, turbulence, finite temperature differences). Entropy production ensures actual efficiency < Carnot limit.

• Combined cycles (Carnot + practical) appear in advanced problems. For multi-stage engines, compute overall efficiency as product of individual stage efficiencies only when heat rejection of one becomes heat input of next appropriately.

7. Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy

• Second law statements: Clausius — heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold to hot body without external work. Kelvin-Planck — no cyclic process can convert all absorbed heat into work.

• Entropy S is a state function defined by dS = dQ_rev/T. For finite reversible process: ΔS = ∫dQ_rev/T. For irreversible process, ΔS_universe > 0.

• Entropy change calculations:
– Isothermal reversible: ΔS = Q_rev/T = nR ln(V₂/V₁).
– Isochoric: ΔS = nC_V ln(T₂/T₁).
– Isobaric: ΔS = nC_P ln(T₂/T₁).
– Adiabatic reversible: ΔS = 0 (isentropic).
– Free expansion of ideal gas: ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) despite Q = 0, because entropy is state function and we compute via hypothetical reversible path.

• Entropy of universe increases in irreversible processes. In isolated system, entropy never decreases. This gives directionality to natural processes.

• Heat death concept: if universe reaches maximum entropy, no more useful work can be extracted. Theoretical extrapolation, not practical concern.

• Gibbs and Helmholtz free energies link entropy to work availability: A = U − TS (Helmholtz), G = H − TS (Gibbs). At constant T,V, spontaneous processes minimize A; at constant T,P, minimize G.

8. Blackbody Radiation and Thermal Physics Extensions

• Blackbody absorbs all incident radiation. Emitted spectrum depends only on temperature, not material. Total radiated power per unit area: M = σT⁴ (Stefan-Boltzmann law), σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴.

• Peak wavelength follows Wien's displacement law: λ_max T = b, b ≈ 2.898×10⁻³ m·K. Hotter objects emit more at shorter wavelengths.

• Planck's law gives spectral radiance: B(λ,T) = (2hc²/λ⁵)·1/(exp(hc/λk_BT) − 1). Reduces to Rayleigh-Jeans at long λ, Wien at short λ.

• Emissivity ε = actual emitted intensity / blackbody intensity at same T. Real bodies: 0 < ε ≤ 1. Good absorbers are good emitters (Kirchhoff's law).

• Newton's law of cooling: dT/dt = −k(T − T_env) for small temperature differences. Solves to exponential decay toward ambient temperature. For large ΔT, use Stefan-Boltzmann based cooling rate.

• JEE Advanced occasionally combines radiation with thermodynamic cycles or wave concepts. Practice problems involving entropy change of radiation fields, photon gas pressure P = u/3, and energy density u = aT⁴.

9. Coupled Oscillations and Advanced Wave Topics

• Two coupled pendulums or masses on springs exhibit normal modes. System has two normal frequencies. In mode 1 (in-phase), coupling spring unstretched; in mode 2 (out-of-phase), coupling active. Energy transfers between oscillators at beat frequency if initially excited in one mode.

• Wave equation in 2D and 3D: solutions include circular/spherical waves. Intensity falls as 1/r² for spherical waves due to geometry. Pressure amplitude relates to displacement amplitude via bulk modulus.

• Acoustics of musical instruments: harmonics, timbre, overtones. Real strings have stiffness causing slight inharmonicity. Wind instruments depend on temperature for tuning (v ∝ √T).

• Seismic waves: P-waves (longitudinal, faster) and S-waves (transverse, slower). Shadow zones reveal Earth's liquid outer core (S-waves cannot propagate through liquids).

• Ultrasonics and medical imaging: frequency ranges MHz–GHz. Resolution ∝ λ. Higher frequency → better resolution but less penetration due to absorption.

• Nonlinear acoustics and shock waves: at very high amplitudes, wave profile steepens. Mach cone angle sin θ = v_sound/v_object.

10. Problem-Solving Frameworks and Common JEE Advanced Patterns

• Identify the subsystem: string/pipes for standing waves, gas processes for thermodynamics, coupled combination for advanced problems. Draw clear diagrams showing boundaries, nodes, antinodes, PV paths.

• Dimensional analysis checks: verify final answers have correct units. Wave speed [LT⁻¹], frequency [T⁻¹], intensity [MT⁻³], entropy [ML²T⁻²K⁻¹].

• Graph interpretation: PV diagrams → work from area; PT diagrams → use ideal gas relation to infer volume changes; TS diagrams → heat from area under curve.

• Limiting cases validate results: infinite mass ratio, zero tension, high/low temperature limits. If formula reduces to known result in limiting case, likely correct.

• Multi-step chaining: wave speed determines string frequency; frequency determines Doppler shift; Doppler shift determines beat frequency. Track variables carefully across steps.

• Sign discipline: maintain consistent sign convention throughout problem. Re-check after each algebraic manipulation. JEE Advanced questions often test sign-sensitive scenarios like source moving faster than sound.

Important facts

  • Wave speed on string v = √(T/μ) depends on tension and linear density, not amplitude or frequency.
  • Standing wave condition for string fixed at both ends: L = nλ/2, fundamental frequency ν₁ = v/(2L).
  • Closed pipe supports only odd harmonics: ν_n = (2n−1)v/(4L). End correction ≈ 0.6r per open end.
  • Beat frequency equals absolute difference of two source frequencies: ν_beat = |ν₁ − ν₂|.
  • Doppler formula sign convention: observer toward source → numerator (+v_o); source toward observer → denominator (−v_s).
  • Ideal gas rms speed v_rms = √(3RT/M); most probable speed v_p = √(2RT/M); average speed v_avg = √(8RT/πM).
  • Equipartition theorem: each quadratic degree of freedom contributes ½k_BT per molecule; monatomic f=3, diatomic f=5 at room temp.
  • Adiabatic process: PV^γ = constant, γ = C_P/C_V. Work W = (P₁V₁ − P₂V₂)/(γ−1).
  • Carnot efficiency η = 1 − T_C/T_H depends only on reservoir temperatures.
  • Entropy change for free expansion of ideal gas: ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁), even though Q = 0.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann law: radiated power per unit area M = σT⁴, σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴.
  • Second law: entropy of isolated system never decreases; irreversible processes increase universal entropy.

Common mistakes

  • Using wrong sign in Doppler formula leads to incorrect frequency shift direction. Always verify: approaching → higher pitch, receding → lower pitch.
  • Neglecting end correction in organ pipe problems causes systematic error in calculated resonant frequencies, especially for narrow pipes.
  • Assuming adiabatic process means T constant. Adiabatic means Q = 0, not T = constant. Temperature changes in adiabatic expansion/compression.
  • Confusing C_P and C_V in isochoric vs isobaric heat calculations. Isochoric uses C_V; isobaric uses C_P.
  • Treating entropy as path-dependent. Entropy is a state function; ΔS depends only on initial and final states, computed via reversible path even for irreversible process.
  • Forgetting Kelvin temperature scale in Carnot efficiency and entropy calculations. Using Celsius gives completely wrong numerical answer.
  • Applying free expansion formula W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) to adiabatic free expansion. In free expansion W = 0, but ΔS ≠ 0 because irreversible.
  • Mixing up degrees of freedom for diatomic gas. At room temperature f = 5 (3 trans + 2 rot); vibration activates only at high T (~1000 K+).
  • Neglecting that sound needs medium. Light from stars reaches us, but sound cannot travel through vacuum—common misconception in space-themed problems.
  • Using average speed instead of rms speed in kinetic energy and temperature relations. RMS speed appears in ½mv_rms² = 3/2 k_BT derivation.
