Kinematics: One-Dimensional Motion

Kinematics is the foundation of all mechanics. In one dimension, motion is described using displacement, velocity, acceleration, and time. The three equations of motion apply ONLY under constant acceleration:

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as

Here, u = initial velocity, v = final velocity, a = acceleration, t = time, s = displacement.

Displacement vs distance: displacement is vector (final position − initial position); distance is scalar (total path length). For motion with constant acceleration, if the particle reverses direction, distance ≠ magnitude of displacement.

Average velocity = total displacement / total time. Average speed = total distance / total time. These are equal only when the particle moves in one direction without turning back.

For free fall (projectile under gravity alone), take a = −g = −9.8 m/s² (or −10 m/s² for estimation). Time of flight for a body projected upward from ground with speed u: T = 2u/g. Maximum height: H = u²/(2g). Time to reach max height = u/g.

Relative motion in one dimension: if A moves with velocity vA and B with vB (both along same line), then velocity of A relative to B is vAB = vA − vB. If both move in same direction, relative speed is |vA − vB|; if opposite directions, it is |vA + vB|.

Graphical interpretation: in a v–t graph, the area under the curve gives displacement (signed area); slope gives acceleration. In an x–t graph, slope gives velocity. A straight-line x–t graph means uniform motion; a parabola means constant acceleration.

Important JEE-Advanced case: motion with variable acceleration where a = f(v) or a = f(x). Use the relation a = v(dv/dx) to integrate. Example: if a = −kv, then dv/dx · v = −kv, leading to v = v₀e^(−kx).

Kinematics: Two-Dimensional Motion (Projectile & Relative Motion)

In two dimensions, resolve all quantities into horizontal (x) and vertical (y) components. Key principle: horizontal and vertical motions are INDEPENDENT.

Projectile motion from ground level with initial speed u at angle θ to horizontal:

Horizontal component: ux = u cosθ, ax = 0
Vertical component: uy = u sinθ, ay = −g

Range: R = u²sin2θ/g. Maximum range at θ = 45°, Rmax = u²/g.
Maximum height: H = u²sin²θ/(2g)
Time of flight: T = 2u sinθ/g

Trajectory equation: y = x tanθ − gx²/(2u²cos²θ) — this is a parabola.

At the highest point, velocity is purely horizontal: v = u cosθ. Acceleration remains g downward throughout.

For projection from a height h above ground with horizontal launch:
Time to fall: t = √(2h/g). Range: R = u√(2h/g).

Projectile on an inclined plane: if the plane makes angle β with horizontal and the projectile is launched at angle α to the horizontal (or angle θ to the incline), the range along the incline is:
R = 2u²sin(θ)cos(θ+β) / [g cos²β]
Maximum range down the incline occurs when θ = π/4 − β/2.

Relative motion in 2D: the concept extends naturally — vA/B = vA − vB as vectors. A classic JEE problem: rain-man problem. Rain falls vertically with speed vr, man walks horizontally with speed vm. The rain appears to come at an angle θ to vertical where tanθ = vm/vr. The man should tilt his umbrella at this angle.

Crossing a river: a boat has speed vb in still water, river flows with speed vw perpendicular to the bank. To cross in shortest time, aim perpendicular to flow. To cross with shortest displacement (reach directly opposite), the boat must be aimed upstream at angle θ where sinθ = vw/vb (requires vb > vw). Shortest crossing distance = width of river.

Newton's Laws of Motion and Friction

Newton's First Law: A body remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a net external force. This defines inertial frames. Non-inertial frames require pseudo forces.

Newton's Second Law: Fnet = ma. This is valid only in inertial frames. In a frame accelerating with acceleration A, every mass m experiences a pseudo force = −mA (directed opposite to frame's acceleration).

Newton's Third Law: Action and reaction act on different bodies. They never cancel each other. F_AB = −F_BA.

Common constraint problems:
- Massless string: tension is same throughout.
- Massless pulley: net force on it is zero → tension relations follow from equilibrium.
- Inextensible string: accelerations of connected bodies are related by geometry. If one end moves a distance x, another moves b·x where b depends on pulley arrangement.
- Wedge constraint: if block stays on wedge, their accelerations along the common normal are equal.

Free Body Diagrams (FBD): Always draw the FBD of ONE body at a time. Include ALL forces acting ON that body: gravity (mg downward), normal reaction (perpendicular to surface), tension (along string, away from body), friction (opposing relative motion or tendency of motion), spring force (kx, restoring).

Friction:
Static friction: fs ≤ μsN. It is self-adjusting — it equals the applied force up to its maximum value μsN.
Kinetic friction: fk = μkN. It opposes relative motion between surfaces and is generally less than maximum static friction (μk < μs).
Direction of friction: always opposes the relative motion (or tendency of relative motion) between the contacting surfaces, NOT necessarily the motion of the body.

Wedge-block problems: Common setup — block on accelerating wedge. For the block to remain stationary relative to the wedge, the wedge must accelerate at a = g tanθ (if frictionless). With friction, there is a range of acceptable accelerations.

Connected blocks: When two blocks of masses m₁ and m₂ are connected by a string over a pulley (Atwood machine), acceleration a = (m₁−m₂)g/(m₁+m₂) and tension T = 2m₁m₂g/(m₁+m₂).

Equilibrium of concurrent forces: For a body in equilibrium under n forces, the vector sum is zero. Lami's theorem applies for exactly three forces: each force is proportional to the sine of the angle between the other two.

Work, Energy and Power

Work done by a constant force: W = F·s·cosφ = F·s⃗, where φ is the angle between force and displacement.
Work done by a variable force: W = ∫F·dx (dot product integral along the path).
Work done by spring (from x₁ to x₂): W = ½k(x₁² − x₂²). Note: work done BY the spring is positive when the spring returns to natural length.
Work done by friction: always negative (dissipative). W_friction = −fk·d, where d is the path length (not displacement).

Work-Energy Theorem: W_net = ΔK = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i². This holds in ALL inertial frames.

Conservative vs non-conservative forces: Conservative forces (gravity, spring, electrostatic) have path-independent work; their work over a closed loop is zero. Non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance) dissipate energy.

Potential Energy: U = −∫F_conservative·dr. For gravity near Earth: U = mgh. For spring: U = ½kx².

Mechanical Energy Conservation: If only conservative forces do work, E = K + U = constant.
If non-conservative forces act: W_nc = ΔE = ΔK + ΔU.

Power: P = dW/dt = F·v (instantaneous). Average power = W/t.

Potential Energy curves and equilibrium:
- Stable equilibrium: dU/dx = 0 and d²U/dx² > 0 (minimum of U).
- Unstable equilibrium: dU/dx = 0 and d²U/dx² < 0 (maximum of U).
- Neutral equilibrium: dU/dx = 0 and d²U/dx² = 0.
Force related to potential: F = −dU/dx. The force points toward decreasing potential energy.

Variable mass systems (rocket equation): For a rocket ejecting mass at rate dm/dt with exhaust speed u relative to the rocket:
v_final = u·ln(m_initial/m_final). This is derived from momentum conservation applied to the system (rocket + ejected fuel).

Key JEE problem types: Block sliding down a curved track (use energy conservation to find speed, then centripetal force for normal reaction), ball bouncing with coefficient of restitution e (speed after nth bounce = eⁿ × initial speed), spring-block systems on rough surfaces.

Centre of Mass, Linear Momentum and Collision

Centre of Mass (COM):
For a system of particles: r_com = Σmᵢrᵢ/Σmᵢ
For continuous body: r_com = ∫r dm/∫dm
Velocity of COM: v_com = Σmᵢvᵢ/Σmᵢ = P_total/M_total
Acceleration of COM: a_com = F_ext_net/M_total

The COM moves as if all mass were concentrated there and all external forces acted at that point. Internal forces do not affect COM motion.

Linear Momentum: p = mv. Total momentum of a system: P = Σmᵢvᵢ = M·v_com.
Conservation of momentum: If F_ext = 0, then P = constant. This holds even if internal forces are large (explosions, collisions).

Impulse: J = ∫F dt = Δp. For constant force: J = F·Δt.

Collision types:
- Elastic collision: kinetic energy is conserved (KE_final = KE_initial). Both momentum and energy conserved.
- Inelastic collision: KE is NOT conserved (some lost to heat/deformation). Momentum still conserved if no external force.
- Perfectly inelastic: bodies stick together after collision; maximum KE loss.

One-dimensional elastic collision formulas (derived from conservation laws):
v₁' = [(m₁−m₂)/(m₁+m₂)]v₁ + [2m₂/(m₁+m₂)]v₂
v₂' = [2m₁/(m₁+m₂)]v₁ + [(m₂−m₁)/(m₁+m₂)]v₂

Special cases:
- Equal masses exchanging velocities: if m₁ = m₂ and v₂ = 0, then v₁' = 0, v₂' = v₁.
- Light struck by heavy: if m₁ >> m₂ and v₂ = 0, the light body rebounds with approximately 2v₁, heavy body unaffected.
- Heavy striking light: if m₁ >> m₂ and v₂ = 0, heavy body continues almost unchanged, light body shoots off at ~2v₁.

Coefficient of restitution: e = (relative speed of separation)/(relative speed of approach) = |v₂' − v₁'|/|v₁ − v₂|
- e = 1: perfectly elastic
- e = 0: perfectly inelastic
- 0 < e < 1: real-world collisions

Two-dimensional collisions: Resolve along the line of impact (normal direction) using the coefficient of restitution. Perpendicular to line of impact, each particle's velocity component remains unchanged (no impulse in that direction).

Explosion: A body breaks into fragments. Initial momentum = 0 (if at rest). Fragment momenta sum to zero vectorially. KE increases (chemical/internal energy converted to KE).

Rotational Motion — Dynamics and Kinematics

Key quantities and their linear analogs:

| Linear | Rotational |
|--------|------------|
| displacement x | angle θ (rad) |
| velocity v | angular velocity ω = dθ/dt |
| acceleration a | angular acceleration α = dω/dt |
| mass m | moment of inertia I |
| force F | torque τ = r×F |
| momentum p = mv | angular momentum L = Iω |
| KE = ½mv² | KE_rot = ½Iω² |
| Impulse J = F·t | Angular impulse = τ·t |

Moment of Inertia: I = Σmᵢrᵢ² (discrete), I = ∫r²dm (continuous). It depends on axis, mass distribution, and total mass.

Standard moments of inertia:
- Ring about diameter: MR²/2
- Ring about tangent: 3MR²/2
- Disc about axis through center ⊥ plane: MR²/2
- Disc about diameter: MR²/4
- Thin rod about center ⊥ rod: ML²/12
- Thin rod about end ⊥ rod: ML²/3
- Solid sphere about diameter: 2MR²/5
- Hollow sphere about diameter: 2MR²/3
- Solid cylinder about axis: MR²/2

Theorems:
Parallel axis theorem: I = I_com + Md² (d = perpendicular distance between axes).
Perpendicular axis theorem (planar lamina only): Iz = Ix + Iy.

Torque: τ = rFsinφ = r×F. Net torque determines angular acceleration: τ_net = Iα (about a fixed axis or COM).

Rolling motion (pure rolling): Condition v_com = Rω and a_com = Rα. No slipping at contact point.
For a body rolling down an incline of angle θ:
a_com = g sinθ / (1 + I/(mR²))
v_com at bottom of height h: v = √(2gh/(1 + I/(mR²)))

Note: lighter bodies roll slower than heavier ones ONLY if their I/(mR²) ratio differs. A solid sphere (I = 2/5 MR²) beats a ring (I = MR²) regardless of mass or radius.

Friction in rolling: Static friction provides the necessary torque for rolling. Direction: for a body rolling DOWN an incline, friction acts UP the incline (it prevents slipping and provides clockwise torque for the rolling body). For a body rolling UP, friction acts DOWN.

Angular momentum: L = Iω (about fixed axis). Torque-angular momentum relation: τ = dL/dt.

Rotational KE + Translational KE = Total KE for a rolling body: KE = ½mv_com² + ½Iω² = ½mv_com²(1 + I/(mR²)).

Gravitation

Newton's Law of Gravitation: F = GMm/r², attractive, along the line joining centers. Valid for point masses and spherical symmetric bodies (shell theorem).

Shell theorem results:
- Uniform spherical shell of mass M, radius R: outside (r > R): F = GMm/r². Inside (r < R): F = 0. Potential inside is constant = −GM/R.
- Solid sphere of mass M, radius R: outside (r ≥ R): F = GMm/r². Inside (r ≤ R): F = GMmr/R³ (linear in r).

Gravitational potential: V = −GM/r (point mass). Potential energy of two masses: U = −GMm/r. Force = −dU/dr.

Escape velocity: set KE = |PE|: ½mv_e² = GMm/R → v_e = √(2GM/R) = √(2gR). At Earth's surface: v_e ≈ 11.2 km/s. Independent of mass of projectile.

Orbital velocity (circular orbit at height h): v₀ = √(GM/(R+h)). At surface: v₀ = √(gR) ≈ 7.9 km/s.
Kepler's third law: T² ∝ a³ (a = semi-major axis). For circular orbit: T² = 4π²r³/(GM).

Energy of a satellite in circular orbit:
KE = GMm/(2r)
PE = −GMm/r
Total E = −GMm/(2r) = −KE

Important: total energy is negative (bound orbit). To escape, need E ≥ 0.

Variation of g with height: g_h = g(1 − 2h/R) for h << R, and g_h = gR²/(R+h)² generally.
Variation with depth: g_d = g(1 − d/R) assuming uniform density.

Gravitational field intensity E = −dV/dr = GM/r².

Potential inside a solid sphere: V(r) = −GM(3R² − r²)/(2R³).

Black hole condition: escape velocity ≥ c → R_s = 2GM/c² (Schwarzschild radius).

JEE advanced highlights: gravitational potential energy variation with distance (graph questions), comparison of orbits with different energies, satellite energy changes during orbital maneuvers.

Fluid Mechanics

Density: ρ = m/V. Specific gravity = ρ_substance/ρ_water.

Pressure: P = F/A (normal force per unit area). Unit: Pascal (Pa) = N/m².

Pascal's law: pressure applied to enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished in all directions.

Hydrostatic pressure: P = P₀ + ρgh, where h is depth below free surface. Pressure depends only on depth, not on container shape.

Archimedes' principle: buoyant force = weight of fluid displaced. F_b = ρ_fluid·V_submerged·g.

Floating condition: for a body of density ρ_b and volume V floating in fluid of density ρ_f:
V_submerged/V = ρ_b/ρ_f

Bernoulli's theorem (energy conservation for steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow along a streamline):
P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant
Where P = static pressure, ½ρv² = dynamic pressure, ρgh = hydrostatic pressure.

Applications:
- Torricelli's theorem: speed of efflux from hole at depth h: v = √(2gh).
- Venturi meter: pressure drops where speed increases (narrow section).
- Lift on airplane wing: faster flow over top surface → lower pressure above → upward force.

Equation of continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ (incompressible flow, mass conserved).

Viscosity: internal friction between fluid layers. Shear stress: τ = η(dv/dy), where η is coefficient of viscosity.

Stokes' law (terminal velocity of sphere): F_drag = 6πηrv. Terminal velocity: v_t = 2r²(ρ_s − ρ_f)g/(9η).

Surface tension: force per unit length, or energy per unit area. Soap bubble has TWO surfaces → excess pressure = 4T/R. Single liquid surface: excess pressure = 2T/R.

Capillary rise: h = 2Tcosθ/(ρgr). For water in clean glass tube (θ ≈ 0): h = 2T/(ρgr).

JEE Advanced focuses heavily on Bernoulli's applications, continuity equation combinations, and buoyancy problems with changing fluid levels.

Circular Motion

Uniform circular motion: object moves in a circle at constant speed. Velocity is tangential; acceleration is centripetal (toward center).

Centripetal acceleration: a_c = v²/R = ω²R. This acceleration changes the DIRECTION of velocity, not its magnitude.

Centripetal force: F_c = mv²/R = mω²R. This is NOT a new force — it is the net radial component of existing forces (tension, gravity, friction, normal reaction).

Common scenarios:

Banked curve (no friction needed): tanθ = v²/(Rg). If v exceeds this, friction acts inward; if less, friction acts outward.

Car on convex bridge (top): mg − N = mv²/R → N = mg − mv²/R. For contact loss: N = 0 → v = √(gR).

Conical pendulum: string of length L makes angle θ with vertical. T cosθ = mg, T sinθ = mω²R where R = L sinθ. Period: T_period = 2π√(L cosθ/g).

Vertical circular motion (particle on string):
Minimum speed at lowest point to complete the circle: v_min = √(5gR).
At highest point: v_top = √(gR) (minimum for string to stay taut).
Tension at bottom: T_bottom = mg + mv²/R (maximum tension).
Tension at top: T_top = mg + mv²/R (minimum tension).
Difference: T_bottom − T_top = 6mg.

For rigid rod (can support compression): minimum speed at top = 0 (rod can push).

Loop-the-loop (block on track): Normal force replaces tension. Condition for leaving the track: N = 0.

Non-uniform circular motion: tangential acceleration a_t = dv/dt exists. Net acceleration = √(a_c² + a_t²). Net force = m√(a_c² + a_t²).

Common Mistakes, High-Yield Traps, and Problem-Solving Strategy

高频错误和陷阱（JEE Advanced Mechanics）：

1. Confusing distance with displacement in work calculations. Work depends on displacement for constant force, but on PATH LENGTH for friction.

2. Wrong direction of friction in rolling. Friction opposes relative motion AT THE CONTACT POINT, not the motion of the center of mass. A rolling body accelerating down an incline has friction pointing UP the incline.

3. Using v² = u² + 2as with variable acceleration. These equations are ONLY for constant acceleration.

4. Forgetting that pseudo forces act in the OPPOSITE direction of the accelerating frame's acceleration.

5. Applying conservation of energy when non-conservative forces (like kinetic friction) do work. Must include W_nc = ΔE.

6. In collision problems, applying conservation of KE when e < 1. KE is conserved ONLY for elastic collisions (e = 1).

7. Taking moment of inertia about the wrong axis without using parallel axis theorem.

8. Mixing up gravitational PE sign convention. U = −GMm/r is negative; it becomes LESS negative (increases) as r increases.

9. In fluid problems, forgetting that atmospheric pressure acts on BOTH ends of a horizontal pipe (so it cancels in Bernoulli's equation for horizontal flow).

10. In circular motion, treating centripetal force as an additional force. It is the RESULTANT of real forces, not a separate force to add to the FBD.

Problem-solving strategy for JEE Advanced Mechanics:
- Step 1: Identify the type (kinematics, dynamics, energy, rotation, collision, fluid).
- Step 2: Draw a clear FBD or energy diagram.
- Step 3: Choose the right tool — kinematics equations, F = ma, energy conservation, momentum conservation, or rotational dynamics.
- Step 4: Check constraints (inextensible string, pure rolling, no slip conditions).
- Step 5: Verify your answer with limiting cases (e.g., θ → 0, m₁ → m₂, h → 0).

Important facts

  • Equations of motion (v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as) are valid ONLY for constant acceleration.
  • Range of projectile is maximum at θ = 45°: R_max = u²/g.
  • For elastic collision of equal masses with one initially at rest, they exchange velocities.
  • In pure rolling, the condition is v_com = Rω and a_com = Rα. Friction is static and does no work.
  • Acceleration of a rolling body down an incline: a = g sinθ / (1 + I/(mR²)). Solid sphere beats hollow sphere regardless of mass/radius.
  • Minimum speed at lowest point for vertical circular motion with string: v = √(5gR). With rigid rod: v = √(gR) at top is sufficient (can be zero).
  • Escape velocity v_e = √(2gR) ≈ 11.2 km/s at Earth's surface. Independent of projectile mass.
  • Orbital velocity v₀ = √(GM/r). Total energy of satellite E = −GMm/(2r).
  • Moment of inertia of solid sphere = 2MR²/5; hollow sphere = 2MR²/3; disc = MR²/2; ring = MR².
  • Bernoulli's theorem: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline (steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow).
  • Excess pressure inside soap bubble = 4T/R; inside liquid drop = 2T/R.
  • Coefficient of restitution e = (separation speed)/(approach speed). e = 1 for elastic, e = 0 for perfectly inelastic.
  • Pseudo force in an accelerating frame = −ma_frame (acts opposite to frame's acceleration).
  • In a system with no external force, the centre of mass moves with constant velocity (or remains at rest).
  • Work done by friction is always negative (dissipative), equal to −f_k × (path length).

Common mistakes

  • Using kinematic equations for variable acceleration without converting to differential form (a = v·dv/dx or a = d²x/dt²).
  • Assuming friction always opposes motion — it opposes RELATIVE motion at the contact surface. For rolling without slipping on an incline, static friction points UP the incline.
  • Adding centripetal force as an extra force in FBD — it is the NET radial force, not a separate force.
  • Applying work-energy theorem without accounting for work done by non-conservative forces (friction, tension in moving pulleys).
  • Forgetting that kinetic friction depends on path length, not displacement, when calculating work done by friction.
  • Using conservation of kinetic energy in inelastic collisions — only momentum is conserved; KE is lost.
  • Confusing moment of inertia about COM with moment about any other axis — must use parallel axis theorem: I = I_com + Md².
  • Taking gravitational PE as positive — by convention U = −GMm/r, so it increases (becomes less negative) as r increases.
  • For soap bubbles, using 2T/R instead of 4T/R — soap bubbles have two free surfaces.
  • In projectile motion on an incline, using horizontal/vertical range formulas directly without rotating the coordinate system or using the correct inclined-plane formula.
  • In banking of roads, confusing the angle of banking with the angle of friction — tanθ = v²/(Rg) for frictionless banking.
  • In river-crossing problems, using sinθ = vw/vb for shortest time (correct for shortest displacement only); for shortest time, the boat should head perpendicular to flow (θ = 90° to bank).
  • In collision with coefficient of restitution e, forgetting that e relates ONLY the velocity components along the line of impact.
  • In rocket problems, treating mass as constant — the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation requires variable mass treatment.
  • In fluid dynamics, applying Bernoulli's equation across streamlines — it applies only along a single streamline for steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow.
