1. Reflection at Spherical Mirrors — Sign Convention, Mirror Formula and Magnification

Geometrical optics is built on two pillars — reflection and refraction — both following the ray model of light. For JEE Advanced, the first hurdle is internalizing the Cartesian sign convention, because every mirror/lens formula written without it leads to sign errors under exam pressure.

Cartesian Sign Convention for Spherical Mirrors:
- The pole (P) of the mirror is taken as the origin.
- The incident light direction is always taken as positive (normally taken along +x axis).
- Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive; opposite to incident light are negative.
- Heights measured upward from the principal axis are positive; downward are negative.
- Focal length of a concave mirror is negative (focus lies on the side from which light comes, i.e., against incident direction). For a convex mirror, focal length is positive.

Mirror Equation:
The standard relation connecting object distance u, image distance v and focal length f is:
1/v + 1/u = 1/f
Derivation sketch: Consider a concave mirror of focal length f. A ray parallel to the principal axis passes through focus after reflection; a ray through the center of curvature reflects back on itself. Using similar triangles formed by the object and image, one arrives at the mirror formula above.

Lateral Magnification:
m = h_i / h_o = -v/u
A negative m means the image is inverted relative to the object; positive m means erect.

Important special cases for concave mirror:
- Object at infinity → image at focus (real, point-sized)
- Object beyond C → image between F and C (real, diminished, inverted)
- Object at C → image at C (real, same size, inverted)
- Object between C and F → image beyond C (real, enlarged, inverted)
- Object at F → image at infinity
- Object between F and P → image behind mirror (virtual, enlarged, erect)

Important special cases for convex mirror:
- Image is always virtual, erect and diminished regardless of object position.

New Cartesian sign convention for mirror formula:
Use: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f where u and v carry algebraic signs according to the convention above. A common source of error is writing u = -10 cm for an object 10 cm in front of a concave mirror and then substituting u = -10 directly — the formula then gives 1/v = 1/f - 1/u. Always plug in the signed value explicitly.

2. Refraction at Plane and Spherical Surfaces — Lens Maker's Formula

Refraction governs how light bends at interfaces between media of different refractive indices, and is the foundation of all lens design in JEE Advanced optics.

Snell's Law:
n_1 sin(i) = n_2 sin(r)
where n_1, n_2 are refractive indices of media 1 and 2, and i, r are angles of incidence and refraction measured from the normal.

Apparent Depth (Plane Surface):
When an object is in a denser medium (refractive index μ) and viewed from air:
Apparent depth d' = d / μ
Shift in apparent position s = d - d' = d(1 - 1/μ)
If viewed from the denser medium toward air, the image is shifted away from the interface.

Refraction at a Spherical Surface (Single):
Consider a spherical surface of radius R separating media n_1 (incident side) and n_2 (transmitted side). For paraxial rays:
n_2/v - n_1/u = (n_2 - n_1)/R
Sign convention for R: positive if the center of curvature lies on the side of the outgoing light; negative otherwise.

Lens Maker's Formula (Thin Lens):
A thin lens has two spherical surfaces. Applying the spherical refraction formula twice and assuming negligible thickness, we obtain:
1/f = (μ - 1)(1/R_1 - 1/R_2)
Here μ is the refractive index of the lens material relative to the surrounding medium. If the lens is in a medium of refractive index μ_m rather than air, replace μ by μ_lens / μ_m.
- For a biconvex lens: R_1 > 0 (first surface convex toward incident light), R_2 < 0 (second surface convex away), giving f > 0 (converging).
- For a biconcave lens: R_1 < 0, R_2 > 0, giving f < 0 (diverging).
- A plano-convex lens: one radius is infinite; the flat side contributes nothing to curvature term.

Divergence of rays through a lens:
A converging lens causes parallel rays to meet at the focus. A diverging lens causes them to appear to come from the virtual focus. The nature of the image depends on object distance relative to focal length, analogous to spherical mirrors but with reversed logic for virtual images.

3. Thin Lenses — Lens Formula, Combination and Power

The thin lens is one of the most tested devices in JEE Advanced optics. Mastery requires fluency with the lens formula, magnification, combinations, and power.

Thin Lens Formula:
1/v - 1/u = 1/f
Note the sign difference from the mirror formula: for a convex (converging) lens with real object, u is negative (object on the incoming side), v is positive (real image on the outgoing side), and f is positive.
Using the sign convention consistently:
With u = -|u| for a real object: 1/v - 1/(-|u|) = 1/f → 1/v + 1/|u| = 1/f

Magnification for Thin Lens:
m = v/u = h_i/h_o
For a real image formed by a convex lens, m is negative (inverted). For a virtual image (object inside focal length), m is positive (erect) and |m| > 1.

Combination of Two Thin Lenses in Contact:
When two thin lenses of focal lengths f_1 and f_2 are in contact:
1/F = 1/f_1 + 1/f_2
The combined power P = P_1 + P_2, where P = 1/f (in meters) and is measured in dioptres (D).
If separated by a small distance d:
1/F = 1/f_1 + 1/f_2 - d/(f_1 f_2)
Power of combination: P = P_1 + P_2 - d·P_1·P_2

Magnification of combination:
m_total = m_1 × m_2

Lens Power:
P = 1/f (f in metres). A convex lens has P > 0; concave lens has P < 0. The unit is dioptre (D).

Nature of Images by Convex Lens:
- Object at infinity → image at F (real, point-sized)
- Object beyond 2F → image between F and 2F (real, diminished, inverted)
- Object at 2F → image at 2F (real, same size, inverted)
- Object between F and 2F → image beyond 2F (real, enlarged, inverted)
- Object at F → image at infinity
- Object between F and optical centre → image virtual, erect, enlarged, on same side as object

Nature of Images by Concave Lens:
Image is always virtual, erect and diminished, on the same side as the object.

Common JEE trap — immersion in liquid:
A convex lens made of glass (μ_g = 1.5) immersed in a liquid of μ_l > μ_g acts as a diverging lens because (μ_rel - 1) becomes negative, reversing f's sign.

4. Prisms, Dispersion and Minimum Deviation

Prisms introduce angular deviation and wavelength-dependent dispersion, both extensively tested in JEE Advanced.

Deviation through a Prism:
For a prism of refracting angle A and refractive index μ, a ray incident at angle i undergoes deviation δ given by:
δ = i + e - A
where e is the angle of emergence.

At minimum deviation δ_m, the ray passes symmetrically through the prism (i = e and r_1 = r_2 = A/2). The refractive index is then:
μ = sin[(A + δ_m)/2] / sin(A/2)
This is the standard experimental method to determine μ of a prism material.

Conditions for minimum deviation:
- The ray inside the prism is parallel to the base (for an isosceles prism).
- Both faces are equally inclined to the incident ray.
- The deviation is the smallest possible for the given prism and wavelength.

Dispersion:
White light splits into its constituent colours because μ depends on wavelength (Cauchy's relation): μ increases as wavelength decreases. Violet (shorter λ) deviates more than red (longer λ).
Angular dispersion: θ = δ_v - δ_r = (μ_v - μ_r)A (for small prism angles)
Mean deviation: δ_y = (μ_y - 1)A
Dispersive power: ω = (μ_v - μ_r)/(μ_y - 1)
Note: ω is a dimensionless quantity characteristic of the material, independent of prism angle.

Achromatic combination of prisms:
Two prisms of different materials can be combined to produce deviation without dispersion (or dispersion without deviation). For no net deviation:
(μ_1 - 1)A_1 = (μ_2 - 1)A_2
For no dispersion with net deviation:
ω_1 δ_1 + ω_2 δ_2 = 0
This requires one prism to be inverted relative to the other.

Total Internal Reflection in Prisms:
Right-angle isosceles prisms (45°-45°-90°) are used as reflectors: light entering normally on one face strikes the hypotenuse at 45°, which exceeds the critical angle for glass-air (≈42°), so it undergoes TIR and emerges deviated by 90° or 180° depending on configuration. This is used in periscopes, binoculars and retroreflectors.

5. Total Internal Reflection and Optical Fibres

Total internal reflection (TIR) is a threshold phenomenon that occurs only under two strict conditions, and JEE Advanced frequently tests both the conditions and their consequences.

Conditions for TIR:
1. Light must travel from a denser medium (higher refractive index μ_1) toward a rarer medium (lower refractive index μ_2), i.e., μ_1 > μ_2.
2. The angle of incidence i must exceed the critical angle C, where:
sin(C) = μ_2/μ_1
For a glass-air interface with μ_g = 1.5: C = arcsin(1/1.5) ≈ 41.8°

Critical angle dependence:
- Increases as the refractive index of the denser medium decreases.
- Depends on wavelength (since μ depends on λ); C is larger for red than for violet.
- Does not depend on the angle of incidence — it is a property of the medium pair.

Applications of TIR:
- Mirage formation: hot air near ground has lower μ than cooler air above; light from sky undergoes TIR, creating the illusion of water.
- Brilliance of diamonds: high μ ≈ 2.42 gives C ≈ 24.4°; precise cutting ensures most rays undergo multiple TIR before emerging.
- Optical fibres: light is guided through a core of higher μ surrounded by cladding of lower μ. For a fibre with core μ_1 and cladding μ_2, the numerical aperture is NA = √(μ_1² - μ_2²). The acceptance angle θ_a satisfies sin(θ_a) = NA.
- Rainbow formation: primary rainbow involves one internal reflection inside water droplets; secondary rainbow involves two internal reflections, reversing the colour order.

Optical fibre details:
Light enters the fibre within the acceptance cone defined by θ_a. Once inside, it strikes the core-cladding interface at an angle greater than C and undergoes successive TIRs, propagating along the fibre with minimal loss. Single-mode fibres have very small cores (≈8 µm) supporting one propagation mode; multi-mode fibres have larger cores and support many modes, suffering from modal dispersion.

JEE Advanced tip:
In problems involving a glass slab with a silvered face (combined reflection-refraction), treat the silvered surface as a mirror and trace the ray through refraction into the slab, reflection, and refraction back out. The equivalent mirror formula approach simplifies calculation.

6. Wave Optics — Huygens' Principle and Interference (YDSE)

Wave optics explains phenomena that ray optics cannot — interference, diffraction, and polarization — by treating light as an electromagnetic wave governed by Huygens' principle.

Huygens' Principle:
Every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets that propagate outward at the speed of light in that medium. The new wavefront at any later time is the tangential envelope of all these secondary wavelets. This principle correctly predicts reflection, refraction and the straight-line propagation of light (in homogeneous media).

Interference — General Principle:
When two or more coherent waves superpose, the resultant intensity at any point is:
I = I_1 + I_2 + 2√(I_1 I_2) cos φ
where φ is the phase difference between the waves. For coherent sources of equal intensity I_0:
I_max = 4I_0 (constructive, φ = 0, 2π, …)
I_min = 0 (destructive, φ = π, 3π, …)
For incoherent sources: I = I_1 + I_2 (no interference term).

Young's Double Slit Experiment (YDSE):
Two narrow slits S_1 and S_2, separated by distance d, are illuminated by a coherent monochromatic source of wavelength λ. On a screen at distance D (D >> d), interference fringes are observed.

Path difference at a point P at distance y from the central axis:
Δx = (d·y)/D (for small angles, sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ y/D)

Condition for bright fringes (constructive): Δx = nλ → y_n = nλD/d
Condition for dark fringes (destructive): Δx = (2n-1)λ/2 → y_n = (2n-1)λD/(2d)

Fringe width (β):
β = λD/d — the separation between consecutive bright (or dark) fringes. It is constant across the screen for small angles.

Intensity at any point:
I = I_max cos²(φ/2) = I_max cos²(πd y / λD)

Key variables and their effects:
- Increasing λ → β increases (fringes spread apart)
- Increasing d → β decreases (fringes come closer)
- Increasing D → β increases
- Immersing the entire setup in a liquid of refractive index μ: λ' = λ/μ, so β' = β/μ (fringes contract by factor μ)
- If the source is off-axis by angle α: the central fringe shifts by y_0 = αD
- Covering one slit reduces I_max but does not change β (since β depends only on geometry and λ)

Phase change on reflection:
When a wave reflects from a denser medium, it undergoes a phase change of π (equivalent to path change λ/2). In thin-film interference, this must be included: the condition for constructive interference in a film of thickness t and refractive index μ, with reflection from one denser and one rarer interface, becomes:
2μt cos r = (2n-1)λ/2

7. Diffraction and Polarization

Diffraction and polarization represent the wave nature of light beyond simple interference, and JEE Advanced tests both with conceptual clarity and quantitative precision.

Single-Slit Diffraction:
When monochromatic light of wavelength λ passes through a narrow slit of width 'a', the wavefront across the slit acts as a collection of secondary sources (Huygens' principle). On a distant screen, the interference of these secondary wavelets produces a diffraction pattern.

Minima occur at:
a sin θ = nλ, n = ±1, ±2, ±3, …
Corresponding positions on screen: y_n = nλD/a (for small angles)

The central maximum is the brightest and widest feature, extending from n = -1 to n = +1, so its angular width is 2λ/a (twice that of any secondary maximum).

Secondary maxima occur approximately at:
a sin θ ≈ (2n+1)λ/2, n = 1, 2, 3…
Their intensities drop rapidly: I_1 ≈ 4.5% of central max, I_2 ≈ 1.6%, etc.

Diffraction vs Interference distinction for JEE:
- Interference: two or more discrete coherent sources; fringes are equally spaced and of equal intensity (in ideal YDSE).
- Diffraction: continuous distribution of secondary sources across a single aperture; central maximum is much broader and brighter; minima are equally spaced but maxima decrease in intensity.

Polarization:
Light is a transverse electromagnetic wave. Polarization refers to the orientation of the electric field vector. Unpolarized light has random E-field orientations; plane-polarized light oscillates in one definite plane.

Brewster's Law:
When unpolarized light is incident on a transparent medium at the polarizing angle θ_p (Brewster angle), the reflected light is completely plane-polarized perpendicular to the plane of incidence. At this angle, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular:
tan θ_p = μ
where μ is the refractive index of the medium.

Malus' Law:
When fully plane-polarized light of intensity I_0 passes through an analyser whose transmission axis makes an angle θ with the polarization direction:
I = I_0 cos²θ
At θ = 0°, I = I_0 (maximum transmission). At θ = 90°, I = 0 (crossed polarizers, extinction). At θ = 45°, I = I_0/2.

Partial polarization by scattering:
Sunlight scattered by atmospheric molecules is partially polarized. At 90° scattering angle, the scattered light is completely polarized perpendicular to the plane containing the incident and scattered rays. This explains why the sky appears brightest blue (and polarized) 90° from the sun.

8. Ray Tracing Techniques, Resolving Power and Important JEE-Advanced Applications

JEE Advanced frequently combines optics with mechanics (e.g., moving mirrors/lenses) and asks for rates of image motion, or presents layered media problems requiring careful application of Snell's law at each interface. The following techniques and results are essential.

Key ray-tracing rules for mirrors and lenses:
- Ray parallel to principal axis → passes through focus after reflection/refraction.
- Ray through focus → emerges parallel to principal axis.
- Ray through centre of curvature (mirror) or optical centre (lens) → undeviated.
These three rays are sufficient to locate any image geometrically.

Moving object/mirror/lens problems:
Differentiating the mirror or lens formula with respect to time gives the relationship between object velocity v_o and image velocity v_i. For a spherical mirror:
v_i = -m² · v_o (along the principal axis), where m is the lateral magnification. The negative sign indicates direction: if the object moves toward the mirror, the image moves accordingly. Perpendicular to the axis, v_i⊥ = m · v_o⊥.

Microscope and Telescope resolving power:
The minimum resolvable angular separation (Rayleigh criterion):
θ_min = 1.22 λ / D
where D is the diameter of the objective aperture.
Resolving power = 1/θ_min = D/(1.22λ)
For a microscope: RP = 2μ sin θ / λ = numerical aperture / λ. Immersion oil (μ > 1) improves resolution.
For a telescope: RP = D/(1.22λ). Larger objective diameter gives better resolution.

Layered media — effective shift:
When a object is viewed through multiple parallel slabs of thickness t_i and refractive index μ_i, the total apparent shift is:
s = Σ t_i (1 - 1/μ_i)
This is additive because each slab independently shifts the apparent position.

Optical instruments summary:
- Simple microscope (magnifying glass): angular magnification M = 1 + D/f (image at near point, D = 25 cm) or M = D/f (image at infinity).
- Compound microscope: M = -(v/u)·(D/f_e); tube length L ≈ v + u; overall M ≈ -(L·D)/(f_o·f_e).
- Astronomical telescope (normal adjustment): M = f_o/f_e; length = f_o + f_e.

Common JEE Advanced mistakes to avoid:
- Confusing the sign of f for convex vs concave lenses in the lens formula.
- Using the mirror formula 1/v + 1/u = 1/f with u taken as positive for real objects (u must carry the sign per convention).
- Forgetting that in YDSE, immersing in liquid changes λ but NOT d or D, so β scales as 1/μ.
- Mixing up the conditions for interference maxima vs diffraction minima.
- In polarization problems, forgetting that unpolarized light passing through a polarizer loses half its intensity immediately (I = I_0/2), and only then does Malus' law apply.

Important facts

  • Cartesian sign convention: distances measured in direction of incident light are positive; heights upward from principal axis are positive. Focal length of concave mirror is negative; convex mirror is positive.
  • Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f. Lateral magnification: m = -v/u. Always apply sign convention consistently.
  • Lens maker's formula: 1/f = (μ_rel - 1)(1/R_1 - 1/R_2). When lens is immersed in liquid, use μ_rel = μ_lens/μ_liquid.
  • Combination of thin lenses in contact: P = P_1 + P_2. Separated by distance d: P = P_1 + P_2 - d·P_1·P_2.
  • Prism minimum deviation: μ = sin[(A+δ_m)/2]/sin(A/2). Dispersive power ω = (μ_v - μ_r)/(μ_y - 1) is material-dependent and dimensionless.
  • Critical angle: sin C = μ_2/μ_1 (light going from denser to rarer). TIR requires i > C and light traveling dense → rare.
  • YDSE fringe width: β = λD/d. Immersing in liquid of refractive index μ: β' = β/μ.
  • YDSE intensity: I = I_max cos²(πd y/λD). Path difference Δx = dy/D for small angles.
  • Single-slit diffraction minima: a sin θ = nλ. Central maximum angular width = 2λ/a.
  • Brewster's law: tan θ_p = μ. At Brewster angle, reflected light is completely plane-polarized.
  • Malus' law: I = I_0 cos²θ. Unpolarized light through a polarizer: transmitted intensity = I_0/2.
  • Moving image velocity (mirror): v_i = -m²·v_o along principal axis. Transverse: v_i⊥ = m·v_o⊥.
  • Resolving power of microscope: RP = 2μ sin θ/λ. Of telescope: RP = D/(1.22λ).
  • Total apparent shift through n parallel slabs: s = Σ t_i(1 - 1/μ_i).
  • Convex lens in medium with μ_m > μ_lens acts as diverging lens. Concave lens in medium with μ_m < μ_lens acts as converging lens.

Formulas and relationships

Thin lens formula: 1/v - 1/u = 1/f | m = v/u

Common mistakes

  • Using u as a positive number for real objects in the mirror formula — u must carry the sign from the Cartesian convention (negative for real objects in front of concave mirrors). This sign error propagates through every numerical answer.
  • Confusing the lens maker's formula sign: (1/R_1 - 1/R_2) depends on which surface the light hits first. Swapping R_1 and R_2 gives the wrong sign for f.
  • Applying the mirror formula 1/v + 1/u = 1/f to lenses — lenses use 1/v - 1/u = 1/f. Mixing these two is a classic JEE trap.
  • Forgetting that immersing YDSE in liquid changes the wavelength (λ → λ/μ) but NOT the slit separation d or screen distance D, so β changes proportionally to 1/μ.
  • Using the diffraction minima formula a sin θ = nλ for maxima — that formula gives MINIMA. Maxima lie approximately midway between minima but require solving dI/dθ = 0.
  • Assuming all interference fringes have equal intensity — only true for ideal YDSE with identical slits. Diffraction envelopes modulate the interference pattern in real experiments.
  • Applying Malus' law to unpolarized light directly — unpolarized light through a polarizer always gives I = I_0/2 regardless of orientation; Malus' law applies only to already-polarized light.
  • Taking the critical angle from air to glass — critical angle is only defined when light goes from denser to rarer medium. Air-to-glass never has a critical angle.
  • Forgetting the π phase change on reflection from a denser medium in thin-film interference — this shifts the constructive/destructive conditions by λ/2.
  • Mixing up the resolving power formulas for microscope and telescope — microscope RP depends on wavelength and numerical aperture; telescope RP depends on aperture diameter D.
  • Treating the separation distance d in lens combination as always reducing power — d/(f_1 f_2) subtracts from combined power only when both lenses are converging (positive f). Signs matter.
  • Assuming the image formed by a concave mirror is always real — when the object is within the focal length, the image is virtual, erect and enlarged (behind the mirror).
