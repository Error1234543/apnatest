1. Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter

This section establishes that both light and matter exhibit wave-particle duality, which is the foundational concept of quantum physics.

Photoelectric Effect
- When light of sufficiently high frequency is incident on a metal surface, electrons are emitted from it. These emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.
- Key experimental observations (Hertz & Lenard):
* There exists a threshold frequency ν₀ below which no emission occurs, regardless of intensity.
* The photocurrent is directly proportional to the intensity of light (number of photons per unit area per unit time), but the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons is independent of intensity.
* Emission is instantaneous (negligible time lag, < 10⁻⁹ s).
* Maximum kinetic energy depends linearly on the frequency of incident light and is independent of its intensity.
- Einstein's Photoelectric Equation: K_max = hν − φ₀ = h(ν − ν₀), where:
* h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck's constant)
* ν = frequency of incident radiation
* φ₀ = work function of the metal (minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface)
* ν₀ = threshold frequency = φ₀/h
* K_max = (1/2)mv_max² = eV_s, where V_s is the stopping potential
- Stopping Potential (V_s): The minimum retarding potential applied to the anode for which the photocurrent becomes zero. It satisfies eV_s = K_max = hν − φ₀.
- Intensity of light in photon picture: I = n·hν, where n is the photon flux (photons per unit area per second). Doubling intensity doubles n but not hν.

Matter Waves (de Broglie Hypothesis)
- Louis de Broglie proposed that every moving particle has an associated wave called matter wave or de Broglie wave.
- de Broglie wavelength: λ = h/p = h/(mv) for a non-relativistic particle.
- For a charged particle accelerated through potential V: p = √(2mqV), so λ = h/√(2mqV).
* Electron: λ = √(150/V) Å ≈ 12.27/√V Å (when V is in volts)
* Proton: λ_electron/√(m_p/m_e) ≈ λ_e/42.8
- Kinetic energy in terms of de Broglie wavelength: K = h²/(2mλ²)
- Photon wavelength vs particle wavelength: For a photon, E = hc/λ_photon; for a massive particle, λ = h/√(2mK). Equating energies gives different λ values.
- Applications: Electron microscope uses the much smaller de Broglie wavelength of electrons (accelerated through kV potentials) compared to visible light, achieving far greater resolving power.

Key Comparisons
| Quantity | Light (Photon) | Matter Wave |
|----------|---------------|-------------|
| Speed | c always | v < c |
| Wavelength | λ = c/ν | λ = h/mv |
| Energy | E = hν | E = K + mc² |
| Momentum | p = h/λ | p = h/λ |

2. Atomic Spectra and Bohr's Model

Bohr's model successfully explained the line spectrum of hydrogen and hydrogen-like atoms by combining classical mechanics with quantum postulates.

Spectral Series of Hydrogen
- The wavelengths of spectral lines are given by the Rydberg formula: 1/λ = R_H · Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), where R_H = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹, Z = atomic number, n₁ < n₂ are integers.
- Series for hydrogen (Z = 1):
* Lyman series: n₁ = 1, n₂ = 2,3,4,... (UV region)
* Balmer series: n₁ = 2, n₂ = 3,4,5,... (Visible region)
* Paschen series: n₁ = 3, n₂ = 4,5,6,... (IR region)
* Brackett series: n₁ = 4, n₂ = 5,6,7,... (IR region)
* Pfund series: n₁ = 5, n₂ = 6,7,8,... (Far IR)
- Minimum wavelength (series limit, n₂ → ∞): 1/λ_min = R_H · Z²/n₁²
- Maximum wavelength (first line, n₂ = n₁ + 1): compute directly.

Bohr's Postulates
1. Electrons revolve in certain discrete stable orbits (stationary orbits) without radiating energy.
2. Angular momentum is quantized: mvr = nh/(2π) = nℏ, where n = 1,2,3,...
3. Radiation is emitted or absorbed only when an electron jumps between stationary orbits: hν = E_n₂ − E_n₁ (emission if n₂ > n₁, absorption if n₂ < n₁).

Bohr Model — Derived Quantities for Hydrogen-like Atoms (nuclear charge Ze, single electron)
- Radius of nth orbit: r_n = n²a₀/Z, where a₀ = 0.529 Å (Bohr radius).
* r₁ for hydrogen = 0.529 × 10⁻¹⁰ m
* r ∝ n²/Z
- Velocity in nth orbit: v_n = Zαc/n = 2.188 × 10⁶ · Z/n m/s, where α = e²/(4πε₀ℏc) ≈ 1/137 (fine structure constant).
* v ∝ Z/n
- Total energy: E_n = −13.6 · Z²/n² eV
* Kinetic energy: K = +13.6 · Z²/n² eV = |E_n|
* Potential energy: U = −27.2 · Z²/n² eV = 2E_n
* E = K + U = −K (virial theorem for Coulomb force)
- Linear momentum: p = mv = Z e²/(2ε₀nh)
- Angular momentum: L = nℏ (by postulate)
- Time period: T_n = 2πr_n/v_n ∝ n³/Z²
- Frequency of revolution: f = 1/T_n ∝ Z²/n³
- Current due to electron in nth orbit: I = e/T_n = ev/(2πr_n)
- Magnetic field at nucleus due to electron: B ∝ I/r_n ∝ Z⁴/n³
- Magnetic moment: μ = I·A = (ev/2πr_n)·(πr_n²) = evr_n/2 = nℏe/(2m) = nμ_B, where μ_B = eh/(4πm) = 9.27 × 10⁻²⁴ J/T (Bohr magneton).

Energy Level Diagram and Transitions
- Ground state: n = 1, E₁ = −13.6 Z² eV
- Ionization energy = |E₁| = 13.6 Z² eV
- Excitation energy from ground to nth state = E_n − E₁ = 13.6Z²(1 − 1/n²) eV
- Number of spectral lines when electron falls from nth level to ground: N = n(n−1)/2
- Number of spectral lines in transition from n₂ to n₁: N = (n₂ − n₁)(n₂ − n₁ + 1)/2
- Wavelength of emitted photon for transition n₂ → n₁: 1/λ = R_H Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²)
- Energy of emitted photon: ΔE = 13.6Z²(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) eV

Limitations of Bohr's Model
- Cannot explain fine structure of spectral lines (resolved only by Sommerfeld and later quantum mechanics).
- Fails for multi-electron atoms.
- Does not account for wave nature of electrons (superseded by Schrödinger equation).
- Violates Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (precise orbit vs position-momentum trade-off).
- Cannot explain Zeeman and Stark effects.

3. Nuclear Physics — Structure and Stability

The nucleus consists of protons and neutrons (nucleons). Understanding nuclear properties requires grasping size, density, binding energy, and stability criteria.

Nuclear Radius and Density
- Nuclear radius: R = R₀ A^(1/3), where R₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m = 1.2 fm, A = mass number.
* This implies nuclear volume ∝ A, meaning nucleons are closely packed with nearly constant separation.
- Nuclear density: ρ = mass/volume ≈ (A · u)/(4/3 π R³) = constant ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³, independent of A.
* This enormous density explains why neutron stars are so dense.

Mass Defect and Binding Energy
- Mass defect: Δm = [Z·m_p + (A−Z)·m_n] − M_nucleus, where m_p = 1.007825 u, m_n = 1.008665 u, and M_nucleus is the actual nuclear mass.
* Note: Often atomic masses are given instead of nuclear masses. If M_atom is the atomic mass: Δm = [Z·M_H + (A−Z)·m_n] − M_atom, where M_H = 1.007825 u (mass of hydrogen atom ≈ proton + electron).
- Binding Energy: BE = Δm · c² = Δm (in u) × 931.5 MeV/u
- Binding Energy per nucleon: BE/A — this is the key measure of nuclear stability.

Binding Energy Curve and Its Implications
- BE/A vs A curve peaks near Fe-56 (BE/A ≈ 8.8 MeV), making iron the most stable nucleus.
- Light nuclei (A < 30): BE/A increases with A → energy released in fusion (combining light nuclei).
- Heavy nuclei (A > 200): BE/A decreases with A → energy released in fission (splitting heavy nuclei).
- Fusion: Two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus with higher BE/A. Example: D + T → He-4 + n + 17.6 MeV.
- Fission: A heavy nucleus splits into two中等mass fragments. Example: U-235 + n → Ba-141 + Kr-92 + 3n + ~200 MeV.
- The energy released equals the increase in total binding energy: Q = BE_products − BE reactants.

Nuclear Stability
- Stable nuclei lie in the band of stability on N vs Z plot.
- For light nuclei (A < 20): N ≈ Z is stable.
- For heavier nuclei: N > Z is needed (up to N/Z ≈ 1.5 for heavy nuclei) due to increasing Coulomb repulsion among protons.
- Even-even nuclei (even Z, even N) are most stable; odd-odd nuclei are least stable.
- Magic numbers (2, 8, 20, 28, 52, 82, 126): nuclei with these proton or neutron counts show exceptional stability (analogous to closed shells in atomic physics).
- Radioactive decay occurs when a nucleus is unstable — it transforms to reach a more stable configuration.

4. Radioactivity

Radioactive decay is a spontaneous process by which an unstable nucleus emits particles/radiation to become more stable. It is a first-order stochastic process.

Types of Decay
- Alpha (α) decay: Nucleus emits a helium nucleus (₂He⁴). Parent → Daughter + α.
* Equation: _Z^A X → _{Z−2}^{A−4}Y + _2^4He + Q
* Q-value = [M_parent − (M_daughter + M_α)]c²
* Kinetic energy of α particle: K_α = Q·(A−4)/A (from conservation of momentum; recoil nucleus gets K_recoil = Q·4/A)
* Alpha particles have low penetrating power (stopped by paper/skin) but high ionizing power.
- Beta minus (β⁻) decay: A neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino.
* Equation: _Z^A X → _{Z+1}^{A}Y + e⁻ + ν̄_e + Q
* The energy spectrum of β⁻ is continuous (0 to K_max = Q) because the antineutrino carries away variable energy.
* Q = [M_parent − M_daughter]c² (using atomic masses; electron masses cancel appropriately).
- Beta plus (β⁺) decay: A proton converts to a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino.
* Equation: _Z^A X → _{Z−1}^{A}Y + e⁺ + ν_e + Q
* Q = [M_parent − M_daughter − 2m_e]c² (note the 2m_e term because atomic masses include electrons)
* Positron annihilation: e⁺ + e⁻ → 2γ (each γ has energy 0.511 MeV).
- Gamma (γ) decay: An excited nucleus transitions to a lower energy state by emitting a photon. No change in Z or A.
* Energy of γ photon = difference between nuclear energy levels.
* Internal conversion: Instead of γ emission, the excitation energy is transferred to an orbital electron which is ejected.

Decay Law and Half-Life
- Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics: N = N₀e^{−λt}, where λ is the decay constant.
- Activity: A = −dN/dt = λN = A₀e^{−λt}, measured in becquerel (Bq = 1 decay/s) or curie (Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq).
- Half-life: t_{1/2} = ln 2/λ ≈ 0.693/λ — time after which N reduces to N₀/2.
- Mean life: τ = 1/λ = t_{1/2}/ln 2 ≈ 1.443 · t_{1/2}
- Number of half-lives: n = t/t_{1/2}; fraction remaining = (1/2)^n; fraction decayed = 1 − (1/2)^n.
- For decay chains (secular equilibrium): When parent half-life >> daughter half-life, after sufficient time, activity of daughter equals activity of parent: λ_d N_d = λ_p N_p.
- Mixed decay: If a nucleus can decay by two independent modes with constants λ₁ and λ₂, effective λ = λ₁ + λ₂, and t_{1/2, eff} = ln2/(λ₁ + λ₂).

Age Determination (Radiocarbon Dating)
- Living organisms maintain constant C-14/C-12 ratio from atmospheric CO₂. After death, C-14 decays (t_{1/2} = 5730 years).
- Age t = (1/λ)·ln(N₀/N) = (t_{1/2}/ln 2)·ln(A₀/A), where A₀ and A are specific activities of modern and sample carbon respectively.

5. Nuclear Reactions — Fission and Fusion

Nuclear reactions involve transformation of nuclei with release/absorption of enormous energy, governed by conservation laws.

Conservation Laws in Nuclear Reactions
- Conservation of nucleon number (A): sum of mass numbers before = after.
- Conservation of charge (Z): sum of atomic numbers before = after.
- Conservation of energy (including mass-energy): Q = (Σm_reactants − Σm_products)c².
* Q > 0: exothermic/exoergic reaction (energy released).
* Q < 0: endothermic/endogergic reaction (energy absorbed); must have threshold kinetic energy.
- Conservation of momentum: used to find energy sharing between products.
- Threshold energy for endothermic reactions: E_th = |Q|·(m_projectile + m_target)/m_target = |Q|(M_A + M_a)/M_A (in lab frame where target is at rest).

Nuclear Fission
- A heavy nucleus (A > 230) splits into two lighter nuclei (unequal masses typically), releasing neutrons and energy.
- U-235 fission: _92^235U + _0^1n → _92^236U* → _56^141Ba + _36^92Kr + 3_0^1n + ~200 MeV
* The 200 MeV breakdown: ~165 MeV in kinetic energy of fragments, ~5 MeV in prompt γ rays, ~5 MeV in β decay, ~10 MeV in neutrinos (lost), ~15 MeV from delayed neutrons/γ.
- Chain reaction: Neutrons from one fission trigger further fissions. Critical mass is the minimum amount of fissile material needed to sustain a chain reaction.
- Moderators (heavy water, graphite) slow down neutrons to thermal energies (~0.025 eV) where fission cross-section is maximum.
- Fast breeder reactors convert U-238 to Pu-239 using fast neutrons.

Nuclear Fusion
- Two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus at extremely high temperatures (10⁷–10⁸ K) to overcome Coulomb barrier.
- Key fusion reactions:
* D + D → T(1.01 MeV) + p(3.02 MeV)
* D + D → He-3(0.82 MeV) + n(2.45 MeV)
* D + T → He-4(3.5 MeV) + n(14.1 MeV) — most accessible, Q = 17.6 MeV
* D + He-3 → He-4 + p + 18.3 MeV
* In Sun (pp chain): 4p → He-4 + 2e⁺ + 2ν_e + 26.7 MeV
- Coulomb barrier height: V = Ze₁e₂/(4πε₀R) ≈ 1.44·Z₁Z₂/R(fm) MeV. For D-T, V ≈ 0.1 MeV, requiring T ~ 10⁸ K.
- Lawson criterion: nτT > 3 × 10²⁰ keV·s/m³ for net energy gain (n = plasma density, τ = confinement time, T = temperature).
- Fusion is preferred over fission: abundant fuel, less radioactive waste, inherently safe (no chain reaction runaway).

Q-value Calculations — Worked Examples
- Example 1: Find Q for α decay of U-238 → Th-234 + α.
* Q = [M(U-238) − M(Th-234) − M(He-4)] × 931.5 MeV/u
* K_α = Q × 234/238
- Example 2: Threshold energy for _7^14N(α,p)_8^17O with Q = −1.19 MeV.
* E_th = 1.19 × (14+4)/14 = 1.53 MeV

6. Semiconductors and p-n Junction Diode

Semiconductors are materials with electrical conductivity between conductors and insulators. Their properties are central to modern electronics.

Classification of Solids
- Based on energy bands:
* Conductor: Valence band overlaps conduction band (or partially filled) → electrons move freely.
* Insulator: Large band gap (> 5 eV) → negligible electrons in conduction band at room temperature.
* Semiconductor: Small band gap (Si: 1.12 eV, Ge: 0.66 eV at 300 K) → moderate conductivity.
- Intrinsic semiconductor: Pure semiconductor (Si or Ge) with no impurities. Number of electrons = number of holes: n = p = n_i (intrinsic carrier concentration).
- Extrinsic semiconductor: Doped with impurities to increase conductivity.
* n-type: Doped with pentavalent impurity (P, As, Sb). Donor level just below conduction band. Electrons are majority carriers (n >> p).
* p-type: Doped with trivalent impurity (B, Al, Ga, In). Acceptor level just above valence band. Holes are majority carriers (p >> n).
- Mass action law: n·p = n_i² (holds for both intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors at thermal equilibrium).
- Conductivity: σ = neμ_e + peμ_h, where μ_e and μ_h are electron and hole mobilities.
- Temperature dependence: n_i ∝ T^{3/2}e^{−E_g/(2kT)} — conductivity increases with temperature (opposite to metals).

p-n Junction Diode
- Formation: When p-type and n-type semiconductors are joined, diffusion of carriers across the junction creates a depletion region with built-in potential V₀ (~0.7 V for Si, ~0.3 V for Ge).
- Forward bias: p-side connected to positive, n-side to negative. Depletion width decreases, barrier lowers → large current flows. Current equation: I = I₀(e^{eV/(nkT)} − 1), where I₀ is reverse saturation current, n ≈ 1–2.
* At room temperature, eV/(kT) ≈ V/0.0259, so I rises exponentially with V.
* Dynamic resistance: r_d = dV/dI ≈ kT/(eI) in forward bias.
- Reverse bias: p-side negative, n-side positive. Depletion width increases, barrier rises → very small current I₀ (saturation current). At breakdown voltage (Zener or avalanche), current increases sharply.
- Zener diode: Heavily doped p-n junction designed to operate in reverse breakdown. Used as voltage regulator. Zener voltage V_Z is maintained across load.

Rectifiers and Filters
- Half-wave rectifier: Converts AC to pulsating DC using one diode. Efficiency = 40.6%, ripple factor = 1.21.
- Full-wave rectifier (center-tap): Two diodes, each conducts on alternate half-cycles. Efficiency = 81.2%, ripple factor = 0.48.
- Bridge rectifier: Four diodes, no center tap needed. Same performance as full-wave.
- Filter capacitor reduces ripple: Ripple factor γ ≈ 1/(4√3 fRC) for full-wave with capacitor filter.

Logic Gates
- Basic gates: AND (Y = AB), OR (Y = A+B), NOT (Y = Ā).
- Universal gates: NAND (Y = Ā+B̄), NOR (Y = Ā·B̄) — any logic function can be built from these alone.
- XOR (Y = A⊕B = ĀB + A B̄): Output high when inputs differ.
- Truth tables must be memorized for JEE Advanced.

7. X-Rays

X-rays are electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths in the range 0.01 nm to 10 nm, produced by deceleration of fast electrons or electronic transitions in inner shells.

Production of X-Rays
- X-ray tube: Hot cathode emits electrons by thermionic emission. Electrons are accelerated through a high potential V (typically 20–100 kV) toward a metal anode (target: Tungsten, Molybdenum, Copper).
- Upon striking the target, two types of X-ray spectra are produced:
* Continuous spectrum (Bremsstrahlung): Decelerated electrons emit photons with a range of energies up to a maximum.
* Characteristic spectrum: Sharp peaks superimposed on continuous spectrum, arising from inner-shell electron transitions in target atoms.

Cut-off Wavelength (Duane-Hunt Law)
- Maximum photon energy: hν_max = eV, so λ_min = hc/(eV) = 12400/V Å (V in volts).
- This cut-off depends only on accelerating voltage, not on target material.
- Doubling V halves λ_min; doubling V also quadruples the total X-ray power (since Power ∝ iV, and more electrons have higher energy).

Characteristic X-Rays and Moseley's Law
- K-alpha line: Transition from L shell (n=2) to K shell (n=1). Energy: E_Kα = 13.6(Z−1)²(1 − 1/4) = 10.2(Z−1)² eV (screening constant ≈ 1 for K series).
- More precisely, Moseley's Law: √ν = a(Z − b), where b ≈ 1 for K-series, b ≈ 7.4 for L-series.
- K-absorption edge: Minimum energy to eject a K-shell electron = 13.6Z² eV (approximate, ignoring screening).
- To produce K-alpha X-rays, the incident electron energy must exceed the K-shell binding energy: eV ≥ E_K = 13.6(Z−1)² eV.
* Thus, minimum accelerating potential for K-alpha: V_min ≈ 13.6(Z−1)²/e volts.

Properties and Applications
- Penetrating power increases as wavelength decreases (higher energy).
- Diffraction by crystals (Bragg's law: 2d sin θ = nλ) is used to determine crystal structure and X-ray wavelengths.
- Medical imaging, radiotherapy, industrial flaw detection, X-ray spectroscopy for elemental analysis.

8. Comprehensive Problem-Solving Strategies and JEE Advanced High-Yield Topics

This section consolidates the most frequently tested problem types and conceptual traps in JEE Advanced Modern Physics.

High-Yield Problem Types
1. Photoelectric effect with retarding potential: Given I-V curve, extract V_s, K_max, and relate to frequency. Multiple metals on same graph → compare work functions from intercepts.
2. de Broglie wavelength comparisons: Rank λ for electron, proton, alpha particle, deuteron accelerated through same/different potentials. Remember λ ∝ 1/√(mV).
3. Bohr model numericals: Compute radius, velocity, energy, angular momentum for any n and Z. Track how quantities scale: r ∝ n²/Z, v ∝ Z/n, E ∝ Z²/n², T ∝ n³/Z².
4. Spectral line counting: From nth excited state (principal quantum number n+1), total lines = n(n+1)/2. Lines in a specific series: count transitions ending at that n₁.
5. Nuclear decay calculations: Use N = N₀(1/2)^{t/t_{1/2}}. For mixed decay or consecutive decay, apply differential equations.
6. Q-value and threshold energy: Always use atomic masses consistently. For β⁺ decay, remember the 2m_e subtraction. For threshold, E_th = |Q|(M_a + M_A)/M_A.
7. p-n junction circuits: Analyze diode as ideal (zero resistance forward, infinite reverse) or with cut-in voltage (0.7 V Si, 0.3 V Ge). Current direction depends on bias.
8. X-ray cutoff and characteristic lines: Relate accelerating voltage to λ_min and to whether characteristic lines can be produced.

Common Mistakes and Confusions
- confusing work function φ with threshold frequency ν₀ (φ = hν₀, not φ = h/cλ₀ unless you're careful with units);
- forgetting that in β⁺ decay the Q-value uses (M_parent − M_daughter − 2m_e)c², while in β⁻ it uses (M_parent − M_daughter)c² with atomic masses;
- assuming the stopping potential depends on light intensity (it does not — only on frequency);
- using nuclear masses when atomic masses are given (or vice versa) without adjusting for electron masses;
- confusing mean life with half-life (τ = t_{1/2}/0.693 ≈ 1.44 t_{1/2});
- forgetting that in a p-n junction under forward bias, the potential barrier is REDUCED by V_applied, not increased;
- assuming all spectral lines from a transition fall in the same region (they span UV, visible, IR depending on final n);
- neglecting the recoil of the nucleus in alpha decay when computing K_α (always use momentum conservation: K_α = Q·(A−4)/A);
- in de Broglie problems, using λ = h/p for photons (where p = E/c = h/λ) and confusing the formulas for massive particles;
- forgetting that in the Bohr model, all derived formulas assume a single electron (hydrogen-like); multi-electron atoms require empirical corrections.
