JEE Advanced Physics Notes: Electricity and Magnetism

JEE AdvancedEnglishNotes

Electrostatics: Charge, Coulomb’s Law, and Electric Field

Charge is a scalar intrinsic property of matter responsible for electromagnetic interactions. It exists in two types: positive (proton) and negative (electron). Charge is conserved (total charge in an isolated system remains constant) and quantized (q = ne, where e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C). Coulomb’s Law gives the force between two point charges: F = kq₁q₂/r², where k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 9×10⁹ N·m²/C². The force acts along the line joining the charges, is attractive for opposite signs and repulsive for like signs. In vector form, F₁₂ = (kq₁q₂/r³)r⃗₁₂. Superposition principle states that the net force on a charge due to multiple charges is the vector sum of individual forces. Electric field E⃗ at a point is defined as the force per unit positive test charge: E⃗ = F⃗/q₀. For a point charge Q, E = kQ/r² directed radially. For continuous charge distributions, integrate dE⃗ over the charge element dq: E⃗ = ∫ k*dq/r² r̂. Key geometries: infinite line charge (E = λ/(2πε₀r)), infinite plane sheet (E = σ/(2ε₀)), charged ring on axis (E = kQx/(x²+R²)^(3/2)), solid sphere (inside: E = kQr/R³, outside: E = kQ/r²), spherical shell (inside: E=0, outside: same as point charge). Electric field lines never cross, are tangent to field direction, density indicates strength, and originate from positive and terminate on negative charges.

Electric Potential and Gauss’s Law

Electric potential V at a point is the work done per unit charge in bringing a positive test charge from infinity to that point against the electric field: V = W/q₀ = -∫∞ʳ E⃗·dl⃗. For a point charge Q, V = kQ/r. Potential is a scalar; total potential due to multiple charges is algebraic sum V = Σ kqᵢ/rᵢ. Potential difference ΔV = V_B - V_A = -∫_A^B E⃗·dl⃗. Relationship with field: E⃗ = -∇V (in one dimension E = -dV/dx), so field points in direction of steepest decrease of potential. Equipotential surfaces are loci of points at same potential; no work is done moving charge on them, and they are perpendicular to electric field lines. Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium have constant potential throughout and on surface; electric field inside conductor is zero, and any excess charge resides on outer surface. Gauss’s Law relates flux through a closed surface to enclosed charge: Φ_E = ∮ E⃗·dA⃗ = Q_enclosed/ε₀. Useful for symmetric charge distributions to find E⃗ efficiently. Applications: infinite line (cylindrical Gaussian surface), infinite plane (pillbox), spherical shell/solid sphere (concentric sphere). Capacitance C is ability to store charge per unit potential: C = Q/V. For parallel plate capacitor C = ε₀A/d (air), with dielectric C = Kε₀A/d where K is dielectric constant. Energy stored U = ½CV² = Q²/(2C) = ½QV. Energy density in electric field u = ½ε₀E². When capacitors are connected in parallel, equivalent C = ΣCᵢ; in series, 1/C_eq = Σ1/Cᵢ.

Current Electricity: Resistance, Ohm’s Law, and Circuits

Electric current I is rate of flow of charge: I = dq/dt (unit ampere). Current density J⃗ = I/A (for uniform distribution) or microscopically J⃗ = nev_d, where n is charge carrier density and v_d is drift velocity. Drift velocity v_d = eEτ/m = μE, where τ is relaxation time and μ mobility. Ohm’s Law: V = IR holds for ohmic conductors at constant temperature; R is resistance. Resistivity ρ = R*A/l, conductivity σ = 1/ρ. Temperature dependence: ρ_T = ρ₀(1 + αΔT). Series combination: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + ...; current same, voltage divides. Parallel combination: 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...; voltage same, current divides. Electromotive force (emf) E is work done per unit charge by source; terminal voltage V = E - Ir (discharging) or V = E + Ir (charging). Kirchhoff’s Laws: Junction rule (ΣI_in = ΣI_out, based on charge conservation) and Loop rule (ΣΔV = 0 around closed loop, based on energy conservation). Wheatstone bridge balances when R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄, null deflection in galvanometer. Meter bridge is practical form using uniform wire. Potentiometer measures potential difference without drawing current; principle: E₁/E₂ = l₁/l₂ for balanced lengths. Heating effect: power P = VI = I²R = V²/R; energy H = I²Rt (Joule’s law). Cells in series: E_eq = ΣEᵢ, r_eq = Σrᵢ; cells in parallel (same emf): E_eq = E, r_eq = r/n. Maximum power transfer theorem: power delivered to load R is max when R = r (internal resistance).

Magnetic Effects of Current: Biot-Savart and Ampere’s Law

Moving charges produce magnetic fields. Biot-Savart Law: dB⃗ = (μ₀/4π) (Idl⃗ × r̂)/r² for current element, or dB⃗ = (μ₀/4π) (qv⃗ × r̂)/r² for moving charge. μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T·m/A. Direction given by right-hand thumb rule. Contributions: infinite straight wire B = μ₀I/(2πr); on axis of circular loop B = μ₀IR²/(2(R²+x²)^(3/2)); at center B = μ₀I/(2R). Solenoid (long, n turns/unit length): inside B = μ₀nI (uniform), outside ≈ 0. Toroid: inside B = μ₀nI, outside B = 0. Ampere’s Circuital Law: ∮ B⃗·dl⃗ = μ₀I_enclosed, useful for symmetric current distributions. Force between parallel currents: F/l = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd), attractive if currents same direction, repulsive if opposite. Lorentz force on charge q moving with velocity v⃗ in B⃗: F⃗ = q(v⃗ × B⃗). If electric field also present, F⃗ = q(E⃗ + v⃗ × B⃗). Motion in uniform B field: if v⃗ perpendicular to B⃗, particle moves in circle radius r = mv/(qB), frequency f = qB/(2πm) (cyclotron frequency), time period T = 2πm/(qB). If v⃗ has component parallel to B⃗, helical path. Force on current-carrying conductor: F⃗ = I(l⃗ × B⃗). Torque on rectangular coil (N turns, area A): τ⃗ = N I (A⃗ × B⃗); magnitude τ = NIAB sinθ. Moving coil galvanometer converts current to angular deflection; torsional restoring torque κθ balances magnetic torque NIAB at equilibrium, giving θ ∝ I. Sensitivity = θ/I = NAB/κ. To convert galvanometer to ammeter, shunt resistance S = (I_gR_g)/(I - I_g) placed in parallel; to voltmeter, series resistance R = V/I_g - R_g.

Magnetism and Matter: Dia-, Para-, Ferro-magnetism

Magnetic dipole moment μ⃗ for current loop: μ = IA (area vector). Torque τ⃗ = μ⃗ × B⃗. Potential energy U = -μ⃗·B⃗. Earth’s magnetism: declination (angle between geographic and magnetic meridian), inclination/dip (angle magnetic field makes with horizontal), horizontal component B_H = B cosδ, vertical component B_V = B sinδ, B_H = B cosδ. Magnetic materials classified by response to external field: diamagnetic (weakly repelled, χ < 0 small, e.g., Cu, Bi, water), paramagnetic (weakly attracted, χ > 0 small, e.g., Al, O₂, depends on temperature inversely Curie’s law χ = C/T), ferromagnetic (strongly attracted, χ >> 0, e.g., Fe, Co, Ni, exhibits domains). Ferromagnetic behavior: hysteresis loop shows retentivity (residual magnetism at H=0) and coercivity (reverse field to demagnetize). Hard magnets (high retentivity, coercivity) for permanent magnets; soft magnets (low hysteresis loss) for transformers. Curie temperature T_C: above which ferromagnetic becomes paramagnetic. Magnetic field intensity H⃗ and magnetic field B⃗ related by B⃗ = μ₀(H⃗ + M⃗) = μ₀(1+χ)H⃗ = μ₀μ_r H⃗, where M is magnetization, μ_r relative permeability. For diamagnetic μ_r < 1, para μ_r slightly > 1, ferro μ_r >> 1.

Electromagnetic Induction (EMI)

Faraday’s Law: induced emf E = -dΦ_B/dt, where Φ_B = ∫ B⃗·dA⃗ is magnetic flux. Negative sign is Lenz’s law: induced emf opposes change in flux producing it. For coil of N turns, E = -N dΦ/dt. Motional emf: conductor of length l moving with velocity v⃗ perpendicular to B⃗, E = Blv. General motional emf E = ∫ (v⃗ × B⃗)·dl⃗. Self-inductance L: flux linkage Φ = LI, so E = -L dI/dt. For solenoid L = μ₀n²Al = μ₀N²A/l. Mutual inductance M: emf in coil 2 due to changing current in coil 1, E₂ = -M dI₁/dt. For two coaxial solenoids M = μ₀N₁N₂A/l. Energy stored in inductor U = ½LI². LR circuit: current growth I = I₀(1 - e^(-t/τ)) with τ = L/R, decay I = I₀e^(-t/τ). RC circuit charging V_C = ε(1 - e^(-t/τ)), discharging V_C = εe^(-t/τ), τ = RC. Displacement current I_d = ε₀ dΦ_E/dt added by Maxwell to complete Ampere’s law. Eddy currents: circulating currents in bulk conductor due to changing flux, cause energy loss (transformer cores laminated to reduce). Electrical generator converts mechanical to electrical energy; AC generator E = NBAω sin(ωt), peak emf E₀ = NBAω.

Alternating Current (AC) Circuits

AC voltage V = V₀ sin ωt, current I = I₀ sin(ωt ± φ). Impedance Z combines resistance and reactance. Resistor: V and I in phase, Z = R, average power P = VrmsI rms = I²rmsR. Inductor: voltage leads current by π/2, inductive reactance X_L = ωL, Z = X_L, average power zero (ideal). Capacitor: voltage lags current by π/2, capacitive reactance X_C = 1/(ωC), Z = X_C, average power zero. Series LCR circuit: impedance Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²), tan φ = (X_L - X_C)/R. Resonance occurs when X_L = X_C, i.e., ω₀ = 1/√(LC), impedance minimum Z = R, current maximum, voltage across L and C can exceed source voltage. Quality factor Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR) = (1/R)√(L/C) measures sharpness of resonance. Average power P_avg = V_rmsI_rmscos φ = I²rms*R. Power factor cos φ = R/Z. Transformers: step-up increases voltage (N_s > N_p), step-down decreases; ideal transformer V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p = I_p/I_s, power input = power output (neglecting losses). Real transformers have copper loss (I²R heating), iron loss (hysteresis + eddy currents), flux leakage. Three-phase AC uses three alternating voltages phase-shifted by 120°; delivers more power with less conductor material; rectification converts AC to DC using diodes (half-wave uses one diode, full-wave bridge uses four).

Worked Examples and Numerical Application

Example 1: Two identical capacitors C connected in series across battery V. Energy stored U = ½(C/2)V² = CV²/4. If now disconnected and reconnected in parallel, total charge Q = CV/2 is conserved; new capacitance 2C, new voltage V' = Q/(2C) = V/4, energy U' = ½(2C)(V/2)² = CV²/8. Energy loss = CV²/8 dissipated as heat during redistribution. Example 2: Wire of resistance R bent into circle of radius r. Resistance between diameter endpoints: two semicircular arcs each of resistance R/2 in parallel, R_eq = (R/2)/2 = R/4. Example 3: Charged particle entering uniform B field perpendicularly with speed v. Radius r = mv/(qB), period T = 2πm/(qB), independent of v and r. If enters at angle θ to B, helical path with pitch p = v cosθ T. Example 4: Rod of length l rotating with angular velocity ω about one end in plane perpendicular to uniform B. Motional emf between ends: integrate dE = Bv*dx = Bωx dx from 0 to l gives E = Bωl²/2. Example 5: LR circuit switched on at t=0. Current I = (ε/R)(1 - e^(-Rt/L)). Time to reach 63% of max current equals τ = L/R. Inductor voltage decays exponentially.

Common Mistakes and Confusion Points

Confusing electric potential (scalar) with electric field (vector): potential can be zero where field is non-zero (midpoint of dipole). Forgetting sign convention in Gauss’s law: outward normal is positive, inward flux negative. Assuming ohm’s law applies to all devices: semiconductors, diodes, batteries under charge do not obey V = IR linearly. In capacitors with dielectric, if battery remains connected voltage fixed and charge increases; if disconnected charge fixed and voltage decreases. Treating inductors as open circuit in steady state DC: actually short circuit (zero resistance ideal). Mixing up self and mutual inductance formulas. In AC circuits, forgetting that V_rms = V₀/√2 and average power depends on power factor cosφ. Assuming magnetic field does work on charged particle: Lorentz force is always perpendicular to velocity, so no work, only changes direction. Confusing electric flux and magnetic flux units: weber (Wb) for magnetic, N·m²/C for electric. In electromagnetic induction, forgetting Lenz’s law sign leads to wrong current direction. For potentiometer, assuming direct proportionality without constant potential gradient. In transformer equations, ignoring that ideal transformer assumes no flux leakage and zero resistance windings.

Important facts

  • Coulomb constant k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 9×10⁹ N·m²/C².
  • Elementary charge e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C; charge is quantized q = ne.
  • Electric field inside conductor in electrostatics is zero; excess charge resides on surface.
  • Gauss's law ∮E⃗·dA⃗ = Q_enc/ε₀ is most useful for highly symmetric charge distributions.
  • Potential difference V = -∫E⃗·dl⃗; electric field points toward decreasing potential.
  • Capacitance of parallel plate capacitor C = ε₀A/d; with dielectric C = Kε₀A/d.
  • Energy stored in capacitor U = ½CV²; energy density u = ½ε₀E².
  • Drift velocity v_d = I/(neA); relation between current and microscopic motion.
  • Kirchhoff's junction rule conserves charge; loop rule conserves energy.
  • Wheatstone bridge balance condition R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄.
  • Biot-Savart law dB = (μ₀/4π)Idl sinθ/r².
  • Magnetic field due to infinite wire B = μ₀I/(2πr).
  • Solenoid inside B = μ₀nI; toroid B = μ₀NI/(2πr).
  • Lorentz force F⃗ = q(E⃗ + v⃗×B⃗); magnetic component does no work.
  • Radius of circular path in B field r = mv/(qB); cyclotron frequency f = qB/(2πm).
  • Force per unit length between parallel wires F/l = μ₀I₁I₂/(2πd).
  • Galvanometer sensitivity θ/I = NAB/κ; convert to ammeter with shunt, voltmeter with series resistor.
  • Faraday's law E = -dΦ/dt; Lenz's law gives direction opposing change.
  • Motional emf E = Blv for perpendicular motion; general E = ∫(v⃗×B⃗)·dl⃗.
  • Self-inductance solenoid L = μ₀n²Al; energy U = ½LI².
  • Mutual inductance E₂ = -M dI₁/dt.
  • LCR resonance angular frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC); quality factor Q = ω₀L/R.
  • AC rms values: V_rms = V₀/√2, I_rms = I₀/√2; average power P = V_rms I_rms cosφ.
  • Transformer ratio V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p = I_p/I_s (ideal).
  • Diamagnetic χ < 0, paramagnetic χ > 0 small, ferromagnetic χ >> 0.
  • Curie's law for paramagnets χ = C/T; Curie temperature T_C for ferro→para transition.
  • Earth's magnetic field components: B_H = B cosδ, B_V = B sinδ.
  • Displacement current I_d = ε₀ dΦ_E/dt completes Ampere-Maxwell law.

Common mistakes

  • Treating electric potential as vector; potential is scalar, field is vector.
  • Applying Gauss's law naively without symmetry; only useful when E is constant on Gaussian surface.
  • Forgetting that potential can be zero where field is non-zero (example: midpoint of dipole).
  • Sign errors in Faraday's and Lenz's law; induced emf always opposes change in flux.
  • Assuming capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously but current through inductor can; opposite is true.
  • Using DC formulas for AC without converting to RMS or including phase angles.
  • Confusing self-inductance L with mutual inductance M; L is for single coil, M for two coupled coils.
  • Neglecting internal resistance of cell in circuit calculations when not stated ideal.
  • Assuming magnetic force does work; magnetic force is always perpendicular to velocity, so zero work.
  • Forgetting that in resonance X_L = X_C, impedance is minimum and purely resistive.
  • Mixing up expressions for field of line charge (∝ 1/r) vs plane sheet (constant).
  • Using wrong expression for capacitor energy when dielectric is inserted with/without battery connected.
  • Incorrectly adding resistances in series/parallel for complex networks without simplifying stepwise.
  • In potentiometer, assuming length directly equals potential without ensuring uniform potential gradient.
  • Forgetting that galvanometer has some resistance; shunt and series calculations must account for R_g.
  • Treating transformer as 100% efficient in problems that ignore copper/iron losses.
  • Applying Biot-Savart law without proper vector cross product for direction.
  • Confusing emf and potential difference; emf is source energy per charge, PD is energy dissipated per charge.
