JEE Advanced Mathematics — Trigonometry: Quick Revision

JEE AdvancedEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Trigonometric functions are ratios of sides in a right triangle or coordinates on the unit circle: sin θ = y/r, cos θ = x/r, tan θ = y/x, cot θ = x/y, sec θ = r/x, cosec θ = r/y, where r = √(x²+y²).
  • An angle is in standard position when its vertex is at the origin and initial side lies along the positive x-axis; the terminal side determines the quadrant and sign of trigonometric ratios.
  • A radian is the measure of a central angle subtending an arc equal in length to the radius: θ (rad) = s/r. One complete revolution = 2π radians = 360°.
  • Trigonometric equations are equations involving trigonometric functions of unknown angles; solutions are called general solutions or principal solutions depending on the domain restriction.
  • Inverse trigonometric functions return the angle whose trig ratio equals a given value: sin⁻¹x gives θ such that sin θ = x, with restricted range to make the function single-valued.
  • The principal value branch of sin⁻¹x is [−π/2, π/2], cos⁻¹x is [0, π], tan⁻¹x is (−π/2, π/2), cosec⁻¹x is [−π/2, π/2] − {0}, sec⁻¹x is [0, π] − {π/2}, cot⁻¹x is (0, π).
  • The Law of Sines states a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C = 2R, where R is the circumradius of the triangle.
  • The Law of Cosines states a² = b² + c² − 2bc cos A (and cyclic permutations), relating side lengths to one included angle.
  • The Law of Tangents states (a−b)/(a+b) = tan½(A−B)/tan½(A+B), useful in certain triangle solution problems.

Key concepts

  • ANGLE MEASURE SYSTEMS AND CONVERSION: Degree–grade–radian relationships: 180° = π rad = 200g. To convert degrees to radians multiply by π/180; to convert radians to degrees multiply by 180/π. Arc length formula: s = rθ (θ in radians). Sector area: A = ½r²θ. These formulas are the foundation — every advanced trig problem assumes θ is in radians unless degrees are explicitly stated.
  • TRIGONOMETRIC RATIOS FOR STANDARD ANGLES AND QUADRANT SIGNS: Remember sin 0°=0, sin 30°=½, sin 45°=1/√2, sin 60°=√3/2, sin 90°=1. The CAST rule or 'All Students Take Calculus' tells signs in quadrants I–IV: All positive, Sine positive, Tangent positive, Cosine positive. Key identity: sin(90°−θ)=cos θ, cos(90°−θ)=sin θ, tan(90°−θ)=cot θ (co-function identities). For JEE Advanced, you must also know sin(18°)=(√5−1)/4, cos(18°)=√(10+2√5)/4, sin(36°), cos(36°)=(1+√5)/4, sin(72°), and their supplementary/complementary counterparts.
  • PRODUCTION FORMULAS (EXPANDING sum into product): sin C + sin D = 2sin½(C+D)cos½(C−D); sin C − sin D = 2cos½(C+D)sin½(C−D); cos C + cos D = 2cos½(C+D)cos½(C−D); cos C − cos D = −2sin½(C+D)sin½(C−D). Product-to-sum: 2sinA cosB = sin(A+B)+sin(A−B); 2cosA cosB = cos(A+B)+cos(A−B); 2sinA sinB = cos(A−B)−cos(A+B). These are indispensable for solving trigonometric equations and simplifying expressions in JEE Advanced.
  • MULTIPLE-ANGLE AND SUB-MULTIPLE-ANGLE FORMULAE: sin 2θ = 2sinθcosθ; cos 2θ = cos²θ−sin²θ = 2cos²θ−1 = 1−2sin²θ; tan 2θ = 2tanθ/(1−tan²θ). Triple angle: sin 3θ = 3sinθ−4sin³θ; cos 3θ = 4cos³θ−3cosθ; tan 3θ = (3tanθ−tan³θ)/(1−3tan²θ). Half-angle: sin²θ = (1−cos2θ)/2, cos²θ = (1+cos2θ)/2, tanθ = sin2θ/(1+cos2θ) = (1−cos2θ)/sin2θ. For JEE Advanced, memorise sin⁻¹x + cos⁻¹x = π/2 and the expansion of sin(nθ) and cos(nθ) via De Moivre's theorem.
  • GENERAL SOLUTIONS OF TRIGONOMETRIC EQUATIONS: sin θ = sin α implies θ = nπ + (−1)ⁿα, n ∈ ℤ. cos θ = cos α implies θ = 2nπ ± α, n ∈ ℤ. tan θ = tan α implies θ = nπ + α, n ∈ ℤ. For equations of the form a cos θ + b sin θ = c, write √(a²+b²) sin(θ+φ) = c where φ = tan⁻¹(a/b); real solutions exist only if |c| ≤ √(a²+b²). For quadratic trig equations, factor or use the substitution t = tan(θ/2). JEE Advanced frequently combines these with domain restrictions.
  • INVERSE TRIGONOMETRIC FUNCTIONS — IDENTITIES AND DOMAIN/RANGE: sin⁻¹x + cos⁻¹x = π/2 for |x| ≤ 1. tan⁻¹x + cot⁻¹x = π/2 for all real x. sec⁻¹x + cosec⁻¹x = π/2 for |x| ≥ 1. tan⁻¹x + tan⁻¹y = tan⁻¹((x+y)/(1−xy)) when xy < 1; add π if xy > 1 and x,y > 0; subtract π if xy > 1 and x,y < 0. For 3tan⁻¹x = tan⁻¹(x³−3x)/(3x²−1) when |x| < 1. Monotonicity: sin⁻¹x is increasing on [−1,1], cos⁻¹x is decreasing on [−1,1], tan⁻¹x is increasing on ℝ. Graph sketches of all six inverse functions are frequently asked.
  • PROPERTIES OF TRIANGLES — AREA AND HALF-ANGLE FORMULAE: Semi-perimeter s = (a+b+c)/2. Area Δ = √(s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)) — Heron's formula. Also Δ = ½ab sin C = ½bc sin A = ½ca sin B = abc/(4R) = rs, where r is inradius and R is circumradius. Half-angle formulae: sin(A/2) = √((s−b)(s−c)/(bc)), cos(A/2) = √(s(s−a)/(bc)), tan(A/2) = √((s−b)(s−c)/(s(s−a))). These are critical for JEE Advanced numerical-value questions.
  • TRIANGLE CENTRES AND SPECIAL POINTS: Centroid G divides medians in 2:1. Circumcentre O is intersection of perpendicular bisectors; distance OA=OB=OC=R. Orthocentre H is intersection of altitudes; in an acute triangle H lies inside. Incentre I is intersection of angle bisectors; distance from I to each side equals r. Nine-point centre N is midpoint of OH; radius = R/2. Euler line: O, G, H are collinear with OG:GH = 1:2. JEE Advanced may ask coordinate geometry proofs involving these centres.
  • GEOMETRICAL APPLICATIONS — HEIGHTS AND DISTANCES: From a point on horizontal ground, elevation angle α gives height h = d tan α where d is horizontal distance. If angles of elevation/depression from two points at distance d apart are α and β on same side: h = d·tanα·tanβ/(tanβ−tanα). On opposite sides: h = d·tanα·tanβ/(tanα+tanβ). For JEE Advanced, combine with coordinate geometry and vector methods for 3D applications.
  • DE MOIVRE'S THEOREM AND ITS APPLICATIONS: (cos θ + i sin θ)ⁿ = cos nθ + i sin nθ. Expanding the left using binomial theorem and equating real/imaginary parts yields expansions of cos nθ and sin nθ. Roots of unity: zⁿ = 1 has roots e^(2πik/n), k=0,1,...,n−1; their sum is zero; they form vertices of a regular n-gon on the unit circle. Sum of roots of unity and products are classic JEE Advanced combinatorics-trig hybrid questions.

Important facts

  • sin(nπ + θ) = (−1)ⁿ sin θ; cos(nπ + θ) = (−1)ⁿ cos θ; sin(2nπ + θ) = sin θ; cos(2nπ + θ) = cos θ — these reduction formulas are used constantly.
  • Maximum value of a cos θ + b sin θ + c is c + √(a²+b²); minimum is c − √(a²+b²). This result is proved by writing a cos θ + b sin θ = √(a²+b²)cos(θ−α).
  • For any triangle ABC: A + B + C = π; sin A + sin B + sin C ≤ (3√3)/2; cos A + cos B + cos C ≤ 3/2; sin 2A + sin 2B + sin 2C = 4 sin A sin B sin C; cos 2A + cos 2B + cos 2C = −1 − 4 cos A cos B cos C.
  • If A + B + C = π, then tan A + tan B + tan C = tan A · tan B · tan C (provided none of the tangents is undefined). This is a favourite JEE Advanced identity.
  • If A + B + C = π/2, then cot A · cot B · cot C = cot A + cot B + cot C. This identity is used in several JEE Advanced problems.
  • sin⁻¹(−x) = −sin⁻¹x (odd function); cos⁻¹(−x) = π − cos⁻¹x; tan⁻¹(−x) = −tan⁻¹x (odd function). These symmetry properties simplify many inverse trig problems.
  • sin⁻¹(sin θ) = θ only when θ ∈ [−π/2, π/2]; otherwise it reduces to the nearest angle in that interval using periodicity and symmetry. Similarly for cos⁻¹(cos θ) and tan⁻¹(tan θ). JEE Advanced loves testing this piecewise behaviour.
  • In any triangle: r = 4R sin(A/2) sin(B/2) sin(C/2); distance between circumcentre and orthocentre OH = R√(1 − 8 cos A cos B cos C); distance between circumcentre and incentre OI = √(R(R−2r)) — Euler's theorem in geometry.
  • Area of triangle formed by lines y = m₁x + c₁, y = m₂x + c₂, y = m₃x + c₃ is ½ | (c₂−c₁)(c₃−c₁)(m₃−m₂) / ((m₁−m₂)(m₂−m₃)(m₃−m₁)) | — useful for coordinate geometry + trig combinations.
  • The number of solutions of a trig equation in [0, 2π) depends on the frequency: sin nx = k has at most 2n solutions in one period; cos nx = k similarly. Counting solution branches correctly is a recurring JEE Advanced trap.

Memory aids

  • CAST mnemonic: Quadrant I — All positive; II — Sine positive; III — Tangent positive; IV — Cosine positive. Draw a cross dividing the plane into four quadrants and label each zone — this visual is faster than rote memorisation under exam pressure.
  • Panda bear rule for standard angles: write sin values for 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90° as √0/2, √1/2, √2/2, √3/2, √4/2 — i.e., take square root of 0,1,2,3,4 and divide by 2. Cosine is the reverse order. This avoids confusion between sine and cosine values.
  • The phrase 'Some Students Take Chemistry' maps to Sin positive (QII), tan positive (QIII), cos positive (QIV). Pair this with drawing the unit circle and marking reference angles — the spatial memory locks in faster.
  • For production formulae: 'Sin plus sin → 2 sin half-sum cos half-diff'; 'Cos plus cos → 2 cos half-sum cos half-diff'. The pattern is always: the function that appears in the sum (sin or cos) stays outside as the product, and the other function goes inside. Practice deriving them once from the addition formulas to internalise.
  • For tan⁻¹ identities: remember the '1−xy' denominator as the gatekeeper — if xy < 1 the basic formula holds; if xy > 1 you add or subtract π depending on the signs of x and y. Think of it as a 'cross-multiplication threshold'.
  • De Moivre's theorem: think 'power on the outside becomes coefficient on the inside' — (cis θ)ⁿ = cis nθ. Write 'cis' for cos θ + i sin θ as shorthand; it makes the theorem notationally lighter and reduces errors during derivation.

Exam tips

  • JEE Advanced trigonometry questions often combine 2–3 topics in a single problem. For example, a question may use De Moivre's theorem to derive a trigonometric identity, then apply it to solve a triangle. Practice hybrid problems rather than isolated topic drills.
  • When a question asks for the number of solutions in a given interval, draw the graphs of both sides (or sketch y = sin x and y = kx/π type intersections). Graphical intuition dramatically speeds up counting and eliminates algebraic errors.
  • For inverse trigonometric function questions, always verify the domain of x before applying any identity. Questions like 'find x if sin⁻¹x + sin⁻¹(2x) = π/2' require |x| ≤ 1/2 simultaneously; solving without this constraint gives extraneous answers.
  • Memorise the exact values: sin 18°, cos 18°, sin 36°, cos 36°, sin 72°, cos 72°. JEE Advanced has asked direct numerical questions using these, especially in combination with multiple-angle formulae.
  • For triangle problems, if you're stuck algebraically, try a specific case (equilateral, right-angled, isosceles) to eliminate options in MCQs. This works especially well in multi-correct questions where you need to verify each option independently.
  • Use the substitution t = tan(θ/2) (Weierstrass substitution) judiciously — it converts any rational trigonometric expression into a rational function of t, but introduces extraneous solutions at θ = π + 2nπ where tan(θ/2) is undefined. Always check this point separately.
  • For maximum/minimum questions involving trigonometric expressions, consider rewriting everything in terms of a single function (usually sin θ or cos θ) using Pythagorean identities before differentiating. The AM-GM inequality also frequently applies to expressions like sin θ + cosec θ.
  • In JEE Advanced, numerical value type questions on trigonometry often have answers that are simple fractions or radicals. If your answer looks like an ugly decimal, re-examine your approach — there's likely a cleaner identity or geometric interpretation you missed.

Common traps

  • Assuming sin⁻¹(sin θ) = θ without checking the domain. If θ = 5π/6, sin⁻¹(sin 5π/6) = sin⁻¹(1/2) = π/6, not 5π/6. Always reduce θ to the principal branch [−π/2, π/2] using symmetry relations.
  • Applying tan⁻¹x + tan⁻¹y = tan⁻¹((x+y)/(1−xy)) blindly when xy > 1. If x = 2, y = 3, then xy = 6 > 1 and the correct answer is π + tan⁻¹(5/−5) = π − π/4 = 3π/4, not tan⁻¹(−1) = −π/4. Always check the condition xy < 1 first.
  • Writing general solution as θ = nπ + (−1)ⁿα and then substituting n = 0, 1, 2... to get specific values without considering that different n values may give the same angle modulo 2π. This double-counts or misses solutions in bounded intervals.
  • Using the Law of Sines a/sin A = b/sin B to find an angle and then choosing the wrong angle (acute vs obtuse) when sin A = sin(π−A). If the problem gives additional constraints (like 'A is obtuse' or 'a > b'), use them to resolve the ambiguity.
  • Forgetting that cos⁻¹x is a decreasing function while sin⁻¹x and tan⁻¹x are increasing. This matters when comparing values like sin⁻¹(1/3) vs cos⁻¹(1/3) — since cos⁻¹(1/3) = π/2 − sin⁻¹(1/3), you immediately know cos⁻¹(1/3) > sin⁻¹(1/3).
  • Writing the maximum/minimum of a sin θ + b cos θ + c without verifying that |c| ≤ √(a²+b²) for real solutions to exist. If |c| > √(a²+b²), the expression never reaches zero and the extremum analysis changes.
  • Confusing r (inradius) and R (circumradius) in formulas. r = 4R sin(A/2)sin(B/2)sin(C/2) is easy to misremember as cos instead of sin. Remember: inradius involves half-angles with sine; Euler's formula OI² = R(R−2r) requires R > 2r for a valid triangle (which is always true).
  • In inverse trig problems involving compositions like sin⁻¹(cos x), converting directly without finding the reference angle in the correct quadrant. Use the identity cos x = sin(π/2 − x) and then bring π/2 − x into [−π/2, π/2] by adding/subtracting multiples of 2π or using symmetry.
