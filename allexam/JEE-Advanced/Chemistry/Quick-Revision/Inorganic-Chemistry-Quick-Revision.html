Inorganic Chemistry — Complete Quick Revision for JEE Advanced

JEE AdvancedEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • s-Block Elements: Elements of Group 1 (alkali metals) and Group 2 (alkaline earth metals) characterized by filling of ns orbital; highly reactive metals with low ionization energies.
  • p-Block Elements: Elements of Groups 13–18 where the last electron enters a p orbital; exhibit variable oxidation states, catenation (especially C, Si), and show both metallic and non-metallic character across a period.
  • d-Block Elements (Transition Metals): Elements of Groups 3–12 where d orbitals are progressively filled; display variable oxidation states, coloured ions, catalytic properties, and formation of coordination compounds due to small size, high charge density and vacant d orbitals.
  • f-Block Elements (Inner Transition Metals): Lanthanoids (4f series, Ce–Lu) and Actinoids (5f series, Th–Lr); characterized by progressive filling of f orbitals, show lanthanoid contraction, and exhibit paramagnetism and colour in solution.
  • Coordination Compound: A neutral or charged complex consisting of a central metal atom/ion bonded to surrounding ligands through coordinate covalent bonds; described by coordination number, geometry, and ligand type.
  • Hybridisation (Valence Bond Theory): Mixing of atomic orbitals of comparable energy on the same atom to form new equivalent hybrid orbitals; common types for coordination chemistry are sp³ (tetrahedral), dsp² (square planar), sp³d (trigonal bipyramidal), d²sp³ (octahedral inner-orbital), and sp³d² (octahedral outer-orbital).
  • IUPAC Nomenclature of Coordination Compounds: Ligands named alphabetically before the metal; anionic ligands end in -o (chlorido, cyano, oxido); neutral ligands retain special names (ammine, aqua, carbonyl); oxidation state of metal indicated in Roman numerals in parentheses; overall charge written as superscript if ionic.
  • Lanthanoid Contraction: The steady decrease in atomic and ionic radii across the lanthanoid series (La to Lu) due to poor shielding by 4f electrons, leading to nearly identical sizes of Zr-Hf, Nb-Ta, Mo-W pairs.
  • Catenation: The ability of an element to form chains or rings by bonding to itself; maximum in carbon (C-C bond energy ~348 kJ/mol), significant in silicon and sulfur, decreasing down the group.
  • Allotropy: Existence of an element in two or more different physical forms having similar chemical properties but different structural arrangements; e.g., O₂ and O₃ (ozone), P₄ (white) and red/black phosphorus, S₈ (rhombic) and monoclinic sulfur.

Key concepts

  • Ionization enthalpy decreases down the group due to increasing atomic size; however, Be > Mg < Ca > Sr > Ba shows anomaly at Mg due to filled 3s². Hydration energy decreases down the group. Solubility of sulfates decreases down Group 1 (Li₂SO₄ < Na₂SO₄ > K₂SO₄ > Rb₂SO₄ > Cs₂SO₄) and increases down Group 2 (BeSO₄ > MgSO₄ > CaSO₄ > SrSO₄ > BaSO₄). Carbonate thermal stability increases down both groups. Li shows diagonal relationship with Mg: both form nitrides (Li₃N, Mg₃N₂), their bicarbonates exist only in solution, and both carbonates decompose on heating.
  • Na₂O₂ + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂O₂ (peroxide gives H₂O₂ with water). Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH. Na₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + 2NaOH (Solvay process step). NaCl + NH₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → NaHCO₃↓ + NH₄Cl (Solvay — NaHCO₃ precipitates due to common ion effect). NH₄Cl cannot be used in Solvay because it would precipitate NaHCO₃ along with NH₄HCO₃. KO₂ + 2H₂O → KOH + H₂O₂ + O₂ (superoxide liberates O₂ — used in oxygen cylinders for space/submarines). LiAlH₄ is a powerful reducing agent (reduces esters, carboxylic acids, amides). NaBH₄ is milder (reduces only aldehydes/ketones). BaO₂ + H₂SO₄(dil) → BaSO₄↓ + H₂O₂.
  • Boron exists as B₁₂ icosahedra in α-rhombohedral and β-rhombohedral forms; crystalline boron is extremely hard (9.5 on Mohs scale). B₂H₆ (diborane) has 3 center-2 electron (3c-2e) bonds — two bridging H atoms. BF₃ is a strong Lewis acid due to back bonding from F(2p)→B(2p) being partially ineffective; BCl₃ > BF₃ in Lewis acidity toward hard bases (NH₃) because B-F back bond is stronger. Borax bead test: Co borate gives blue CoBO₂ + CoO (blue bead) on oxidizing flame. Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O + H₂SO₄ → 4H₃BO₃ + Na₂SO₄ + 5H₂O. 2Al + 2NaOH + 6H₂O → 2Na[Al(OH)₄] + 3H₂↑ (aluminate formation — amphoteric nature). Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O.
  • Diamond: sp³, 3D network, hardest natural substance, insulator. Graphite: sp², layered, conducts electricity, soft. Fullerenes (C₆₀): truncated icosahedron, 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons. CO is a stronger field ligand than CO₂; CO binds to hemoglobin 200× more strongly than O₂. SiO₂ exists as quartz (3D network of SiO₄ tetrahedra); each Si bonded to 4 O, each O shared between 2 Si. Silicates classified by sharing of O atoms: orthosilicates (isolated SiO₄⁴⁻), pyrosilicates (Si₂O₇⁶⁻), chain silicates (single/double), sheet silicates (mica, talc), 3D silicates (feldspar, quartz). PbO₂ is a strong oxidizing agent in acidic medium: PbO₂ + 4HCl → PbCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O.
  • NH₃: pyramidal, sp³, lone pair on N; H-bonding makes it liquid at room temperature. N₂: triple bond (one σ + two π), very inert due to high bond dissociation energy (941 kJ/mol). NO is a free radical (11 valence e⁻). HNO₃ is a strong oxidizing agent; with Cu: Cu + 4HNO₃(conc.) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O; with dilute: 3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O. PH₃: prepared by Ca₃P₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2PH₃; white P + excess NaOH + H₂O → PH₃ + NaH₂PO₂ (hypophosphite). H₃PO₃ is dibasic (2 OH groups); H₃PO₄ is tribasic. P₄ + 3NaOH + 3H₂O → PH₃ + 3NaH₂PO₂.
  • H₂O: bent (104.5°), hydrogen bonding, highest boiling point in group. H₂O₂: non-planar open-book structure (94.8° dihedral angle in gas); acts as oxidizing and reducing agent. H₂O₂ + MnO₄⁻ + H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + O₂ + H₂O (purple decolorization test). SO₂: bent, sp², dipole-dipole interactions; reducing agent: SO₂ + 2Fe³⁺ + 2H₂O → SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ + 4H⁺. H₂SO₄: contact process (V₂O₅ catalyst, 450°C, 2 atm) — SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄. Oleum: H₂S₂O₇ (fuming sulfuric acid). SO₂ decolorizes acidified KMnO₄ and K₂Cr₂O₇ (turns green). SF₆ is kinetically inert despite being thermodynamically unstable — steric protection by 6 F atoms prevents nucleophilic attack.
  • F₂: strongest oxidizing agent (E° = +2.87 V) due to high hydration energy of F⁻ and weak F-F bond (159 kJ/mol). I₂: weakest oxidizing agent among halogens. HOCl (hypochlorous acid) is the active bleaching agent — bleaching requires moisture: Cl₂ + H₂O → HOCl + HCl. KI + starch turns blue in presence of oxidizing agents (I₂ liberated). Cl₂ + cold dilute NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O; hot conc. NaOH → 3NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O. X₂ + H₂O → HX + HOX (except F₂ which gives HF + O₂ + O₃). HBr and HI reduce H₂SO₄ to SO₂, S, or H₂S respectively (reducing power order: HF < HCl < HBr < HI).
  • He: used in cryogenics (lowest boiling point 4.2 K), diving mixtures (He-O₂ to avoid nitrogen narcosis). XeF₂: linear (sp³d), XeF₄: square planar (sp³d²), XeF₆: distorted octahedral (sp³d³ — monomer in gas, tetramer in solid). XeF₆ + SiF₄ → [XeF₅]⁺[SiF₆]⁻. XeO₃: pyramidal, strong oxidizing agent, explosive when dry. Noble gases form clathrates ( xenon hydrate Xe·5.75H₂O) — trapped in cavity of water lattice. Ionization enthalpies of noble gases decrease down the group.
  • All transition metals have higher melting points than s-block (due to involvement of (n-1)d electrons in metallic bonding), except Zn, Cd, Hg (filled d¹⁰). First row 3d series: Sc(1541°C) to Zn(420°C); peak at Cr(1907°C) and Fe(1538°C). Standard reduction potentials: Mn³⁺/Mn²⁺ = +1.57 V (most positive due to stable d⁵ → d⁶), while Zn²⁺/Zn = -0.76 V. Color arises from d-d transitions (requires unpaired d electrons): Ti³⁺(d¹, violet), Cu²⁺(d⁹, blue), Fe²⁺(d⁶, pale green), Fe³⁺(d⁵, pale violet/yellow), Mn²⁺(d⁵, pale pink), Cr³⁺(d³, green/violet). Sc³⁺(d⁰) and Zn²⁺(d¹⁰) are colourless.
  • KMnO₄: purple, strong oxidant. Acidic: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (Mn goes +7→+2). Neutral/weak alkaline: MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂ + 4OH⁻ (Mn goes +7→+4, brown ppt). Strongly alkaline: MnO₄⁻ + e⁻ → MnO₄²⁻ (green manganate, +7→+6). Equivalent mass in acid = M/5, neutral = M/3, alkaline = M/1. K₂Cr₂O₇: orange, strong oxidant (weaker than KMnO₄). In acid: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O (orange→green, Cr +6→+3). Both turn acidic Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ and I⁻ to I₂. K₂Cr₂O₇ can oxidize Fe²⁺ directly; KMnO₄ cannot oxidize Cl⁻ in presence of HCl (Cl₂ liberated — why H₂SO₄ used instead of HCl for acidification).
  • CrO₄²⁻ (chromate, yellow) ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻ (dichromate, orange) + H₂O; equilibrium shifts to dichromate in acidic medium and to chromate in alkaline medium. Ag₂CrO₄ is brick-red ppt (used in Mohr's method). MnO₄²⁻ (manganate, green) disproportionates in acidic medium: 3MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂ + 2H₂O. MnO₄⁻ is purple, diamagnetic. VO₂⁺ (vanadyl, yellow) → VO²⁺ (blue) → V³⁺ (green) → V²⁺ (violet) upon reduction.
  • General electronic config: [Xe]4f¹⁻¹⁴5d⁰⁻¹6s². Common oxidation state: +3 (Ln³⁺). La³⁺(4f⁰), Lu³⁺(4f¹⁴) are diamagnetic; others paramagnetic. Eu²⁺(4f⁷) and Yb²⁺(4f¹⁴) are also stable — strong reducing agents. Ce⁴⁺(4f⁰) is a strong oxidizing agent (E° = +1.74 V). Ln³⁺ ions show characteristic colours due to f-f transitions (lanthanoids other than f⁰ and f¹⁴). La(OH)₃ is least basic; Lu(OH)₃ is most basic among Ln(OH)₃ (due to lanthanoid contraction increasing charge density). LN³⁺ salts are used as catalysts; CeO₂ used in polishing glass and as an oxidant in organic synthesis.
  • Primary valency (ionizable) = oxidation state; Secondary valency (non-ionizable) = coordination number. [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃ → 4 ions in solution (1 complex cation + 3 Cl⁻), giving AgCl ppt with AgNO₃. [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂ → 3 ions (1 complex cation + 2 Cl⁻). [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl → 2 ions. [Co(NH₃)₃Cl₃] → 1 ion (no Cl⁻ outside, no AgCl precipitate). Werner explained the isomerism: [Co(en)₃]³⁺ shows optical isomerism (Δ and Λ forms); [MA₂B₂] type (square planar) shows geometrical cis-trans isomerism (e.g., [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] — cis is anticancer drug Cisplatin, trans is not active).
  • In octahedral field: d orbitals split into t₂g (dxy, dyz, dzx — lower energy) and eg (dx²-y², dz² — higher energy); splitting energy = Δₒ. In tetrahedral field: e (lower) and t₂ (higher); Δₜ ≈ 4/9 Δₒ (too small for pairing). Weak field ligands (I⁻, Br⁻, Cl⁻, F⁻, OH⁻, H₂O) → high spin (maximum unpaired e⁻). Strong field ligands (CN⁻, CO, NO⁺, en, NH₃) → low spin (electrons pair up). Spectrochemical series: I⁻ < Br⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < NO₃⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < CH₃CN < NH₃ < en < bipy < phen < NO₂⁻ < PPh₃ < CN⁻ < CO. For 3d⁴ in octahedral: weak field → t₂g³ eg¹ (high spin); strong field → t₂g⁴ (low spin).
  • Magnetic moment (spin-only): μ = √(n(n+2)) BM, where n = number of unpaired electrons. [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻: d⁶ low spin, n=0, μ=0 (diamagnetic, yellow). [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻: d⁵ low spin, n=1, μ=1.73 BM. [FeF₆]³⁻: d⁵ high spin, n=5, μ=5.92 BM. [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺: d⁹, n=1, μ=1.73 BM, deep blue. [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻: d⁸ low spin, square planar, diamagnetic. [NiCl₄]²⁻: d⁸ high spin, tetrahedral, paramagnetic (μ=2.83 BM). [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺: d⁶ low spin, inner orbital (d²sp³), diamagnetic.
  • [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃: Hexamminecobalt(III) chloride. [Cr(H₂O)₅Cl]Cl₂·H₂O: Pentaaquachloridochromium(III) chloride monohydrate. K₄[Fe(CN)₆]: Potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II). [PtCl₃(NH₃)]⁻: Trichloridoammineplatinate(II) ion. [Ni(CO)₄]: Tetracarbonylnickel(0). [Co(en)₃]Cl₃: Tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)cobalt(III) chloride. Na₂[Fe(CN)₅(NO)]: Sodium nitroprusside / Sodium pentacyidonitrosylferrate(II). K₃[Cr(C₂O₄)₃]: Potassium trioxalatochromate(III).
  • Mineral: naturally occurring substance containing metal. Ore: mineral from which metal can be extracted economically. Gangue: earthly impurities (silicates, sand, etc.). Concentration: gravity separation (dense ores like tin), froth flotation (sulfide ores with pine oil collector), magnetic separation (magnetic ores like magnetite), leaching (Bayer's process for bauxite — Al₂O₃·2H₂O + NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O; then CO₂ → Al(OH)₃ ↓; calcined to Al₂O₃). Ellingham diagram: plots ΔG vs T for oxide formation; more negative ΔG = more stable oxide. Metal with more negative ΔG can reduce oxide of metal with less negative ΔG.
  • Iron (Blast furnace): hematite + coke + limestone → pig iron → cast iron → steel. Zones: combustion (top, C + O₂ → CO₂), reduction (middle, Fe₂O₃ + CO → Fe + CO₂ at 500–800°C), fusion (bottom, C + CO₂ → 2CO at 1200–1500°C, Fe melts). Slag: CaSiO₃ (CaO + SiO₂). Pig iron contains ~4% C and impurities (S, P, Si, Mn). Copper (roasting sulfide ore → Cu₂S; self-reduction: Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂). Purification: electrolytic refining; blister copper (99.5%). Copper sulfate pentahydrate: CuSO₄·5H₂O — 4 H₂O coordinated, 1 H₂O hydrogen-bonded to O of SO₄²⁻ (structure confirmed by X-ray).
  • H₂ preparation: Zn + H₂SO₄(dil) → ZnSO₄ + H₂. Industrial: steam reforming of natural gas (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂ at 800–900°C over Ni catalyst — 'syngas'); water-gas shift: CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂. Heavy water (D₂O): produced by prolonged electrolysis of water (light water enriched in D). Deuterium exchange: D₂O + ND₃ ↔ ND₂OD + HDO. Hydrides: saline (NaH, CaH₂ — ionic, release H₂ with water), covalent (BH₃, CH₄ — molecular), metallic/interstitial (TiH, PdH — non-stoichiometric, absorb H₂). H₂O₂ used as rocket fuel oxidizer, bleach, and antiseptic.

Important facts

  • Li is the strongest reducing agent in aqueous solution (E° = -3.04 V) despite having the highest first IE among alkali metals — due to highest hydration enthalpy of Li⁺.
  • Be is passivated by HNO₃ due to formation of protective oxide layer; does not react with concentrated HNO₃.
  • Na₂O₂ is paramagnetic (contains O₂²⁻ with 2 unpaired electrons in antibonding π* orbitals).
  • BeO and Al₂O₃ are amphoteric; rest of Group 2 oxides are basic.
  • Boron does not form B³⁺ ion — too high sum of first three ionization energies; forms covalent compounds only.
  • Borax (Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O) heated loses water to form anhydrous borax (glassy bead — NaBO₂ + B₂O₃ mixture).
  • Beryllium halides (BeCl₂) are covalent, soluble in organic solvents; polymeric in solid (chain structure with Cl bridges).
  • AlCl₃ exists as dimer Al₂Cl₆ with two Cl-bridges; acts as Lewis acid in Friedel-Crafts reaction.
  • SiCl₄ hydrolyzes readily (empty 3d orbitals accept H₂O); CCl₄ does not hydrolyze (no d orbitals, steric hindrance).
  • PbO₂ is a strong oxidizing agent; oxidizes Mn²⁺ to MnO₄⁻ in acidic medium (purple color).
  • N₂ is inert at room temperature due to triple bond (bond order 3); reacts with Mg on heating to form Mg₃N₂.
  • NO is paramagnetic (unpaired electron); N₂O is diamagnetic. NO dimerizes to N₂O₂ only at low temperature.
  • White phosphorus (P₄) is soluble in CS₂ and glows in dark (chemiluminescence — 'phosphorescence'). Red phosphorus is insoluble in CS₂.
  • H₂S is passed through acidic medium (not basic) for qualitative analysis of cations to prevent precipitation of Group IV carbonates.
  • O₃ (ozone) decomposes to O₂ on heating; bleaches by oxidation; used as germicide and disinfectant.
  • HF is stored in wax-coated copper vessels (attacks glass); HI is stored in dark bottles (photo-decomposes to I₂ and H₂).
  • ClO₂ is used as bleaching agent for paper pulp — does not form organochlorines unlike Cl₂.
  • XePtF₆ was the first noble gas compound prepared by Neil Bartlett (1962).
  • [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ is called ferricyanide (Fe³⁺); [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ is ferrocyanide (Fe²⁺).
  • Cisplatin [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] is used as anti-cancer drug; transplatin is inactive.
  • EDTA⁴⁻ is a hexadentate ligand (6 donor atoms); forms very stable chelate complexes with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ (used in hardness estimation).
  • Magnetic moment of Cr³⁺ (d³) = 3.87 BM; Mn²⁺ (d⁵ high spin) = 5.92 BM.
  • Cu₂O is red (cuprous oxide); CuO is black (cupric oxide). Cu₂O is diamagnetic; CuO is paramagnetic.
  • HgCl₂ is corrosive sublimate (soluble, poisonous); Hg₂Cl₂ is calomel (insoluble, mild antiseptic). Hg₂Cl₂ + NH₃ → HgNH₂Cl(black) + Hg + NH₄Cl.
  • Ni(CO)₄ is tetrahedral (sp³), diamagnetic; used in Mond's process for purification of nickel (decomposition at 230°C gives pure Ni).
  • AgBr is used in photographic film (photosensitive: AgBr → Ag + Br₂ on exposure to light).
  • Prussian blue = Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃; Turnbull's blue = Fe₃[Fe(CN)₆]₂ — both have same structure.
  • KMnO₄ standardization is done against oxalic acid in acidic medium: 2MnO₄⁻ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O.
  • K₂Cr₂O₇ is preferred over KMnO₄ in volumetric analysis because it is more stable, does not oxidize HCl, and has sharp endpoint.

Memory aids

  • Alkali metal peroxide: Na₂O₂ — 'Na loves oxygen' (two O atoms per two Na). Superoxide: KO₂ — 'Potassium holds one O₂ unit loosely'.
  • Borax bead test mnemonic: 'Co gives blue, Cr gives green, Mn gives violet/amethyst.'
  • Spectrochemical series shortcut: 'I Brought Cl F O Came N Ever Better Phenomenally Cool' → I⁻ Br⁻ Cl⁻ F⁻ OH⁻ C₂O₄²⁻ H₂O NH₃ en bipy phen NO₂⁻ CN⁻ CO.
  • d-orbital splitting memory: In octahedral, eg goes up (like elevated platform), t₂g goes down (terrace ground level). Mnemonic: 'Excellent girls go Up, Tall boys Go Down.'
  • Halogens oxidizing power order (top to bottom decreases): F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ — remember 'Fluorine Fights Chlorine For Bromine's Island.'
  • Nitrogen family hydrides basicity: NH₃ > PH₃ > AsH₃ > SbH₃ > BiH₃ — size increases, electron density on central atom decreases, lone pair less available.
  • Brown ring test for NO₃⁻: FeSO₄ + conc. H₂SO₄ layer — [Fe(H₂O)₅(NO)]²⁺ (brown). Mnemonic: 'Iron Nitrate makes Brown Ring.'
  • KMnO₄ equivalent masses: Acid(M/5), Neutral(M/3), Alkaline(M/1) — remember 'A-N-A' gives 5-3-1.
  • K₂Cr₂O₇ tests: Acidifies to orange; with K⁺ + H₂SO₄ + warm → red CrO₂Cl₂ vapours (chromyl chloride test — confirms Cl⁻).
  • Lanthanoid contraction consequence: Zr and Hf have nearly same size → similar chemistry → difficult separation. Same for Nb-Ta and Mo-W.
  • Group 15 hydrides thermal stability: NH₃ > PH₃ > AsH₃ > SbH₃ > BiH₃ — decreases down the group as M-H bond strength decreases.
  • P₄O₁₀ is the anhydride of H₃PO₄; P₄O₆ is the anhydride of H₃PO₃.
  • Sulfur allotropes: Rhombic (stable below 369 K, α-sulfur, octahedral crystals) and Monoclinic (stable above 369 K, β-sulfur, needle-like). Conversion: rhombic S → monoclinic S at 369 K (transition temperature).

Exam tips

  • JEE Advanced frequently asks matching questions on coordination compounds: match complex with hybridization, geometry, magnetic behavior, and isomerism type — practice tabulating [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺, [CoF₆]³⁻, [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻, [NiCl₄]²⁻, [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻.
  • s-Block diagonal relationship (Li-Mg) is a perennial JEE favorite — expect questions on LiCl being covalent, LiNO₃ decomposing to Li₂O (unlike other nitrates giving NO₂ + O₂), and LiHCO₃ not existing in solid state.
  • p-Block questions often combine multiple concepts: structure (B₂H₆ 3c-2e bonds), acid-base behavior (BF₃ vs BCl₃), and hydrolysis patterns. Practice drawing structures of B₂H₆, P₄, S₈, and XeF₄.
  • For d-Block: know the number of unpaired electrons for all 3d ions (Sc³⁺ to Zn²⁺) in both high-spin and low-spin configurations. Memorize colors of common ions.
  • Coordination chemistry numericals: calculate crystal field stabilization energy (CFSE) for given d-electron configurations in octahedral/tetrahedral fields using Δₒ and pairing energy P.
  • Metallurgy — focus on Ellingham diagram interpretation: which metals can reduce which oxides at what temperatures. Understand why Al reduces Cr₂O₃ but not vice versa.
  • Qualitative analysis (salt analysis) integrates s, p, and d block knowledge: learn confirmatory tests for cations (NH₄⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺, Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺) and anions (CO₃²⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻).
  • HYDROGEN chapter: distinguish between ionic/covalent/metallic hydrides. Know preparation methods, properties of H₂O and H₂O₂ (structure, reactions, uses), and heavy water properties.
  • p-Block nitrogen compounds: distinguish between NO, NO₂, N₂O₄, N₂O, HNO₂, HNO₃. Know laboratory and industrial preparation methods. NO is a radical; NO₂ dimerizes to N₂O₄ on cooling.
  • sulfur compounds: H₂SO₄ manufacture (contact process conditions), all oxides of sulfur (SO₂, SO₃, S₂O), thiosulfate (hypo — Na₂S₂O₃) structure and reactions (with AgBr in photography fixing).
  • Remember: JEE Advanced increasingly frames inorganic questions in assertion-reason and multi-correct format. Practice reasoning-based questions, not just factual recall.

Common traps

  • Assuming all d-block elements show variable oxidation states: Zn, Cd, Hg (d¹⁰ configuration) do NOT show variable oxidation states — they are always +2 (though Hg also shows +1 as Hg₂²⁺).
  • Thinking Zn is a transition element: Zn is NOT a transition metal by IUPAC definition (defined d subshell incomplete in atom or common ion) — it has d¹⁰ in both Zn atom and Zn²⁺ ion.
  • Confusing BF₃ and BCl₃ Lewis acid strength: BF₃ is actually weaker than BCl₃ toward NH₃ because of stronger pπ-pπ back bonding in B-F (both 2p orbitals) vs B-Cl (2p-3p mismatch).
  • Assuming all oxides of nitrogen are paramagnetic: NO and NO₂ are paramagnetic (unpaired electrons); N₂O, N₂O₃, N₂O₄, N₂O₅ are diamagnetic.
  • Forgetting that F₂ is the only halogen that oxidizes water: F₂ + H₂O → HF + O₂ (or O₃); other halogens undergo disproportionation in water.
  • Mixing up Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ tests: Fe²⁺ with K₃[Fe(CN)₆] gives Turnbull's blue ppt; Fe³⁺ with K₄[Fe(CN)₆] gives Prussian blue ppt. Both are essentially the same compound.
  • Assuming all transition metal compounds are coloured: Sc³⁺ (d⁰) and Zn²⁺ (d¹⁰) compounds are colourless because no d-d transitions possible.
  • Confusing CO and NO as ligands: CO is neutral (carbonyl); NO can be neutral (nitrosyl) or NO⁺ (nitrosonium, isoelectronic with CO) — in nitroprusside, NO is present as NO⁺.
  • Mistaking K₂Cr₂O₇ solution colour: It is orange, not red. Orange/red dichromate should not be confused with blood-red FeSCN²⁺ or brick-red Ag₂CrO₄.
  • Assuming H₃PO₃ is tribasic: It is dibasic (only 2 OH groups; the third H is directly bonded to P and does not ionize). Structure: HP(O)(OH)₂.
  • Thinking SiO₂ dissolves in water: SiO₂ is insoluble in water but dissolves in HF (forms SiF₄ or H₂SiF₆) and hot conc. NaOH (forms Na₂SiO₃).
  • Confusing the direction of Ellingham diagram: Lines slope upward (ΔG becomes less negative with increasing T). A metal can reduce an oxide whose line is above it on the diagram (at that temperature).
  • Assuming all lanthanoids are trivalent: Eu and Yb also show +2 state; Ce and Tb show +4 state. These exceptions relate to stability of f⁰, f⁷, f¹⁴ configurations.
  • Wrong assumption about Ni(CO)₄ geometry: It is tetrahedral (Ni is in 0 oxidation state, d¹⁰, sp³ hybridization) — NOT square planar.
  • Misidentifying the brown ring complex: It is [Fe(H₂O)₅(NO)]²⁺ where NO is present as NO⁺ (nitrosonium) and Fe is in +1 oxidation state (d⁷) — not Fe²⁺ with neutral NO.
