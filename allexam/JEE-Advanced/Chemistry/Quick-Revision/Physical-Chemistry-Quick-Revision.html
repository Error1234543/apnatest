Physical Chemistry — Complete Quick Revision for JEE Advanced

JEE AdvancedEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Mole (n): The amount of substance containing exactly Avogadro's number (N_A = 6.022×10²³) of elementary entities. It connects macroscopic mass to microscopic particle count.
  • Equivalent (Eq): The amount of a substance that reacts with or supplies exactly 1 mole of H⁺ ions, electrons, or charged species. Equivalent weight = Molar mass / n-factor.
  • Oxidation Number (O.N.): The hypothetical charge an atom would have if all bonds were 100% ionic. Used to identify oxidation-reduction processes.
  • Enthalpy of Reaction (Δ_rH): The heat absorbed or evolved at constant pressure. Δ_rH = Σ(n_p × Δ_fH_products) − Σ(n_r × Δ_fH_reactants).
  • Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG): The maximum non-expansion work obtainable from a process at constant T and P. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; at equilibrium ΔG = 0.
  • Entropy (S): A measure of the dispersal of energy and matter; a state function quantifying disorder. ΔS_universe > 0 for spontaneous processes.
  • Chemical Equilibrium: The state where forward and reverse reaction rates are equal, and concentrations remain constant (not necessarily equal). K_eq is temperature-dependent only.
  • Acid Dissociation Constant (K_a): For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, K_a = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. pK_a = −log₁₀K_a. Stronger acids have larger K_a and smaller pK_a.
  • Solubility Product (K_sp): For solid AB(s) ⇌ A⁺(aq) + B⁻(aq), K_sp = [A⁺][B⁻] at saturation. Precipitation occurs when Q_sp > K_sp.
  • Standard Electrode Potential (E°): The potential difference between an electrode and the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE) under standard conditions (1 M, 1 bar, 298 K).
  • Rate Constant (k): The proportionality factor in the rate law, Rate = k[A]^m[B]^n. Its value is independent of concentration but depends exponentially on temperature via the Arrhenius equation.
  • Half-life (t_½): Time for the reactant concentration to fall to half its initial value. For first order: t_½ = ln2/k (independent of [A]_0).
  • Colligative Property: A property depending only on the number of solute particles, not their identity. Includes lowering of vapour pressure, elevation of boiling point, depression of freezing point, and osmotic pressure.
  • Adsorption: The accumulation of molecules of a substance at the surface rather than in the bulk. Physisorption involves van der Waals forces; chemisorption involves chemical bond formation.
  • Activity (a): The effective concentration used in place of molarity in the expression for K or μ. For ideal solutions, a ≈ concentration; for real systems, a = γ·c where γ is the activity coefficient.
  • Fugacity (f): The 'effective pressure' of a real gas, defined so that μ = μ° + RT ln(f/f°). For an ideal gas, fugacity equals pressure.

Important facts

  • N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 1.987 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; F = 96485 C mol⁻¹; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; c = 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
  • At 298 K: 2.303RT/F = 0.0591 V; RT/F = 0.02569 V.
  • Standard conditions (IUPAC since 1982): P° = 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa, T = 298.15 K commonly used. Old standard: P° = 1 atm = 101325 Pa.
  • 水的离子积: K_w = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. K_w increases with temperature (autoionisation is endothermic).
  • K_b(water) = 0.512 K kg mol⁻¹; K_f(water) = 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹.
  • MOSFET relation for first-order: fraction remaining after n half-lives = (1/2)^n.
  • For ideal gas: U depends only on T; H depends only on T. For real gases, U also depends on V (intermolecular forces).
  • Carnot efficiency: η = 1 − T_cold/T_hot (temperatures in Kelvin). This is the maximum theoretical efficiency of any heat engine.
  • ΔG° relates to K by ln K = −ΔG°/(RT). A reaction is spontaneous under standard conditions when ΔG° < 0, i.e., K > 1.
  • For electrochemical cells: E°_cell > 0 means spontaneous; E°_cell < 0 means non-spontaneous (needs electrolysis).
  • For FCC crystals: relationship between atomic radius r and edge a is a = 2√2 r. For BCC: a = 4r/√3. For SC: a = 2r.
  • In NaCl structure, the largest sphere that fits in the octahedral void has radius ratio r_void/r_atom = 0.414. CsCl has CN=8:8 and requires r_void/r_atom ≥ 0.732.

Memory aids

  • Order of subshell filling (Aufbau): use the diagonal rule — draw arrows starting from 1s diagonally down-left: 1s→2s→2p→3s→3p→4s→3d→4p→5s→4d→5p→6s→4f→5d→6p→7s→5f→6d→7p.
  • pH calculation shortcut: pH = −log C for strong acids; pH = ½pK_a − ½log C for weak acids (when α ≪ 1).
  • Remember the sign convention with 'WON': Work ON system is positive; Heat ON system is positive. Or: ΔU = q + w (IUPAC convention).
  • Fajan's rules mnemonic: 'Small big charges make covalent bonds' — small cation, big anion, high charge → more covalent character.
  • Le Chatelier quick guide: Pressure up → shift to fewer gas moles; Temperature up → shift to absorb heat (endothermic direction).
  • Electrode potentials: the more positive E°, the stronger the oxidising agent (the species gets reduced). The more negative E°, the stronger the reducing agent (the species gets oxidised).
  • Colloid stability: 'Like charges repel' — lyophobic colloids are stabilized by charge; adding oppositely charged ions causes coagulation (Hardy-Schulze).
  • van't Hoff factor: i = 1 for nonelectrolytes (glucose, urea); i = number of ions for strong electrolytes (NaCl → 2, CaCl₂ → 3); i < 1 for association (acetic acid dimer in benzene).
  • Arrhenius memory: 'k increases exponentially with T and decreases with E_a'. High T or low E_a → faster reaction.
  • Bohr energy formula: remember 'negative and proportional to Z²/n²' — bound states have negative energy, deeper for higher Z, shallower for higher n.

Exam tips

  • JEE Advanced frequently combines multiple physical chemistry concepts in one problem. Practice multi-concept problems: e.g., a gas expansion followed by a reaction, or an equilibrium problem requiring thermodynamic data.
  • Units are critical. Always check consistency: use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for energy calculations, R = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for pressure-volume work in atm-L units.
  • For numerical answer type questions, maintain appropriate significant figures. JEE Advanced typically expects answers to 2-3 decimal places or the nearest integer depending on the question.
  • Master logarithmic calculations — they appear everywhere (pH, kinetics, Nernst, equilibrium). Know that log 2 ≈ 0.301, log 3 ≈ 0.477, log 5 ≈ 0.699, ln 2 ≈ 0.693.
  • For matrix-match and multi-correct questions, verify EACH option independently. Do not assume an option is wrong because another seems correct.
  • Time management: Physical Chemistry is calculation-heavy. Practice setting up problems quickly. Skip and return to very long numericals if time is running short.
  • For JEE Advanced specifically, expect questions that require combining Thermodynamics with Equilibrium (ΔG° → K), Electrochemistry with Kinetics (concentration cells), or Kinetics with Nuclear decay.
  • Always draw a labeled diagram when the problem involves electrochemical cells, phase diagrams, or crystal structures — this prevents sign errors and helps organise information.
  • Memorize standard values: 0.0591 V (Nernst at 298K), 2.303RT/F, ln 2 = 0.693, RT at 298K = 2.479 kJ/mol. These eliminate calculation errors.
  • For surface chemistry and solid state, focus on numerical problems involving density, packing fraction, void sizes, and defect concentrations — these are directly calculable and frequently asked.
  • When solving equilibrium problems, always verify your answer: substitute back into K expression. For kinetics, verify that units of k match the overall order.
  • Read the question carefully for conditions: constant T vs constant P, ideal vs real gas, standard vs non-standard conditions. These distinctions determine which formula applies.

Common traps

  • Confusing K_p and K_c: always check Δn_g. If Δn_g = 0, K_p = K_c. If you forget the (RT)^Δn term, your answer will be wrong by orders of magnitude.
  • Using ΔG = ΔH − TΔS without checking if T is in Kelvin. Celsius must always be converted to Kelvin for thermodynamic calculations.
  • Assuming ΔH = ΔU for all processes. They differ by Δn_g RT for reactions involving gases. Only equal when Δn_g = 0 or for condensed phases only.
  • Forgetting that activity coefficients deviate from 1 in real solutions. In JEE Advanced, assume ideal behavior unless the problem explicitly states otherwise, but know when deviations occur.
  • Confusing half-life of first-order (constant) with half-life of second-order (depends on initial concentration). This is a frequent trap.
  • Applying Henderson-Hasselbalch to strong acids or extremely dilute weak acids where the approximation [HA] ≈ C breaks down. Check the 5% rule.
  • Treating K_sp as having units. K_sp is technically dimensionless (based on activities), but numerically equals the product of molar concentrations in mol L⁻¹. Don't assign units to K_sp.
  • Forgetting that adding an inert gas at constant volume does NOT shift equilibrium (partial pressures unchanged), but adding at constant pressure DOES shift equilibrium.
  • Confusing E°_cell sign conventions. E°_cell = E°_cathode − E°_anode (both as reduction potentials). A positive E°_cell means the reaction as written is spontaneous.
  • Assuming catalysts shift equilibrium position. Catalysts only speed up reaching equilibrium; they do not change K, ΔG°, or yield.
  • Mixing up physisorption and chemisorption temperature dependence. Physisorption decreases with T (exothermic); chemisorption may increase then decrease (activation barrier + exothermic).
  • Incorrectly applying ideal gas law to real gases at high P/low T. Use van der Waals equation when specified: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.
  • Forgetting the i (van't Hoff factor) in colligative property calculations for electrolytes. Using m instead of im gives wrong ΔT values.
  • Using n-factor incorrectly for redox titrations. n-factor depends on the specific reaction, not just the compound. Always write the balanced half-reactions.
