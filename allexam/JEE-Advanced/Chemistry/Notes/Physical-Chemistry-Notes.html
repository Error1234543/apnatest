1. Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry

Mole Concept and Stoichiometry: One mole contains exactly Avogadro's number (N_A = 6.022 × 10²³) of elementary entities. The molar mass of a substance (in g/mol) is numerically equal to its relative atomic/molecular mass. One mole of any ideal gas at STP (0°C, 1 atm) occupies 22.7 L (using IUPAC standard pressure of 1 bar; older convention uses 22.4 L at 1 atm). Key relationships: n = given mass / molar mass = number of particles / N_A = volume of gas at given T,P / molar volume at that T,P.

Equivalent Weight and Law of Equivalence: Equivalent weight = molar mass / n-factor. The n-factor for acids is basicity (number of replaceable H⁺), for bases is acidity (number of replaceable OH⁻), for salts is total positive/negative charge, for redox reactions is change in oxidation number per molecule. At equivalence point: N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ (for titrations). Normality N = n-factor × Molarity.

Concentration Terms: Molarity (M) = moles of solute / volume of solution in litres (temperature-dependent). Molality (m) = moles of solute / mass of solvent in kg (temperature-independent). Mole fraction (χ) = moles of component / total moles (dimensionless). Mass percentage = (mass of component / total mass) × 100. ppm = (mass of solute / mass of solution) × 10⁶. For dilute aqueous solutions, ppm ≈ mg/L.

Relationship between M and m: M = (m × d) / (1 + m × M_solute/1000), where d is density of solution in g/mL. Also, χ_B = (M/1000d) / (m + M/1000d).

Oxidation Number Rules: Free elements have ON = 0. For monatomic ions, ON equals the charge. Hydrogen is +1 (except in metal hydrides where it is –1). Oxygen is –2 (except in peroxides where it is –1, in OF₂ where it is +2, and in superoxides where it is –½). The sum of ONs in a neutral compound is 0; in an ion it equals the charge.

Balancing Redox Reactions: Ion-electron method — write separate half-reactions for oxidation and reduction, balance atoms other than O and H, balance O by adding H₂O, balance H by adding H⁺ (acidic medium) or OH⁻ (basic medium), balance charge by adding electrons, then multiply to equalize electron transfer and add. In basic medium, after balancing with H⁺, add equal OH⁻ to both sides to neutralize H⁺ to H₂O.

2. Atomic Structure

Historical Models: Thomson's plum pudding model was disproved by Rutherford's α-scattering experiment. Rutherford's model had electrons orbiting a dense positively charged nucleus but could not explain atomic stability or line spectra. Bohr's model successfully explained hydrogen spectrum but failed for multi-electron systems.

Bohr's Model — Postulates and Formulas: Electrons revolve in definite circular orbits (stationary states) without radiating energy. Angular momentum is quantized: mvr = nh/2π. Energy of electron in nth orbit of hydrogen-like species: E_n = –13.6 Z²/n² eV per atom = –2.178 × 10⁻¹⁸ Z²/n² J. Radius of nth orbit: r_n = 0.529 n²/Z Å. Velocity: v_n = 2.18 × 10⁶ Z/n m/s. Energy difference between two levels: ΔE = 13.6 Z²(1/n₁² – 1/n₂²) eV. Wave number of emitted/absorbed radiation: 1/λ = RZ²(1/n₁² – 1/n₂²), where R = 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹.

Spectral Series of Hydrogen: Lyman (n₁=1, UV), Balmer (n₁=2, visible), Paschen (n₁=3, IR), Brackett (n₁=4, IR), Pfund (n₁=5, IR). Limit of each series corresponds to n₂ → ∞.

De Broglie Wavelength: λ = h/mv = h/p. For an electron accelerated through potential V: λ = h/√(2meV) = 12.27/√V Å. Wave-particle duality implies matter has wave characteristics.

Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π (or ≥ ℏ/2). Precise simultaneous determination of position and momentum is impossible. This principle led to the replacement of 'orbit' with 'orbital'.

Quantum Numbers: Principal quantum number (n = 1,2,3,...) determines shell and size. Azimuthal quantum number (l = 0 to n–1) determines subshell shape (s,p,d,f). Magnetic quantum number (m_l = –l to +l) determines orbital orientation. Spin quantum number (m_s = +½ or –½) determines electron spin direction. Max electrons in a shell = 2n². Max electrons in a subshell = 2(2l+1).

Orbital Shapes and Node Count: s-orbitals are spherical (0 angular nodes). p-orbitals are dumbbell-shaped (1 angular node, three orientations: p_x, p_y, p_z). d-orbitals have more complex shapes (2 angular nodes, five orientations). Number of radial nodes = n – l – 1. Number of angular nodes = l. Total nodes = n – 1.

3. Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

Ionic Bond: Formed by complete transfer of one or more electrons from a electropositive atom to an electronegative atom. Lattice energy: U = (k·Z⁺·Z⁻)/ (r⁺ + r⁻), where k is a constant, Z are charges, r are ionic radii. Higher lattice energy means greater stability. Born-Haber cycle applies Hess's law to calculate lattice energy from experimental data.

Covalent Bond — VSEPR Theory: Steric number = number of σ bonds + number of lone pairs on central atom. Shapes: SN=2 → linear (sp), SN=3 → trigonal planar (sp²), SN=4 → tetrahedral (sp³), SN=5 → trigonal bipyramidal (sp³d), SN=6 → octahedral (sp³d²). Lone pair-bond pair repulsion > lone pair-lone pair repulsion > bond pair-bond pair repulsion, causing deviations from ideal bond angles. Examples: H₂O (SN=4, 2 LP → bent ~104.5°), NH₃ (SN=4, 1 LP → pyramidal ~107°), BF₃ (SN=3, 0 LP → trigonal planar 120°), SF₆ (SN=6, 0 LP → octahedral 90°).

Hybridization: Mixing of atomic orbitals of similar energies to form new equivalent hybrid orbitals. sp hybridization → linear geometry (BeCl₂, C₂H₂). sp² → trigonal planar (BF₃, C₂H₄). sp³ → tetrahedral (CH₄, NH₄⁺). sp³d → trigonal bipyramidal (PCl₅). sp³d² → octahedral (SF₆). dsp² → square planar (Ni(CN)₄²⁻).

Valence Bond Theory and Metallic Bond: Coordinate/covalent bond forms when one atom supplies both electrons. Resonance occurs when a single Lewis structure cannot represent the molecule; actual structure is a hybrid (e.g., ozone, benzene, carbonate). Greater resonance energy → greater stability.

Molecular Orbital Theory: Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals. Bonding MOs have lower energy than parent AOs; antibonding MOs have higher energy. BO = ½(N_b – N_a). Paramagnetism arises from unpaired electrons (O₂ is paramagnetic per MOT). Order of filling for diatomics up to N₂: σ1s < σ1s < σ2s < σ2s < σ2pz < π2px = π2py < π2px = π2py < σ*2pz. For O₂, F₂, Ne₂, the σ2pz lies above π2p. Bond order correlates with bond length and bond energy: higher BO → shorter bond → stronger bond.

Dipole Moment and Polarity: μ = q × d (vector quantity, unit debye = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ Cm). Net dipole depends on both bond moments and molecular geometry. CO₂ has zero net dipole (linear, symmetric); H₂O has non-zero dipole (bent). Polarizability increases with size; Fajan's rules explain covalent character in ionic compounds: small cation + large anion + high charge → more covalent character.

4. States of Matter — Gases and Liquids

Ideal Gas Equation: PV = nRT, where R = 0.0821 L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ = 1.987 cal K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Combined gas law: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂. Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures: P_total = p₁ + p₂ + p₃ + ...; partial pressure p_i = χ_i × P_total. Graham's Law of Diffusion/Effusion: r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁) = √(d₂/d₁) at constant T,P. Rate of effusion is inversely proportional to square root of molar mass.

Kinetic Theory of Gases: PV = ⅓ nmv²_rms. Root mean square speed: v_rms = √(3RT/M). Most probable speed: v_mp = √(2RT/M). Average speed: v_avg = √(8RT/πM). Ratio: v_mp : v_avg : v_rms = √2 : √(8/π) : √3 ≈ 1 : 1.128 : 1.224. Kinetic energy per mole: KE = ³⁄₂ RT. Deviation from ideal behavior increases at low temperature and high pressure.

Real Gases — van der Waals Equation: (P + a/V²)(V – b) = RT. Constant a measures intermolecular attractive forces; b measures effective molecular volume (exclusion volume). Critical constants: T_c = 8a/27Rb, P_c = a/27b², V_c = 3b. Above T_c, gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone. Joule-Thomson coefficient μ_JT = (∂T/∂P)_H. Inversion temperature: T_i = 2a/Rb.

Liquid State — Properties: Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by vapour in equilibrium with liquid at a given temperature. Clausius-Clapeyron equation: ln(P₂/P₁) = (ΔH_vap/R)(1/T₁ – 1/T₂). Vapour pressure increases with temperature and decreases with addition of non-volatile solute (Raoult's law). Surface tension decreases with temperature. Viscosity decreases with temperature (liquids) but increases with temperature (gases). Cohesive forces exist between like molecules; adhesive forces between unlike molecules.

5. Thermodynamics and Thermochemistry

First Law of Thermodynamics: ΔU = q + w, where w is work done ON the system (IUPAC sign convention). For expansion work: w = –P_ext × ΔV. In an isolated system, ΔU = 0. For an adiabatic process (q = 0): ΔU = w. For isothermal process of ideal gas: ΔU = 0, ΔH = 0, so q = –w. For reversible isothermal expansion: w = –nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = –nRT ln(P₁/P₂) = –2.303 nRT log(P₁/P₂).

Enthalpy: H = U + PV. ΔH = ΔU + Δ(PV). At constant pressure: ΔH = q_p. For ideal gas, ΔH = nC_pΔT. Relationship: C_p – C_v = R (for 1 mole of ideal gas). γ = C_p/C_v. For monoatomic gas: C_v = ³⁄₂R, C_p = ⁵⁄₂R, γ = 5/3. For diatomic: C_v = ⁵⁄₂R, C_p = ⁷⁄₂R, γ = 7/5.

Thermochemical Equations and Hess's Law: Enthalpy of reaction is independent of path. ΔH_reaction = Σ ΔH_f(products) – Σ ΔH_f(reactants). Bond enthalpy: ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) – Σ(bonds formed). Resonance energy = observed ΔH_f – calculated ΔH_f from bond energies. Heat of combustion, hydration, neutralization, and transition are standard types. ΔH_neut (strong acid + strong base) = –57.3 kJ/mol at 25°C.

Second Law and Entropy: Entropy (S) is a measure of disorder/randomness. ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings ≥ 0 (Spontaneous processes). For reversible isothermal expansion: ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = q_rev/T. ΔS_vap = ΔH_vap/T_b. ΔS_fus = ΔH_fus/T_m. Third law: entropy of perfect crystal at 0 K is zero.

Gibbs Energy and Spontaneity: ΔG = ΔH – TΔS. At constant T,P: if ΔG < 0, process is spontaneous; if ΔG = 0, system is at equilibrium; if ΔG > 0, non-spontaneous. ΔG° = –RT ln K. Relationship: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q. Temperature at which reaction becomes spontaneous: T = ΔH/ΔS (when ΔG = 0). Maximum useful work = –ΔG. Free energy function: G = H – TS. Temperature dependence: (∂ΔG/∂T)_P = –ΔS (Gibbs-Helmholtz equation form).

6. Chemical Equilibrium

Law of Mass Action and Equilibrium Constant: For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, K_c = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ/([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ) and K_p = (p_C)ᶜ(p_D)ᵈ/((p_A)ᵃ(p_B)ᵇ). Relation: K_p = K_c(RT)^Δn, where Δn = moles of gaseous products – moles of gaseous reactants. K is dimensionless when using activities; in practice, concentrations/pressures are used with appropriate units. K depends only on temperature.

Le Chatelier's Principle: If a constraint (change in concentration, pressure, temperature) is applied to a system at equilibrium, the system shifts to counteract the constraint. Increase in pressure → shift to side with fewer gas moles. Increase in temperature → shift in endothermic direction. Addition of inert gas at constant volume → no effect; at constant pressure → shift toward more moles of gas. Catalyst does not affect equilibrium position.

Acid-Base Equilibria: pH = –log[H⁺], pOH = –log[OH⁻], pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C). For weak acid HA: [H⁺] = √(K_a × C) (when degree of dissociation << 1). pH = ½ pK_a – ½ log C. For weak base B: [OH⁻] = √(K_b × C), pOH = ½ pK_b – ½ log C. Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pK_a + log([salt]/[acid]). For salt of weak acid and strong base: pH = ½ pK_w + ½ pK_a + ½ log C. Hydrolysis of salts and pH of salt solutions must be analyzed case by case.

Solubility Product: For A_xB_y(s) ⇌ xA^y+(aq) + yB^x-(aq), K_sp = [A^y+]ˣ[B^x–]ʸ. Common ion effect decreases solubility. Solubility s in terms of K_sp: for 1:1 salt, s = √K_sp; for 1:2 salt (e.g., Ag₂CrO₄), s = ∛(K_sp/4).

Equilibrium in Gaseous Reactions: Degree of dissociation (α) relates to K_p. For PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂, if initial moles = 1 and degree of dissociation = α: K_p = α²P/(1–α²). At high pressure, α decreases (fewer moles favored). For N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂: K_p = 4α²P/(1–α²). Temperature dependence follows van't Hoff equation: ln(K₂/K₁) = (ΔH°/R)(1/T₁ – 1/T₂).

7. Ionic Equilibrium

Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry Theory: Arrhenius acids produce H⁺ in water; bases produce OH⁻. Brønsted-Lowry: acid = proton donor, base = proton acceptor. Conjugate acid-base pairs differ by one H⁺. Stronger acid has weaker conjugate base. Water auto-ionization: K_w = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C. K_w increases with temperature.

pH Calculations: Strong acid (HA): [H⁺] = C_a (fully dissociated). Strong base (BOH): [OH⁻] = C_b. Weak acid: [H⁺] = √(K_a·C) if α << 1; exact: [H⁺]² + K_a[H⁺] – K_aC = 0. Weak base: [OH⁻] = √(K_b·C). For mixture of two weak acids: [H⁺] = √(K_a1C₁ + K_a2C₂). For very dilute strong acid (<10⁻⁶ M), include water's contribution: [H⁺] = (C + √(C² + 4K_w))/2.

Buffer Solutions: Resist pH change on dilution or addition of small amounts of acid/base. Acidic buffer: weak acid + its salt (e.g., CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa). Basic buffer: weak base + its salt. pH = pK_a + log([salt]/[acid]). Buffer capacity β = dC_b/dpH = 2.303·(C_acid·C_salt)/(C_acid + C_salt). Maximum buffer capacity when [salt] = [acid] (pH = pK_a). Biological buffers: blood maintains pH 7.4 via H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ system.

Salt Hydrolysis: Salt of strong acid + strong base → no hydrolysis, pH = 7. Salt of weak acid + strong base → anion hydrolyzes, solution basic: K_h = K_w/K_a, h = √(K_h/C), pH = ½pK_w + ½pK_a + ½logC. Salt of strong acid + weak base → cation hydrolyzes, solution acidic: K_h = K_w/K_b, pH = ½pK_w – ½pK_b – ½logC. Salt of weak acid + weak base: K_h = K_w/(K_a·K_b), pH = ½pK_w + ½pK_a – ½pK_b (independent of concentration).

Indicator and Titration Curves: Indicators are weak organic acids/bases where conjugate forms have different colors. Transition range ≈ pK_in ± 1. Phenolphthalein (8.3–10.0) for strong acid-strong base and weak acid-strong base. Methyl orange (3.1–4.4) for strong acid-weak base. At equivalence point, steep pH change occurs. Choice of indicator depends on pH at equivalence point.

8. Electrochemistry

Electrolysis and Faraday's Laws: Faraday's first law: mass deposited/discharged w = ZIt = (E/F)·I·t, where E = equivalent weight, F = 96500 C/mol. Faraday's second law: for same charge passed, masses deposited are proportional to equivalent weights. Electrolytic cells convert electrical energy to chemical energy. Anode (oxidation); Cathode (reduction). During electrolysis of aqueous solutions, overpotential and standard electrode potentials determine which species discharges.

Conductance: Conductivity (κ) = G × (l/A) = 1/ρ, unit S m⁻¹ or S cm⁻¹. Molar conductivity Λ_m = κ/C (C in mol/m³) or Λ_m = κ × 1000/C (C in mol/L), unit S cm² mol⁻¹. Equivalent conductivity Λ_eq = κ × 1000/N. Kohlrausch's law: Λ_m°(electrolyte) = ν₊Λ_m°(cation) + ν₋Λ_m°(anion). For weak electrolytes, Λ_m° is found by extrapolation using strong electrolyte data of common ions. Degree of dissociation α = Λ_m/Λ_m°. Strong electrolytes: Λ_m = Λ_m° – K√C (Debye-Hückel-Onsager equation).

Galvanic Cells and EMF: Galvanic cell converts chemical energy to electrical energy. Cell notation: anode | anode solution || cathode solution | cathode. E_cell = E_cathode – E_anode (both as reduction potentials). Nernst equation: E = E° – (RT/nF) ln Q. At 298 K: E = E° – (0.0591/n) log Q. At equilibrium, E_cell = 0, so E° = (0.0591/n) log K. Relationship: ΔG = –nFE_cell.

Standard Electrode Potentials: The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) has E° = 0.00 V by convention. Li⁺/Li has most negative E° (–3.04 V); F₂/F⁻ has most positive (+2.87 V). Higher E° means stronger oxidizing agent. To find E° for intermediate step from two known steps: use ΔG° additivity. For disproportionation: compare the two relevant half-cell potentials.

Battery Types and Corrosion: Primary cells (dry cell, mercury cell) are non-rechargeable. Secondary cells (lead-acid battery, Ni-Cd cell, Li-ion) are rechargeable. Lead-acid: Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O. Fuel cells (H₂-O₂) directly convert chemical energy to electricity with high efficiency. Corrosion is electrochemical oxidation of metals; rusting of iron requires both O₂ and H₂O. Protection methods: painting, galvanization, cathodic protection, sacrificial anodes.

9. Chemical Kinetics

Rate of Reaction: Rate = –d[R]/dt = d[P]/dt (for simple stoichiometry). Rate law: Rate = k[A]^x[B]^y, where x and y are orders determined experimentally (not from stoichiometric coefficients). Overall order = x + y. Units of k: for nth order reaction, k has units of conc^(1–n) time⁻¹. For zero order: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹; first order: s⁻¹; second order: L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.

Integrated Rate Equations: Zero order: [A] = [A]₀ – kt; t_{½} = [A]₀/(2k); half-life proportional to initial concentration. First order: [A] = [A]₀ e^{–kt}; ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt; t_{½} = 0.693/k (independent of [A]₀). Second order (single reactant): 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt; t_{½} = 1/(k[A]₀). For first-order radioactive decay: N = N₀ e^{–λt}; t_{½} = 0.693/λ; activity A = λN.

Temperature Dependence — Arrhenius Equation: k = A e^{–E_a/RT}. Log form: log(k₂/k₁) = (E_a/2.303R)(1/T₁ – 1/T₂). Activation energy E_a can be determined from slope of log k vs 1/T plot: slope = –E_a/2.303R. For reverse reaction: E_a(forward) – E_a(reverse) = ΔH (for endothermic, forward Ea is larger).

Reaction Mechanisms and Molecularity: Molecularity is the number of molecules colliding in an elementary step (always a small integer, defined only for elementary steps). Reaction mechanism is the sequence of elementary steps. The slowest step (rate-determining step) governs overall rate. For steady-state approximation: d[intermediate]/dt ≈ 0. Example: 2O₃ → 3O₂ via O₃ ⇌ O₂ + O (fast), O + O₃ → 2O₂ (slow). Rate = k₂[K][O₃] = k₂K[O₃]²/[O₂]. catalysis lowers E_a by providing alternative pathway. Positive catalyst increases rate; negative catalyst decreases rate.

Collision Theory: k = P·Z·e^{–E_a/RT}, where Z is collision frequency and P is steric factor. For bimolecular reactions, Z ∝ √T. Effective collisions require sufficient energy (≥ E_a) and proper orientation.

10. Solid State

Unit Cells and Crystal Systems: Seven crystal systems: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, rhombohedral, hexagonal, monoclinic, triclinic. Three cubic unit cells: simple/cubic (sc), body-centered cubic (bcc), face-centered cubic (fcc). Atoms per unit cell: sc = 1, bcc = 2, fcc = 4. Relation between edge length a and atomic radius r: sc → a = 2r; bcc → a = 4r/√3; fcc → a = 4r/√2 = 2√2 r.

Packing Efficiency: sc = 52.4%, bcc = 68%, fcc/hcp = 74% (most efficient). Empty space: sc = 47.6%, bcc = 32%, fcc = 26%. Coordination numbers: sc = 6, bcc = 8, fcc/hcp = 12.

Density of Crystal: ρ = (Z × M)/(a³ × N_A), where Z = atoms per unit cell, M = molar mass, a = edge length. For ionic crystals, use formula mass. Common structures: NaCl (fcc of Cl⁻ with Na⁺ in octahedral voids, CN=6:6), CsCl (simple cubic of Cl⁻ with Cs⁺ in cubic void, CN=8:8), ZnS/zinc blende (fcc of S²⁻ with Zn²⁺ in alternate tetrahedral voids, CN=4:4), CaF₂/fluorite (fcc of Ca²⁺ with F⁻ in all tetrahedral voids).

Void Sizes: In fcc packing, radius of tetrahedral void = 0.225r, octahedral void = 0.414r. Number of tetrahedral voids = 2N (N = number of close-packed spheres); number of octahedral voids = N. In ionic solids, cation occupies tetrahedral void if r⁺/r⁻ < 0.414, octahedral if 0.414 < r⁺/r⁻ < 0.732, cubic if > 0.732.

Crystal Defects: Stoichiometric defects: vacancy (missing ion), interstitial (extra ion in void), Frenkel (ion displaced to interstitial site, common with size mismatch like AgCl), Schottky (pair of cation-anion vacancies, common when sizes are similar like NaCl). Non-stoichiometric defects: metal excess (due to anion vacancy/electronic defect like NaCl heated in Na vapor → yellow color; or extra cation like ZnO heated → turns yellow), metal deficiency (cation vacancy compensated by higher oxidation state, e.g., Fe₁–δO, NaₓFeO₂).

11. Solutions and Colligative Properties

Raoult's Law and Ideal Solutions: For ideal solution, ΔH_mix = 0, ΔV_mix = 0. Raoult's law: P_solution = χ_solvent · P°_solvent. For volatile solute: P_total = p₁ + p₂ = χ₁P₁° + χ₂P₂°. Boiling point of solution: P_solution = atmospheric pressure at boiling point. Total vapor pressure plot is linear for ideal solutions.

Colligative Properties — Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure: For non-volatile solute: (P° – P_s)/P° = χ_solute = n₂/(n₁+n₂) ≈ n₂/n₁ (dilute). Relative lowering equals mole fraction of solute. This is Raoult's law for non-volatile solutes.

Elevation of Boiling Point: ΔT_b = K_b · m, where K_b = RT_b²M₁/(1000ΔH_vap) is ebullioscopic constant. Boiling point of solution T_b(soln) = T_b(solvent) + ΔT_b. Depression of Freezing Point: ΔT_f = K_f · m, where K_f = RT_f²M₁/(1000ΔH_fus) is cryoscopic constant. For solutes that associate or dissociate: ΔT = i·K·m.

Van't Hoff Factor: i = (actual number of particles after association/dissociation)/(formula unit). For dissociation: i = 1 + α(n–1). For association: i = 1 + α(1/n – 1). Abnormal molecular mass = normal molecular mass/i. Osmotic pressure: π = iCRT = inRT/V. Isotonic solutions have same osmotic pressure.

Reverse Osmosis: When pressure greater than osmotic pressure is applied to solution side, solvent flows from solution to pure solvent. Used in desalination. For electrolyte solutions, modify with i: π = iCRT. Molar mass determination: M₂ = (i·W₂·RT)/(π·V) or using colligative property formulas.

12. Surface Chemistry

Adsorption: Physisorption (physical adsorption) involves weak van der Waals forces; it is reversible, multilayer, low heat of adsorption (20–40 kJ/mol), decreases with temperature. Chemisorption (chemical adsorption) involves chemical bond formation; it is irreversible, monolayer, high heat of adsorption (80–240 kJ/mol), increases initially then decreases with temperature. Freundlich adsorption isotherm: x/m = k·P^{1/n} (0.1 < 1/n < 0.5 for physical adsorption). Log form: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)log P. Plot of log(x/m) vs log P gives straight line with slope 1/n and intercept log k. At high pressure, x/m becomes independent of P (saturation). Langmuir isotherm assumes monolayer adsorption on homogeneous surface: θ = KP/(1+KP), where θ is fractional coverage.

Colloids: Particle size 1–1000 nm. Tyndall effect: scattering of light by colloidal particles. Brownian movement: random motion of colloidal particles due to collision with solvent molecules. Electrical properties: colloidal particles carry charge due to preferential adsorption of ions, ionization of surface groups, or friction. Electrophoresis: movement of colloidal particles under electric field. Cataphoresis/electrophoresis direction indicates charge. Isoelectric point: pH at which Zeta potential is zero.

Coagulation and Hardy-Schulze Rule: Coagulation is precipitation of colloidal particles by adding electrolyte. Hardy-Schulze rule: greater the charge on coagulating ion, greater is its coagulating power. For negatively charged sols (As₂S₃): Al³⁺ > Ba²⁺ > Na⁺. For positively charged sols (Fe(OH)₃): PO₄³⁻ > SO₄²⁻ > Cl⁻. Gold number: minimum amount of protective colloid (in mg) to prevent coagulation of 10 mL gold sol by addition of 1 mL of 10% NaCl. Lower gold number = greater protective power. Lyophilic colloids are self-stabilizing; lyophobic colloids require stabilizers.

13. Nuclear Chemistry

Radioactivity: Discovered by Becquerel (1896); studied by Marie and Pierre Curie. Three types of radiation: α-particles (⁴₂He nuclei, +2 charge, low penetration, stopped by paper), β-particles (high-speed electrons, –1 charge, moderate penetration, stopped by aluminum), γ-rays (electromagnetic radiation, no charge, high penetration, requires lead/concrete). α-decay reduces mass number by 4, atomic number by 2. β⁻-decay: neutron → proton + electron + antineutrino (Z increases by 1). β⁺-decay (positron emission): proton → neutron + positron + neutrino (Z decreases by 1). Electron capture: p + e⁻ → n + ν_e.

Radioactive Decay Law: N = N₀ e^{–λt}, where λ is decay constant. Activity A = λN = A₀ e^{–λt}. Half-life: t_{½} = ln 2/λ = 0.693/λ. Mean life: τ = 1/λ = t_{½}/0.693. Relationship: N = N₀(½)^{n}, where n = t/t_{½} (number of half-lives). For successive decay: A→B→C, Bateman equations apply.

Nuclear Stability and Binding Energy: Binding energy BE = Δm × 931.5 MeV, where Δm is mass defect. Binding energy per nucleon curve: peaks at Fe-56 (~8.8 MeV/nucleon). Fusion occurs for light nuclei (A < 56); fission for heavy nuclei (A > 56). Both move toward higher BE/nucleon, releasing energy. Nuclear reaction: total mass number and atomic number are conserved. Q-value = (mass of reactants – mass of products) × 931.5 MeV.

Artificial Transmutation and Nuclear Reactions: Bombardment with neutrons, protons, α-particles, or deuterons. Nuclear fission: ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n + energy (~200 MeV per fission). Chain reaction requires critical mass. Nuclear reactors use moderator (heavy water/graphite) to slow neutrons, control rods (cadmium) to absorb neutrons, and coolant to transfer heat. Nuclear weapons use uncontrolled chain reaction. Fusion: ²H + ³H → ⁴He + n + 17.6 MeV (thermonuclear reaction, requires high T).

Important facts

  • Avogadro's number N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹; one mole of ideal gas at STP (1 bar, 273.15 K) = 22.7 L.
  • Bohr energy formula E_n = –13.6 Z²/n² eV applies only to hydrogen-like (one-electron) species.
  • In VSEPR, lone pair-lone pair repulsion > lone pair-bond pair > bond pair-bond pair; this causes bond angle compression (e.g., 104.5° in H₂O vs 109.5°).
  • Bond order from MOT = ½(N_b – N_a); O₂ has BO = 2 and is paramagnetic due to 2 unpaired electrons in π* orbitals.
  • v_rms : v_avg : v_mp = √3 : √(8/π) : √2 ≈ 1.224 : 1.128 : 1.000.
  • ΔG = –nFE connects electrochemistry to thermodynamics; at equilibrium, E_cell = 0 and ΔG = 0.
  • K_p = K_c(RT)^Δn; for Δn = 0, K_p = K_c.
  • For first-order reactions, t_{½} is independent of initial concentration — this is the key distinguishing feature.
  • FCC packing has 74% efficiency with 4 atoms/unit cell; bcc has 68% with 2 atoms; sc has 52.4% with 1 atom.
  • i (Van't Hoff factor) = 1 for non-electrolytes; for NaCl i ≈ 2, for CaCl₂ i ≈ 3, for葡萄糖 i = 1.
  • Hardy-Schulze rule: coagulating power is proportional to the valency of the oppositely charged ion.
  • t_{½} = 0.693/λ; mean life τ = 1/λ = 1.44 × t_{½}.
  • Binding energy per nucleon is maximum (~8.8 MeV) for ⁵⁶Fe; fusion releases energy for A < 56, fission for A > 56.
  • Frenkel defect does not change density; Schottky defect decreases density (missing ions).
  • The Nernst equation at 298 K simplifies to E = E° – (0.0591/n) log Q.

Common mistakes

  • Using 22.4 L/mol (STP at 1 atm) instead of 22.7 L/mol (STP at 1 bar, IUPAC standard) — JEE Advanced frequently tests this distinction.
  • Confusing molarity (temperature-dependent) with molality (temperature-independent) — always check which concentration term is asked.
  • Applying Bohr's model to multi-electron atoms — it works ONLY for one-electron (hydrogen-like) species (H, He⁺, Li²⁺).
  • Assuming ΔH = 0 for all isothermal processes — true only for ideal gases; real gases have intermolecular forces.
  • Forgetting the Van't Hoff factor (i) in colligative property calculations for electrolytes — leads to wrong molecular mass.
  • Mixing up sign conventions in thermodynamics — IUPAC: w = –P_extΔV (work done BY system is negative); some textbooks use opposite.
  • Using stoichiometric coefficients as reaction orders — orders must be determined experimentally, not from balanced equation.
  • Confusing molecularity (defined only for elementary steps, always integer) with order (experimentally determined, can be fractional).
  • In Nernst equation, using log instead of ln (or vice versa) with wrong coefficient — at 298 K, use 0.0591 for log₁₀, or RT/F for ln.
  • Assuming K_sp directly equals solubility — K_sp and s are related differently for different salt types (1:1 vs 1:2 vs 2:3 etc.).
  • Forgetting that a catalyst affects kinetics (rate) but NOT thermodynamics (equilibrium position, ΔG, K).
  • Applying Le Chatelier's principle incorrectly to inert gas addition — at constant volume, no effect; at constant pressure, shift toward more moles.
  • Ignoring that t_{½} for first-order is concentration-independent — this is unique to first-order among common kinetics.
  • Confusing physisorption (decreases with T) with chemisorption (initially increases then decreases with T).
  • Incorrectly calculating bond order in MOT — must count electrons in bonding AND antibonding orbitals separately.
