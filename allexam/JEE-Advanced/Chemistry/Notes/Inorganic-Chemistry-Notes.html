Periodic Classification and Periodic Trends

Modern periodic law states that the properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. The periodic table is arranged into 18 groups and 7 periods.

Electronic Configurations and Block Classification:
- s-block: Groups 1-2, ns¹⁻² configuration (highly reactive metals)
- p-block: Groups 13-18, ns²np¹⁻⁶ (metals, metalloids, non-metals)
- d-block: Groups 3-12, (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ns⁰⁻² (transition metals)
- f-block: Lanthanoids and Actinoids, (n-2)f¹⁻¹⁴(n-1)d⁰⁻¹ns²

Atomic and Ionic Radii Trends:
- Atomic radius decreases across a period (increasing effective nuclear charge, Z_eff = Z - σ). For isoelectronic species, radius ∝ 1/Z (e.g., N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺).
- Down a group, atomic radius increases due to addition of principal shells.
- Lanthanoid contraction causes the 4d and 5d series elements of the same group to have nearly identical radii (e.g., Zr ≈ Hf, Nb ≈ Ta), making separation extremely difficult.
- Ionic radii: cations are smaller than parent atoms (loss of shell or increased Z_eff); anions are larger (increased electron-electron repulsion).

Ionization Enthalpy (IE):
- General trend: increases across a period, decreases down a group.
- Exceptions: Be > B (2s vs 2p penetration), N > O (half-filled 2p³ stability), Mg > Al, P > S.
- IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃ (successive IEs increase sharply when breaking into a core shell).
- Large jump between successive IEs indicates the valence electron count (e.g., large jump after IE₁ → +1 oxidation state; after IE₂ → +2 state).
- Ionization enthalpy is lowered by: larger size, screening effect, diffuse orbitals, half-filled/filled subshell stability loss.

Electron Gain Enthalpy (EGE):
- More negative EGE means greater tendency to accept an electron.
- Trend: becomes more negative across a period, less negative down a group.
- Exceptions: Cl has the most negative EGE (-349 kJ/mol), even more than F (-328 kJ/mol) because F's small size causes inter-electronic repulsion.
- Noble gases have positive EGE (adding an electron requires entering a new shell).

Electronegativity (EN):
- Pauling scale: F = 4.0 (most electronegative), Cs/Fr = 0.7 (least).
- Increases across a period, decreases down a group.
- Diagonal relationship: Li-Mg, Be-Al, B-Si show similarities due to comparable EN and ionic radii.
- Elements with EN difference > 1.7 tend to form ionic bonds; < 1.7 tend to form covalent bonds.

Metallic and Non-metallic Character:
- Metallic character increases down a group and decreases across a period (opposite of EN).
- Strong reducing agents are on the bottom-left (Cs, Fr); strong oxidizing agents on the top-right (F, O).

Amphoteric Nature:
- Oxides/hydroxides of elements near the metalloid line (Al, Zn, Sn, Pb, Be) are amphoteric — react with both acids and bases.

Chemical Bonding — Ionic, Covalent, Metallic and Modern theories

Ionic Bonding:
- Formed by complete transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal.
- Lattice energy (U): energy released when gaseous ions form one mole of ionic solid. U ∝ (Z⁺·Z⁻)/(r⁺ + r⁻). Higher charge and smaller size → higher lattice energy.
- Fajan's Rules (covalent character in ionic bonds):
1. Small cation → greater polarizing power → more covalent character (LiCl > NaCl > KCl > RbCl > CsCl).
2. Large anion → more polarizable → more covalent character (LiI > LiBr > LiCl > LiF).
3. Cation with 18e⁻ configuration (pseudo noble gas) shows more covalent character than 8e⁻ configuration (e.g., AgCl more covalent than NaCl).
- Born-Haber cycle connects lattice energy with IE, EGE, sublimation energy, bond dissociation energy.

Covalent Bonding — Valence Bond Theory (VBT):
- Bond formation by orbital overlap. Sigma (σ) bond: head-on overlap (stronger); Pi (π) bond: lateral overlap (weaker).
- Hybridization:
- sp: linear, 180° (BeCl₂, CO₂, C₂H₂)
- sp²: trigonal planar, 120° (BF₃, C₂H₄)
- sp³: tetrahedral, 109.5° (CH₄, NH₃, H₂O)
- sp³d: trigonal bipyramidal (PCl₅)
- sp³d²: octahedral (SF₆, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻)
- dsp²: square planar ([Ni(CN)₄]²⁻)
- Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, reducing bond angles: CH₄ (109.5°) > NH₃ (107°) > H₂O (104.5°).

VSEPR Theory:
- Steric number = number of σ bonds + number of lone pairs on central atom.
- Predicts molecular geometry based on minimizing electron pair repulsion.
- Common shapes: SN=2 (linear), SN=3 (trigonal planar), SN=4 (tetrahedral/trigonal pyramidal/bent), SN=5 (T-shaped, see-saw), SN=6 (square planar, square pyramidal).

Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT):
- Atomic orbitals combine to form bonding (lower energy) and antibonding (higher energy) MOs.
- Bond order = ½(N_b - N_a). BO > 0 → stable molecule; BO = 0 → doesn't exist.
- For diatomic molecules up to N₂: σ1s < σ1s < σ2s < σ2s < π2pₓ = π2pᵧ < σ2pz < π2pₓ = π2pᵧ < σ*2pz.
- For O₂, F₂, Ne₂: σ2pz lies below π2p orbitals.
- MOT explains paramagnetism of O₂ (two unpaired electrons in π* orbitals).
- Isoelectronic species have similar bond orders and magnetic properties.

Hydrogen Bonding:
- Intermolecular H-bond: between molecules (e.g., H₂O, HF, NH₃) — raises boiling point.
- Intramolecular H-bond: within same molecule (e.g., o-nitrophenol, salicylic acid) — lowers boiling point relative to isomers.
- Stronger H-bond when donor and acceptor are more electronegative and smaller.
- Ice is less dense than water due to open hexagonal lattice held by H-bonds.

Metallic Bonding:
- Sea of delocalized electrons holding positive metal ions in a lattice.
- Explains conductivity, malleability, ductility, luster.
- Melting points vary: Group 1 metals have low MP (weak metallic bonding); transition metals have high MP (involvement of d-electrons in bonding).

s-Block Elements — Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals

Alkali Metals (Group 1: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr)
- Electronic config: ns¹. Always show +1 oxidation state.
- Physical properties: soft, low MP/BP, good conductors. MP decreases down the group (Li > Na > K > Rb > Cs).
- Flame test colors: Li (crimson red), Na (golden yellow), K (lilac), Rb (red-violet), Cs (blue). Fr is radioactive.
- Anomalous behavior of Lithium (Diagonal relationship with Mg):
- Smallest size, highest IE, highest polarizing power.
- Li forms nitride directly: 6Li + N₂ → 2Li₃N (other alkali metals don't).
- LiCl is covalent and hygroscopic; decomposes on heating. Anhydrous LiCl cannot be obtained by heating hydrated salt (requires NH₄Cl).
- Li metal cannot be obtained by electrolysis of aqueous LiCl (H₂ evolves instead).
- Li is the strongest reducing agent in aqueous solution despite having highest IE due to highly negative hydration enthalpy of Li⁺.
- Li and Mg carbonates decompose on heating; Na₂CO₃ does not.
- Reactivity with water: Increases down the group. Li reacts gently; Na vigorously; K catches fire; Rb and Cs explode.
- Reactivity with air: Form oxides, peroxides, superoxides. Li → Li₂O; Na → Na₂O₂ (major); K, Rb, Cs → MO₂ (superoxide).
- Dissolution in liquid ammonia: Forms deep blue solutions containing solvated electrons. Blue color due to ammoniated electrons; bronze color at high concentration due to metal ions.
- Key compounds:
- NaOH + Na₂CO₃ (Soda ash) — Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda) on heating → Na₂CO₃ (anhydrous, soap industry)
- NaCl → Electrolytic molten → Na + Cl₂ (Downs cell)
- Na₂O₂ — bleaching agent, oxygen source in submarines
- KOH — used in soft soap
- LiAlH₄ (lithium aluminium hydride) — powerful reducing agent in organic chemistry

Alkaline Earth Metals (Group 2: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra)
- Electronic config: ns². Always show +2 oxidation state.
- Anomalous behavior of Beryllium:
- Smallest, highest IE, highest EN. Forms covalent compounds.
- BeCl₂ is covalent, soluble in organic solvents, acts as Lewis acid. In vapour phase exists as monomer (sp, linear) and dimer (Be₂Cl₄).
- Be is passivated by conc. HNO₃ (forms oxide layer).
- Be does not liberate H₂ from steam (protected oxide film); Mg reacts with steam.
- BeO and Be(OH)₂ are amphoteric; other M(OH)₂ are basic.
- Hydration energy trend: Be²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Sr²⁺ > Ba²⁺ (charge density decreases down the group).
- Thermal stability of carbonates: Increases down the group (BeCO₃ least stable, BaCO₃ most stable) — inversely related to polarizing power of cation.
- Solubility of sulphates: Decreases down the group (BeSO₄ > MgSO₄ > CaSO₄ > SrSO₄ > BaSO₄). BeSO₄ and MgSO₄ are more soluble than CaSO₄ because hydration energy dominates over lattice energy for smaller ions.
- Solubility of hydroxides: Increases down the group (Be(OH)₂ insoluble, Ba(OH)₂ soluble) — lattice energy decreases faster than hydration energy.
- Flame tests: Be and Mg do not impart flame color (small size, high IE — electrons cannot be excited by Bunsen flame). Ca (brick red), Sr (crimson), Ba (apple green).
- Key compounds:
- MgO (magnesia) — refractory material
- Mg(OH)₂ — milk of magnesia (antacid)
- CaSO₄·½H₂O — Plaster of Paris (POP), sets to CaSO₄·2H₂O on adding water
- Ca(OH)₂ — slaked lime; CaO — quick lime
- BaSO₄ — radiocontrast agent (barium meal)
- MgCl₂·6H₂O — cannot be dehydrated directly (hydrolysis occurs); must be heated with SOCl₂
- Diagonal relationship Be-Mg and Li-Mg also extends to similar chemical behavior in nitride formation, carbonate decomposition, and chloride deliquescence.

p-Block Elements — Groups 13 and 14

Group 13 (Boron Family: B, Al, Ga, In, Tl)
- Electronic config: ns²np¹. Common oxidation state: +3. Tl shows +1 (inert pair effect).
- Boron anomalies:
- Non-metal, hardest after diamond. Exists as B₁₂ icosahedra in α-rhombohedral boron.
- Only stable +3 oxidation state. Cannot expand octet (no d-orbitals).
- B₂H₆ (diborane) — electron-deficient compound with 3-center-2-electron (3c-2e) bonds (banana bonds).
- Boric acid H₃BO₃ (or B(OH)₃) is a Lewis acid, not a proton donor: B(OH)₃ + 2H₂O ⇌ [B(OH)₄]⁻ + H₃O⁺.
- Borax bead test: B₂O₃ + CuO → Cu(BO₂)₂ (blue bead, hot) / (brown-red, cold).
- Allotropy: Boron has many allotropes; aluminium is cubic close-packed.
- Inert Pair Effect: Increasing down the group, +1 state becomes more stable for Tl (Tl⁺ is more stable than Tl³⁺). TlCl is stable while TlCl₃ is a strong oxidizing agent.
- Aluminium:
- Amphoteric: 2Al + 2NaOH + 6H₂O → 2Na[Al(OH)₄] + 3H₂; Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O; Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O.
- Passivated by conc. HNO₃, conc. H₂SO₄, and air (protective oxide layer).
- Alcl₃·6H₂O exists as [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ 3Cl⁻. On heating, hydrolyzes to Al(OH)₃ then Al₂O₃.
- Alumina (Al₂O₃) is amphoteric; aluminates formed with NaOH.
- Key compounds:
- B₂H₆ (diborane) — prepared from NaBH₄ + I₂ or BF₃ + NaH
- NaBH₄ — reducing agent, safer than LiAlH₄
- Al2(SO4)3 + K2SO4·24H2O — Potash alum (water purifier, mordant)
- Garnet (aluminium silicate) — used in abrasives

Group 14 (Carbon Family: C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb)
- Electronic config: ns²np². Show +4 and +2 oxidation states. Inert pair effect increases down the group: Pb shows more stable +2 than +4.
- Carbon — Allotropes:
- Diamond: sp³ hybridized, 3D network, hardest natural substance, insulator.
- Graphite: sp² hybridized, layered structure, weak van der Waals between layers, conductor, lubricant.
- Fullerenes (C₆₀ — buckyball): truncated icosahedron, 12 pentagons + 20 hexagons.
- Graphene: single layer of graphite, exceptional strength and conductivity.
- CO (carbon monoxide): binds to hemoglobin 200× stronger than O₂ — lethal poisoning mechanism.
- CO₂: linear molecule, dry ice used as refrigerant.
- Silicon:
- Forms SiO₂ (silica) — network covalent, each Si bonded to 4 O in tetrahedral arrangement.
- Silicone polymers: -Si-O- backbone with organic side groups (R₂SiO units). Heat resistant, waterproof, good electrical insulators.
- Silicates: built from [SiO₄]⁴⁻ tetrahedra sharing oxygen atoms.
- Zeolites: microporous aluminosilicates, used as catalysts and molecular sieves.
- Lead (Pb): PbO₂ is a strong oxidizing agent (Pb⁴⁺ unstable); PbCl₂ is sparingly soluble in cold water but soluble in hot water; PbS is black (litharge is PbO, red lead is Pb₃O₄).
- SnCl₄ is a covalent liquid (anhydrous); SnCl₂ is a reducing agent (Sn²⁺ → Sn⁴⁺ + 2e⁻).
- Boron nitride (BN): isostructural with graphite (hexagonal BN) and diamond (cubic BN — superhard).
- Silicon carbide (SiC, carborundum): used as abrasive,硬度接近金刚石.

p-Block Elements — Groups 15, 16, 17 and 18

Group 15 (Pnictogens: N, P, As, Sb, Bi)
- Electronic config: ns²np³. Show -3, +3, +5 oxidation states. -3 stability decreases down the group. Inert pair effect: Bi shows stable +3 over +5.
- Nitrogen anomalies:
- Diatomic N₂ with triple bond (one σ + two π), bond dissociation energy 941 kJ/mol — very inert at room temperature.
- Forms extensive H-bonding (NH₃), unlike PH₃.
- N₂O, NO, N₂O₃, NO₂, N₂O₄, N₂O₅ — multiple oxides with different acidic/basic character.
- HNO₃ is a strong oxidizing agent; pure HNO₃ is colourless, technical grade is yellow due to dissolved NO₂.
- Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (Fe catalyst, 700K, 200 atm).
- Phosphorus allotropes:
- White P (P₄): tetrahedral, highly reactive, toxic, soluble in CS₂, glows in dark (phosphorescence). Stored under water.
- Red P: polymeric chain structure, less reactive, insoluble in CS₂.
- Black P: layered, most stable allotrope, conductor.
- P₄O₆ (phosphorus trioxide) and P₄O₁₀ (phosphorus pentoxide) — powerful dehydrating agent.
- H₃PO₃ (dibasic, 2 OH groups), H₃PO₄ (tribasic, 3 OH groups), H₃PO₂ (monobasic, 1 OH group).
- Phosphine (PH₃): weakly basic, burns in O₂, contaminated with P₂H₄ makes white P ignite spontaneously.
- Oxyacids of phosphorus:
- Hypophosphorous acid H₃PO₂ (P oxidation state +1, monobasic, strong reducing agent)
- Phosphorous acid H₃PO₃ (P +3, dibasic, reducing)
- Orthophosphoric acid H₃PO₄ (P +5, tribasic, non-reducing)
- Pyrophosphoric acid H₄P₂O₇; Metaphosphoric acid (HPO₃)n
- Nitrogen halides: NCl₃ (explosive, hydrolysed to NH₃ + HOCl); NF₃ (stable, doesn't hydrolyse easily due to no d-orbitals on F).

Group 16 (Chalcogens: O, S, Se, Te, Po)
- Electronic config: ns²np⁴. Show -2, +2, +4, +6 oxidation states.
- Oxygen anomalies:
- Liquid O₂ is pale blue (paramagnetic, explained by MOT).
- Ozone (O₃): bent structure, resonance-stabilized, powerful oxidizing agent, blue-black ring test with KI-starch.
- H₂O is liquid at room temp; H₂S is gas — due to H-bonding in water.
- O²⁻ (oxide), O₂²⁻ (peroxide), O₂⁻ (superoxide) — stability depends on cation size (large cations stabilize superoxides: KO₂, RbO₂, CsO₂).
- Sulphur — Allotropes:
- Rhombic (α-S₈, stable below 369K, octahedral crystals)
- Monoclinic (β-S₈, stable above 369K, needle-like crystals)
- Plastic sulfur (amorphous, obtained by quenching molten sulfur in cold water)
- Oleum (H₂S₂O₇): SO₃ dissolved in conc. H₂SO₄ — used in Contact Process.
- Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄):
- Non-volatile acid, dehydrating agent, oxidizing agent (especially hot conc.)
- Contact process: S → SO₂ → SO₃ (V₂O₅ catalyst) → H₂SO₄ (absorbed in 98% H₂SO₄ to form oleum, then diluted)
- Hot conc. H₂SO₄ oxidizes Cu, C, S: Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O; C + 2H₂SO₄ → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O
- Peroxodisulphuric acid (H₂S₂O₈): contains peroxide linkage (-O-O-).
- Thiosulphate (S₂O₃²⁻): reduces I₂ to I⁻ (iodometry); reacts with acid to give S + SO₂.

Group 17 (Halogens: F, Cl, Br, I, At)
- Electronic config: ns²np⁵. Most reactive non-metals. Show -1, +1, +3, +5, +7 (except F which is only -1 and has no d-orbitals).
- Physical properties: F₂ and Cl₂ are gases; Br₂ is liquid; I₂ is solid. Color intensifies down the group: pale yellow → greenish-yellow → reddish-brown → violet.
- Anomalous behavior of Fluorine:
- Weakest X-X bond (F-F 158 kJ/mol < Cl-Cl 242 kJ/mol) due to lone pair repulsion on small atoms.
- Highest EN, strongest oxidizing agent (E° = +2.87 V), reacts with glass (SiO₂ + 4HF → SiF₄ + 2H₂O).
- HF is a weak acid (partial dissociation due to strong H-bonding), but etches glass.
- No positive oxidation state; forms only HOF (unstable, known at low temperature).
- Chlorine compounds:
- Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl (sunlight catalyzes decomposition)
- Cl₂ + excess NH₃ → NH₄Cl + N₂ (white fumes)
- Cl₂ + cold dilute NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O (bleaching powder precursor)
- Cl₂ + hot conc. NaOH → NaCl + NaClO₃ + H₂O
- Oxyacids of chlorine: HOCl (+1) < HClO₂ (+3) < HClO₃ (+5) < HClO₄ (+7) in acidity and thermal stability order.
- Iodoform (CHI₃): yellow crystalline solid, antiseptic (due to free iodine release).
- Iodic acid (HIO₃): strongest among iodoxy acids.

Group 18 (Noble Gases: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn)
- Electronic config: ns²np⁶ (complete octet, except He: 1s²).
- Very low BP and MP; used as cryogenic liquids (liquid He at 4.2K).
- Xe forms compounds with F and O due to lower ionization enthalpy: XeF₂ (linear, sp³d), XeF₄ (square planar, sp³d²), XeF₆ (distorted octahedral), XeO₃ (pyramidal, explosive), XeO₄ (tetrahedral).
- XeF₂ hydrolysed partially: XeF₂ + H₂O → Xe + O₂ + 2HF (complete hydrolysis gives XeO₃).
- XeF₄ + H₂O → Xe + XeO₃ + HF + O₂.
- XeF₆ + 4H₂O (complete) → XeO₄ + 6HF + 2F₂; XeF₆ + 3H₂O (partial) → XeO₃ + 6HF.

d-Block and f-Block Elements

d-Block (Transition Metals: Sc to Zn, Y to Cd, La to Hg)
- Definition: Elements with partially filled d-orbitals in ground state or in any common oxidation state.
- General Properties:
- Metallic character, high MP/BP (except Zn, Cd, Hg), good conductors, form colored compounds, show variable oxidation states, exhibit catalytic behavior, form paramagnetic compounds, form coordination complexes.
- Colored ions arise from d-d transitions (requires incomplete d-subshell). Zn²⁺ (d¹⁰), Sc³⁺ (d⁰), Cu⁺ (d¹⁰) are colorless.
- Magnetic moment: μ = √(n(n+2)) BM, where n = number of unpaired electrons.
- Catalytic activity due to variable oxidation states and ability to form intermediates (e.g., Fe in Haber process, V₂O₅ in Contact process, Ni in hydrogenation).

- 3d Series (First Row Transition Metals):
- Sc (Z=21): [Ar]3d¹4s². Main oxidation state +3. Sc³⁺ is colorless (d⁰). Not truly transitional in many properties (only one oxidation state).
- Ti (Z=22): [Ar]3d²4s². +4 (TiO₂, white pigment — Ti blanc), +3, +2. Ti³⁺ is purple (d¹).
- V (Z=23): [Ar]3d³4s². +5 (VO₄³⁻ yellow, Cr₂O₇²⁻ orange), +4, +3 (green), +2 (red).
- Cr (Z=24): [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ (exception — half-filled d subshell stability). +3 (most stable, green), +6 (chromates/dichromates, strong oxidizing agents). Chromate (CrO₄²⁻, yellow) ⇌ Dichromate (Cr₂O₇²⁻, orange) — equilibrium depends on pH: acid favors dichromate, base favors chromate. Mohr's salt (NH₄)₂SO₄·FeSO₄·6H₂O — double salt, primary standard for standardizing KMnO₄/K₂Cr₂O₇. Potash dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) — primary standard, prepared from chromite ore (FeCr₂O₄).
- Mn (Z=25): [Ar]3d⁵4s². Wide range: +2 (most stable, pale pink), +3, +4 (MnO₂, black — depolarizer), +6 (manganate, green), +7 (permanganate, purple — strong oxidant).
- MnO₄⁻ in acid: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (purple to colorless)
- MnO₄⁻ in neutral: MnO₄⁻ + 2H₂O + 3e⁻ → MnO₂ + 4OH⁻ (purple to brown)
- MnO₄⁻ in alkaline: MnO₄⁻ + e⁻ → MnO₄²⁻ (purple to green)
- KMnO₄ preparation: MnO₂ + KOH + O₂ (or KClO₃) → K₂MnO₄ → disproportionation in acid/neutral → KMnO₄
- Fe (Z=26): [Ar]3d⁶4s². +2 (ferrous, pale green) and +3 (ferric, yellow/brown). +3 more stable due to half-filled d⁵. Fe³⁺ forms blood-red complex with SCN⁻. Magnetite Fe₃O₄ (mixed oxide FeO·Fe₂O₃) is ferrimagnetic. Hematite Fe₂O₃ (reddish brown). Pyrite FeS₂ (fool's gold).
- Co (Z=27): [Ar]3d⁷4s². +2 (rose/pink, [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺), +3 (unstable in aqueous solution, stabilized in complexes like [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺). Vitamin B₁₂ contains cobalt.
- Ni (Z=28): [Ar]3d⁸4s². +2 is most stable (green). Used in hydrogenation (Raney Ni), Coinage metal.
- Cu (Z=29): [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ (exception). +1 (Cu⁺, d¹⁰, colorless, unstable in water — disproportionates: 2Cu⁺ → Cu + Cu²⁺), +2 (Cu²⁺, d⁹, blue, most stable). CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue) on heating → white CuSO₄ + 5H₂O. Tollen's reagent: [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺. Cu₂O (red), CuO (black).
- Zn (Z=30): [Ar]3d¹⁰4s². Only +2 state (d¹⁰ configuration). White metal, blue in flame. ZnO (white, turns yellow on heating due to metal excess defect). Calamine (ZnCO₃), Zinc blende (ZnS), Zincite (ZnO). Zn is used in Galvanization, Daniel cell, and as a reducing agent.

- Ionization Enthalpy Trend: Higher than s-block but lower than p-block. Third IE of Mn is very high (breaking stable d⁵ configuration). Overall IE increases across the series but Ir > Pt > Au anomaly exists.
- Standard Electrode Potential: E°(M²⁺/M) values show Cu has positive E° (+0.34V) — cannot displace H₂ from acids. Zn has very negative E° (-0.76V) — strong reducing agent.
- Intermetallic compounds: Cr与Mn形成多种金属间化合物，用于特种钢。

f-Block (Lanthanoids and Actinoids)
- Lanthanoids (Ce-Lu, 4f series):
- General config: [Xe]4f¹⁻¹⁴5d⁰⁻¹6s². Main oxidation state +3. Ce and Tb can show +4; Eu and Yb show +2 (exceptional stability of empty, half-filled, filled f-subshells).
- Lanthanoid contraction: steady decrease in size across the series due to poor shielding by 4f electrons. Consequence: Zr≈Hf, Nb≈Ta, Mo≈W — similar chemical properties, difficult separation.
- Lanthanoids are paramagnetic (unpaired 4f electrons). Ce⁴⁺ (f⁰) is diamagnetic; Lu³⁺ (f¹⁴) is diamagnetic.
- Lanthanide ions are colored: La³⁺ (f⁰) and Lu³⁺ (f¹⁴) are colorless; others show colors due to f-f transitions.
- La(OH)₃ is more basic than Lu(OH)₃ (basicity decreases with decreasing ionic size across the series).
- Cerium(IV) is a strong oxidizing agent: Ce⁴⁺ + e⁻ → Ce³⁺ (E° ≈ +1.74 V). Used in volumetric analysis.
- NdFeB magnets (strongest permanent magnets) contain lanthanoids.
- Actinoids (Ac-Lr, 5f series):
- All radioactive. General config: [Rn]5f¹⁻¹⁴6d⁰⁻¹7s².
- Show wider range of oxidation states than lanthanoids (+3 to +7) because 5f, 6d, and 7s energies are comparable.
- Actinoid contraction is greater than lanthanoid contraction (5f shields even poorer than 4f).
- Important actinoids: U (uranium, nuclear fuel), Pu (plutonium), Th (thorium).
- UO₂²⁺ (uranyl ion) is very stable and forms yellow-green compounds.
- Separation of actinoids is more complex due to their radioactivity and similar化学性质.

Coordination Compounds

Basic Terminology:
- Coordination compound: central metal atom/ion bonded to surrounding ligands via coordinate (dative) bonds.
- Ligand: ion or molecule that donates a pair of electrons to the metal (Lewis base).
- Coordination number (CN): number of donor atoms directly bonded to the central metal.
- Denticity: number of donor atoms per ligand. Monodentate (H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻), bidentate (en, ox²⁻, dmg), tridentate (dien), tetradentate (EDTA⁴⁻), hexadentate (EDTA).
- Ambidentate ligands: can bind through different atoms (NO₂⁻/ONO⁻, CN⁻/NC⁻, SCN⁻/NCS⁻).

IUPAC Nomenclature:
- Ligands named alphabetically (before the metal). Anionic ligands end in -o: chlorido, oxido, hydroxido, aqua, ammine, carbonyl, nitrosyl.
- Prefixes: bis-, tris-, tetrakis- for polydentate or complex ligand names.
- Metal oxidation state in Roman numerals in parentheses.
- Anionic complexes: metal name ends in -ate (ferrate, cuprate, platinate, zirconate).
- Example: K₄[Fe(CN)₆] — potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II); [Co(en)₃]Cl₃ — tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)cobalt(III) chloride.

Isomerism:
- Structural isomerism: ionisation (e.g., [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ vs [Co(NH₃)₅SO₄]Br), hydrate/solvate, linkage (ambidentate ligands — e.g., [Co(NH₃)₅(NO₂)]Cl₂ vs [Co(NH₃)₅(ONO)]Cl₂), coordination (e.g., [Co(NH₃)₆][Cr(CN)₆] vs [Cr(NH₃)₆][Co(CN)₆]).
- Stereoisomerism: geometrical (cis-trans, fac-mer for MA₃B₃ type), optical (enantiomers in chiral complexes like [Co(en)₃]³⁺).
- cis-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺ is violet and optically active; trans is green and optically inactive.

Werner's Theory:
- Primary valency (ionizable, equal to oxidation state) and secondary valency (non-ionizable, equal to coordination number).
- [Co(NH₃)₆]Cl₃ → 4 ions in solution (1 [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ + 3Cl⁻)
- [Co(NH₃)₅Cl]Cl₂ → 3 ions
- [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]Cl → 2 ions

Valence Bond Theory (VBT):
- Hybridization of metal orbitals determines geometry.
- Inner orbital complexes (low spin): use (n-1)d orbitals — dsp² (square planar), d²sp³ (octahedral). Formed with strong field ligands.
- Outer orbital complexes (high spin): use nd orbitals — sp³d² (octahedral). Formed with weak field ligands.
- [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻: dsp², square planar, diamagnetic (Ni²⁺ d⁸, CN⁻ forces pairing)
- [Ni(Cl)₄]²⁻: sp³, tetrahedral, paramagnetic (weak field, no pairing)
- [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻: d²sp³, octahedral, diamagnetic (low spin d⁶)
- [FeF₆]³⁻: sp³d², octahedral, paramagnetic (high spin d⁵, 5 unpaired e⁻)

Crystal Field Theory (CFT):
- Ligands treated as point negative charges. Splitting of d-orbitals depends on geometry.
- Octahedral: t₂g (dxy, dyz, dzx — lower energy) and eg (dx²-y², dz² — higher energy). Splitting energy: Δ_o.
- Tetrahedral: e (lower) and t₂ (higher). Δ_t ≈ 4/9 Δ_o.
- Square planar: dx²-y² highest, then dxy, then dz², then dxz/dyz lowest.
- Strong field ligands (spectrochemical series): I⁻ < Br⁻ < S²⁻ < SCN⁻ < Cl⁻ < NO₃⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < C₂O₄²⁻ < H₂O < NCS⁻ < CH₃CN < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < PPh₃ < CN⁻ < CO.
- High spin (weak field): electrons occupy all orbitals singly before pairing (Δ_o < P, where P = pairing energy).
- Low spin (strong field): electrons pair in t₂g before occupying eg (Δ_o > P).
- Color: arises from d-d transition; the energy absorbed corresponds to Δ. Complementary color is observed.
- Jahn-Teller distortion: occurs in degenerate electronic states (e.g., d⁹ Cu²⁺ in octahedral field causes elongation/compression along z-axis). Stronger for eg orbital occupation asymmetry than t₂g.
- Spectrochemical series determines whether a complex is high-spin or low-spin. CN⁻ and CO are strong field; H₂O, F⁻ are weak field; I⁻ is very weak field.

18-Electron Rule and Effective Atomic Number (EAN):
- EAN = Z - oxidation state + 2 × CN. Stable complexes often have EAN = next noble gas atomic number.
- [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻: EAN = 26 - 2 + 12 = 36 (Kr)
- [Ni(CO)₄]: EAN = 28 - 0 + 8 = 36 (Kr)
- [Fe(CO)₅]: EAN = 26 + 10 = 36 (Kr)

Important Complexes and Uses:
- [Ni(CO)₄] — Mond's process for purification of Ni (volatilization at 50°C, decomposition at 230°C)
- [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻/³⁻ — ferro/ferricyanide, used in detection of Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺, Prussian blue/Turnbull's blue formation
- K₃[Fe(CN)₆] + Fe²⁺ → Fe₃[Fe(CN)₆]₂ ( Turnbull's blue)
- K₄[Fe(CN)₆] + Fe³⁺ → Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃ (Prussian blue)
- [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ — deep blue, Schweitzer's reagent (dissolves cellulose)
- [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ — Tollen's reagent (aldehyde test)
- [PtCl₄]²⁻ — basis for Cisplatin [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], anti-cancer drug
- Hemoglobin — Fe²⁺ porphyrin complex; Carboxyhemoglobin (CO binding) is lethal
- Chlorophyll — Mg²⁺ porphyrin complex
- Vitamin B₁₂ — Co³⁺ corrin complex
- EDTA chelation therapy for heavy metal poisoning (Pb²⁺, Hg²⁺)

Metallurgy — Principles and Processes

Ore Diagnosis and Mineralogy:
- Ore: rock/mineral from which metal can be extracted economically.
- Gangue: earthy/insoluble impurities (sand, gravel, mica, etc.).
- Key ores:
- Iron: hematite (Fe₂O₃), magnetite (Fe₃O₄), limonite (Fe₂O₃·nH₂O), siderite (FeCO₃), pyrite (FeS₂)
- Copper: copper pyrites (CuFeS₂), chalcopyrite, malachite (CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂), cuprite (Cu₂O), chalcocite (Cu₂S)
- Aluminium: bauxite (Al₂O₃·2H₂O, major), cryolite (Na₃AlF₆), alunite
- Zinc: zinc blende (ZnS), calamine (ZnCO₃), smithsonite
- Lead: galena (PbS), cerussite (PbCO₃)
- Silver: argentite (Ag₂S), horn silver (AgCl)
- Gold: native gold, auriferous pyrites
- Tin: cassiterite (SnO₂), stannite

Concentration (Benefaction):
- Hydraulic washing: separates by density difference (gravity separation). Used for oxide ores.
- Froth flotation: based on preferential wetting. Sulfide ores (ZnS, CuS, PbS) are concentrated. Pine oil (froth stabilizer) and collectors ( fatty acids/xanthates) make ore particles hydrophobic. Depressants: NaCN depresses ZnS to allow PbS floating; sodium ethyl xanthate as collector.
- Magnetic separation: for magnetically susceptible ores (magnetite Fe₃O₄) or non-magnetic gangue. Electromagnetic separator.
- Leaching: chemical method. Bauxite leached with NaOH (Bayer's process): Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2Na[Al(OH)₄]. Copper leached with H₂SO₄ in presence of O₂ (heap leaching).

Extraction of Metals — General Steps:
1. Extraction of metal from ore (concentration → conversion to oxide → reduction)
2. Refining of crude metal

Reduction Methods:
- Self-reduction (Roasting followed by self-reduction): Used for sulfide ores of less active metals (Cu, Pb, Hg).
- Roasting: heating in excess air below melting point. Converts sulfide to oxide/sulfate; removes S, As, Sb as oxides.
- Auto-reduction: Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂ (copper from copper pyrites)
- PbS + 2PbO → 3Pb + SO₂ (lead from galena)
- Carbon/CO reduction: Used for moderately active metals (Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb).
- ZnO + C → Zn + CO (extracted from ZnS after roasting)
- Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (in blast furnace at ~1500K)
- SnO₂ + 2C → Sn + 2CO
- Aluminothermy (Goldschmidt process): Al reduces oxides of Cr, Mn, Fe. Used for welding railway tracks.
- Cr₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Cr + Al₂O₃ (ΔG highly negative due to strong Al-O bond)
- Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃
- Electrolytic reduction: For highly active metals (Na, K, Ca, Mg, Al).
- Na from molten NaCl (Downs cell, CaCl₂ lowers MP from 801°C to ~600°C)
- Mg from molten MgCl₂ (with CaCl₂, NaCl)
- Al from Al₂O₃ (Hall-Héroult process): Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) at ~950°C, electrolyzed with carbon anodes. C + O₂ → CO₂ — anodes consumed. Cu liner is cathode.

Zone Refining:
- Based on differential solubility of impurities in molten and solid metal. Impurities concentrate in molten zone; repeat passage purifies. Used for Ge, Si, B, Ga for semiconductor grade (> 99.9999%).

Van Arkel Process (Iodide Process):
- For purifying Ti, Zr, Ta.
- Ti + 2I₂ → TiI₄ (volatilized at ~523K); TiI₄ → Ti + 2I₂ (decomposed at ~1800K on tungsten filament)

Mond's Process (Carbonyl Process):
- Ni + 4CO → Ni(CO)₄ (volatilized at 330-350K); Ni(CO)₄ → Ni + 4CO (decomposed at 450-470K)

Cupellation:
- Used to separate Ag from Pb. Molten PbO is blown onto porous bone-ash cupel; PbO is absorbed, leaving Ag.

Ellingham Diagram:
- Plot of ΔG° versus temperature for formation of oxides (and sulfides, carbides).
- Used to predict feasibility of reduction: if ΔG°(reduction) < ΔG°(oxidation), the reduction is feasible.
- Below the crossover temperature, C can reduce Fe₂O₃; above it, H₂ becomes effective.
- Line slopes depend on ΔS of reaction. For 2M + O₂ → 2MO, ΔS < 0 (gas consumed), line slopes upward.
- For C + O₂ → CO₂, slope ≈ 0 (no change in moles of gas). For 2C + O₂ → 2CO, slope is negative (moles of gas increase).
- C can reduce most metal oxides at sufficiently high temperatures (but not Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃ — these require electrolysis).
- Al can reduce Cr₂O₃, Fe₂O₃, MnO₂ (aluminothermy).
- The diagram also predicts why thermodynamically favorable reactions may not occur kinetically (activation energy barrier).

Important Reactions, Test and Industrial Processes

Qualitative Tests for Common Ions (JEE Advanced Level):
- Fe²⁺: green precipitate with NaOH, soluble in NH₄Cl; turns brown on standing (Fe(OH)₃). With K₃[Fe(CN)₆] → Turnbull's blue. With SCN⁻ → no color (distinguish from Fe³⁺).
- Fe³⁺: reddish-brown ppt with NaOH; blood-red with SCN⁻ ([Fe(SCN)]²⁺); Prussian blue with K₄[Fe(CN)₆].
- Cu²⁺: blue solution; blue ppt with NaOH (soluble in excess NH₃ giving deep blue [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺); white ppt with KSCN turning black (CuSCN).
- Zn²⁺: white ppt with NaOH, soluble in excess (zincate); white ppt with (NH₄)₂S; no color with SCN⁻.
- Al³⁺: white gelatinous ppt with NaOH, soluble in excess (aluminate); red color with alizarin in presence of Al³⁺.
- Pb²⁺: white ppt with NaOH (soluble in excess); yellow ppt with KI (PbI₂, dissolves in hot water, crystallizes on cooling as golden rain); black ppt with H₂S.
- Ag⁺: white ppt with NaCl (AgCl, soluble in NH₃); yellow ppt with K₂CrO₄ (Ag₂CrO₄, brick red).
- Ba²⁺: yellow ppt with K₂CrO₄ (BaCrO₄, insoluble in acetic acid);
- Ca²⁺: white ppt with (NH₄)₂C₂O₄ (CaC₂O₄, soluble in HCl);
- Mn²⁺: flesh-colored ppt with NaOH; pink solution; oxidation to MnO₄⁻ (purple) with (NH₄)₂S₂O₈/Ag⁺.
- Cr³⁺: green ppt with NaOH (soluble in excess forming [Cr(OH)₄]⁻); yellow solution with excess NaOH + H₂O₂ oxidation to chromate.
- Ni²⁺: green solution; green ppt with NaOH (soluble in excess NH₃); pink/red ppt with dimethylglyoxime (dmg) in ammoniacal medium — confirmatory test for Ni²⁺.
- Co²⁺: pink solution; black ppt with (NH₄)₂S; blue complex with SCN⁻ in amyl alcohol layer.

Industrial Processes Summary:
- Haber's Process (NH₃): N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, Fe catalyst, 700K, 200 atm. Backward exothermic — low temp favors yield but kinetics demand moderate temp.
- Contact Process (H₂SO₄): S → SO₂ → SO₃ (V₂O₅, 720K) → oleum → H₂SO₄. Exothermic — multiple passes over catalyst for high conversion.
- Ostwald's Process (HNO₃): 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O (Pt-Rh catalyst, 1073K); 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂; 3NO₂ + H₂O → 2HNO₃ + NO.
- Bayer's Process (Al₂O₃): Bauxite + NaOH → Na[Al(OH)₄] + impurities filtered; CO₂ added → Al₂O₃·xH₂O precipitated; calcined at 1200K → pure Al₂O₃.
- Hall-Héroult Process (Al): Al₂O₃ + cryolite electrolyzed at 950°C; carbon anodes consumed.
- electrolytic Refining (Cu): Impure Cu anode, pure Cu cathode, CuSO₄ + H₂SO₄ electrolyte. Impurities: Ag, Au settle as anode mud; Fe, Zn, Ni dissolve but don't deposit at cathode.

Important Named Compounds and Their Uses:
- Bleaching powder CaOCl₂·H₂O — bleaching agent, disinfectant
- Bleaching powder + NH₃ → N₂ + 3H₂O + 3CaCl₂ (test for NH₃ leaks)
- Washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O — water softening, glass, soap industry
- Baking soda NaHCO₃ — antacid, fire extinguisher (NaHCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂), baking powder component
- Soap: sodium salts of long-chain fatty acids (C₁₂-C₁₈). Hard soaps (Na) vs soft soaps (K). Micelle formation, cleansing action.
- Detergents: sodium alkyl benzene sulfonate — work in hard water (no scum formation).
- Potash alum K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O — water purification, astringent
- Mohr's salt (NH₄)₂SO₄·FeSO₄·6H₂O — primary standard, doesn't oxidize in air like FeSO₄
- Lange's solution: K₂Cr₂O₇ + conc. H₂SO₄ — chromic acid mixture for cleaning glassware
- Tollen's reagent [Ag(NH₃)₂]OH — test for aldehydes (silver mirror)
- Fehling's solution: CuSO₄ + alkaline tartrate — test for aliphatic aldehydes (red Cu₂O ppt)
- Lucas reagent: ZnCl₂ + conc. HCl — distinguishes 1°, 2°, 3° alcohols
- Shear's reagent: CuCl + NH₃ — absorbs CO (distinguishes from CO₂)
- Nessler's reagent: K₂[HgI₄] + KOH — test for NH₃ (yellow-brown ppt)
- Phillips catalyst: PtO₂ — used for hydrogenation (Adams' catalyst)
- Ziegler-Natta catalyst: TiCl₄ + Al(C₂H₅)₃ — polymerization of ethylene to polyethylene
- Wilkinson's catalyst: [RhCl(PPh₃)₃] — homogeneous hydrogenation of alkenes

Color of Salts — Quick Reference:
- White: NaCl, ZnSO₄, BaSO₄, CaCO₃, PbSO₄, AgCl
- Yellow: PbI₂, AgI, FeS, CdS, BaCrO₄, Fe(SCN)₃, Na₂O₂
- Red: Fe(OH)₃ (rusty brown), Cu₂O (red), Ag₂CrO₄ (brick red), HgI₂ (scarlet red)
- Green: FeSO₄·7H₂O, NiCl₂·6H₂O, CrCl₃·6H₂O, FeO, Fe₃O₄ (black-green)

- Pink: MnSO₄, NiSO₄ (dilute), CoCl₂·6H₂O (pink)
- Violet: KMnO₄, [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (very dilute), [Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺

Important facts

  • Fajan's Rules: smaller cation + larger anion + 18e⁻ cation → more covalent character
  • Lanthanoid contraction: poor shielding by 4f electrons causes Zr≈Hf, Nb≈Ta, Mo≈W size similarity
  • Cl has most negative EGE (-349 kJ/mol) — even more negative than F due to F's small size causing inter-electronic repulsion
  • Li is strongest reducing agent in aqueous solution due to highly negative hydration enthalpy of Li⁺ despite highest IE
  • Cu⁺ disproportionates in aqueous solution: 2Cu⁺ → Cu + Cu²⁺
  • Cu²⁺(aq) is more stable than Cu⁺(aq) due to much higher hydration enthalpy of Cu²⁺ compensating for second IE
  • Mn has anomalously high third IE — removing electron from stable d⁵ configuration
  • B₂H₆ has three 3c-2e (banana) bonds in addition to two regular B-H bonds
  • HNO₃ is yellow due to dissolved NO₂ (from photodecomposition: 4HNO₃ → 4NO₂ + 2H₂O + O₂)
  • P₄O₁₀ is the anhydride of H₃PO₄; P₄O₆ is the anhydride of H₃PO₃
  • XeF₂ is linear (sp³d), XeF₄ is square planar (sp³d²), XeF₆ is distorted octahedral
  • NO is paramagnetic (11e⁻ system, MOT: one unpaired electron in π* orbital)
  • O₃ has bent structure with bond angle ~117°, resonance between two equivalent structures
  • Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O (orange to green, used in dichromate titrations)
  • MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (purple to colorless, used in permanganate titrations)
  • [Ni(dmg)₂] is a bright red precipitate — confirmatory test for Ni²⁺
  • Cisplatin [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] is an anticancer drug; transplatin is inactive
  • Born-Haber cycle: ΔH_f = ΔH_sub + IE + ½ΔH_diss + EGE + U
  • Potash alum K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O is a double salt (disintegrates into simple ions in water); complex would retain identity
  • Zeolite ZSM-5 used in petroleum industry for converting alcohols to gasoline-range hydrocarbons (methanol-to-gasoline process)
  • H₂O₂ acts as oxidizing agent in acidic and basic media; as reducing agent in presence of strong oxidants like KMnO₄
  • Thermal stability of carbonates increases down each group: BeCO₃ < MgCO₃ < CaCO₃ < SrCO₃ < BaCO₃
  • SOCl₂ used to prepare anhydrous MgCl₂ from MgCl₂·6H₂O to prevent hydrolysis
  • Borax bead test: Co²⁺ gives blue bead (Co(BO₂)₂); Cr³⁺ gives green bead (Cr(BO₂)₃); Cu²⁺ gives blue bead (Cu(BO₂)₂) when hot, red when cold
  • Mohr's salt is preferred over FeSO₄ in titrations because it is not oxidized by air (NH₄⁺ provides acidic medium preventing Fe²⁺ oxidation)
