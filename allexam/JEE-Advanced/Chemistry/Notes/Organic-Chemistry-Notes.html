General Organic Chemistry (GOC): Electronic Effects and Stability

General Organic Chemistry forms the foundation of understanding reaction mechanisms in organic chemistry. The central theme is how electrons move within molecules and how electronic effects determine the stability of intermediates and the reactivity of functional groups.

Inductive Effect (I-effect):
The permanent displacement of sigma electrons along a carbon chain due to the presence of an electron-withdrawing or electron-donating group. It operates through sigma bonds and decreases with distance. Electron-withdrawing groups (EWG) such as –NO₂, –CN, –COOH, –F, –Cl pull electrons toward themselves; electron-donating groups (EDG) such as –CH₃, –OCH₃, –NH₂ push electrons away. The order of –I effect among halogens: F > Cl > Br > I. The +I (electron-releasing inductive) effect order among alkyl groups: t-butyl > i-propyl > ethyl > methyl.

Resonance Effect (Mesomeric Effect, M-effect):
The delocalization of π electrons or lone pair electrons across conjugated systems. It is a stronger and longer-range effect than the inductive effect. +M groups donate lone pairs into the π system: –OH, –OR, –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂, –O⁻, –OCOCH₃. –M groups withdraw π electrons: –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COOH, –COOR, –CONH₂, –SO₃H. Resonance stabilization energy is measurable — benzene has ~36 kcal/mol resonance energy. In conjugated systems like buta-1,3-diene, the π electrons are delocalized over all four carbons.

Hyperconjugation (Baker-Nathan Effect):
The delocalization of σ electrons of C–H bonds of an alkyl group directly attached to a conjugated system or an unsaturated center. More α-hydrogens mean greater hyperconjugative stabilization. Order of stability of alkyl carbocations: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺. For free radicals: 3° > 2° > 1°. For carbanions, the order reverses: CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°. Hyperconjugation also explains the stability of alkenes: more substituted alkenes are more stable (Zaitsev's rule).

Aromaticity (Hückel's Rule):
A cyclic, planar, fully conjugated compound with (4n+2) π electrons is aromatic. Examples: benzene (6π), cyclopentadienyl anion (6π), pyridine (6π). Anti-aromatic compounds are cyclic, planar, conjugated with 4n π electrons (e.g., cyclobutadiene, 4π). Non-aromatic compounds fail one or more criteria (not planar, not fully conjugated, etc.).

Acidity and Basicity Trends:
Acidity increases with electron withdrawal and resonance stabilization of the conjugate base. Phenol (pKa ≈ 10) is more acidic than ethanol but less acidic than carboxylic acids (pKa ≈ 4–5). Picric acid (2,4,6-trinitrophenol) is one of the strongest phenolic acids due to three –NO₂ groups. The order of acidic strength: carboxylic acids > phenols > alcohols. For bases, the availability of the lone pair determines basicity: NH₃ > aniline (resonance delocalization reduces availability). Among amines in aqueous medium: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃ (due to combined solvation and inductive effects). Pyridine is less basic than piperidine because the lone pair in pyridine resides in an sp² orbital, while in piperidine it is in an sp³ orbital.

Reaction Intermediates and Mechanisms

Understanding reaction intermediates is crucial for predicting the course of organic reactions.

Carbocations (R₃C⁺): Planar sp² hybridized, empty p-orbital. Stability order: allyl/benzyl > 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺ > vinyl/aryl. Rearrangements (1,2-hydride shift, 1,2-methyl shift) occur when a more stable carbocation can be formed. Example: neopentyl bromide undergoes rearrangement in SN1.

Carbanions (R₃C⁻): Pyramidal sp³ hybridized, lone pair in sp³ orbital. Stability order: CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°. Stabilized by EWG (–NO₂, –CN, –C=O) through resonance and –I effect. Acetone enolate and malonate esters are classic stabilized carbanions.

Free Radicals (R₃C·): Planar sp² hybridized. Stability: allyl/benzyl > 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃·. Generated homolytically (peroxide, UV light, heat). Bromination is selective for the most stable radical (tertiary > secondary > primary).

Carbenes (:CR₂): Neutral, divalent, highly reactive species. Singlet carbene has paired electrons in one orbital; triplet carbene has two unpaired electrons in different orbitals (diradical character). CH₂ generated from diazomethane photolysis inserts into C–H bonds. With alkenes, carbenes form cyclopropanes.

Benzyne (Aryne): Intermediate formed in nucleophilic substitution of aryl halides under strong basic conditions (e.g., chlorobenzene + NaNH₂/liq. NH₃ → aniline via benzyne). Benzyne is a highly strained, reactive intermediate with a triple bond incorporated in a six-membered ring.

Key Mechanistic Types:
- SN1: Unimolecular nucleophilic substitution; rate = k[RX]; involves carbocation intermediate; racemization occurs; favored by polar protic solvents and tertiary substrates.
- SN2: Bimolecular; rate = k[RX][Nu⁻]; backside attack; inversion of configuration (Walden inversion); favored by polar aprotic solvents and primary substrates.
- E1: Unimolecular elimination; competes with SN1; follows Zaitsev's rule.
- E2: Bimolecular elimination; requires anti-periplanar geometry of H and leaving group.
- Electrophilic aromatic substitution: EAS mechanism with arenium ion (sigma complex) intermediate.
- Nucleophilic acyl substitution: proceeds through tetrahedral intermediate; order of reactivity of acid derivatives: acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide.

Isomerism in Organic Compounds

Isomerism is classified into structural isomerism and stereoisomerism.

Structural Isomerism:
- Chain isomerism: different carbon skeletons (e.g., n-butane and isobutane).
- Position isomerism: different position of functional group/double bond/substituent (e.g., 1-chloropropane vs 2-chloropropane).
- Functional isomerism: different functional groups (e.g., alcohol-ether, aldehyde-ketone, carboxylic acid-ester, nitro-alcohol).
- Metamerism: different alkyl chains on either side of a bridging functional group (e.g., diethyl ether vs methyl propyl ether).
- Tautomerism: dynamic equilibrium between two structural isomers differing in position of a proton (e.g., keto-enol tautomerism; acetone has negligible enol content ~10⁻⁷%, but acetylacetone has significant enol form ~76% due to intramolecular H-bonding and conjugation).

Stereoisomerism:
- Geometric (cis-trans/E-Z) isomerism: restricted rotation around C=C or in cyclic compounds. E/Z notation: priority rules (CIP) determine higher-priority groups. cis/trans: similar groups on same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans). For C=C with four different groups, use E/Z system.
- Optical isomerism: chirality arising from a carbon atom bonded to four different groups (stereocenter). Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images; they rotate plane-polarized light in equal magnitude but opposite directions.
- D/L and R/S nomenclature: D/L based on glyceraldehyde reference; R/S (CIP priority rules) is unambiguous. R = clockwise, S = anticlockwise when lowest priority group is pointing away.
- Meso compounds: contain chiral centers but possess a plane of symmetry, making the molecule optically inactive. Example: meso-tartaric acid has (R,S) configuration with internal compensation.
- Number of stereoisomers: for n chiral centers with no symmetry, max = 2ⁿ. With symmetry (meso possibility), fewer isomers exist.
- Conformational isomerism: different spatial arrangements due to rotation around single bonds. For n-butane: anti > gauche > eclipsed (staggered conformations more stable). Cyclohexane: chair conformation is most stable; boat is least stable; half-chair and twist-boat are transition states.

Hydrocarbons: Alkanes, Alkenes, Alkynes and Aromatic Compounds

Alkanes (CnH2n+2):
Preparation: Wurtz reaction (2RX + 2Na → R–R + 2NaX), hydrogenation of alkenes/alkynes over Pt/Pd/Ni, reduction of alkyl halides (Zn/HCl, LiAlH₄). Reactions: free radical halogenation (selectivity: 3°H > 2°H > 1°H for Cl; even more selective for Br), combustion, cracking. Conformations of butane: anti is most stable (no steric strain), fully eclipsed is least stable.

Alkenes (CnH2n):
Preparation: dehydration of alcohols (H₂SO₄, heat), dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides (alc. KOH, follows Zaitsev's rule), reduction of alkynes (Lindlar's catalyst for cis, Na/NH₃(l) for trans). Markovnikov's rule: H adds to the carbon with more H's; X adds to the carbon with fewer H's (carbocation stability governs). Anti-Markovnikov addition: HBr in presence of peroxide (Kharasch effect) gives radical mechanism → opposite regioselectivity. Only applies to HBr, not HCl or HI. Reactions: hydration (H₂O/H⁺), hydroboration-oxidation (BH₃·THF then H₂O₂/OH⁻ → anti-Markovnikov alcohol, syn addition), ozonolysis (O₃ then Zn/H₂O → aldehydes/ketones; O₃ then H₂O₂ → carboxylic acids), halogenation (Br₂/CCl₄ → vicinal dibromide, anti addition), polymerization.

Alkynes (CnH2n-2):
Preparation: from vicinal/dihalides by double dehydrohalogenation (alc. KOH), from calcium carbide (CaC₂ + H₂O → HC≡CH). Terminal alkynes are weakly acidic (pKa ≈ 25); react with strong bases (NaNH₂, NaNH₄) to form acetylides. Reactions: hydration (HgSO₄/H₂SO₄ → enol → tautomerizes to ketone; with terminal alkyne → methyl ketone via Markovnikov); hydroboration-oxidation → aldehyde (anti-Markovnikov); reduction to cis/trans alkenes; addition of halogens (1 eq → dihaloalkene; 2 eq → tetrahaloalkane).

Aromatic Compounds (Benzene and derivatives):
Electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) reactions: nitration (HNO₃/H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺), sulfonation (fuming H₂SO₄ → SO₃ as electrophile), Friedel-Crafts alkylation (RX/AlCl₃ → carbocation electrophile; suffers from polyalkylation and rearrangement), Friedel-Crafts acylation (RCOCl/AlCl₃ → acylium ion R–C≡O⁺; does not rearrange, no polyacylation). Directing effects: o,p-directors activate ring (–OH, –NH₂, –OCH₃, –CH₃); m-directors deactivate ring (–NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COOH, –SO₃H). Steric effects can favor para product. Stork-Eschenmoser synthesis, Clemmensen reduction (Zn(Hg)/HCl) and Wolff-Kishner reduction (NH₂NH₂/KOH, glycol) reduce C=O to CH₂.

Halogen Derivatives: Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

Haloalkanes (Alkyl Halides, R–X):
Preparation: from alcohols (HX, PX₃, PX₅, SOCl₂), free radical halogenation of alkanes, addition of HX to alkenes (Markovnikov). SN1 vs SN2 comparison:
- SN2: primary > secondary >> tertiary (not feasible); single step; inversion of configuration; strong nucleophile needed; polar aprotic solvent favors. Rearrangements do NOT occur.
- SN1: tertiary > secondary >> primary; two steps (carbocation formation is RDS); racemization; weak nucleophile; polar protic solvent favors. Rearrangements possible.

Solvolysis (SN1 with solvent as nucleophile): water → alcohol, alcohol → ether. Neighboring group participation (anchimeric assistance): –COO⁻ or –SH can participate forming cyclic intermediate, leading to retention of configuration. Finkelstein reaction: R–Cl/Br + NaI/acetone → R–I (NaCl/NaBr precipitates, driving equilibrium). Swarts reaction: R–Cl/Br + AgF/Hg₂F₂/SbF₃ → R–F.

Haloarenes (Aryl Halides, Ar–X):
Less reactive than alkyl halides toward nucleophilic substitution because: (1) partial double bond character due to resonance, (2) sp² hybridized C–X bond is shorter and stronger, (3) phenyl carbocation is highly unstable. However, under extreme conditions (high T, high P) or with strong electron-withdrawing groups at ortho/para positions, nucleophilic substitution occurs. Benzyne mechanism: strong base (NaNH₂) abstracts ortho-H, forming benzyne intermediate, then Nu⁻ attacks. Dow process: chlorobenzene + NaOH (623K, 300atm) → phenol.

Important named reactions involving halides:
- Wurtz reaction: 2R–X + 2Na (dry ether) → R–R
- Grignard reagent: R–X + Mg (dry ether) → R–Mg–X (powerful nucleophile and base)
- Corey-House synthesis: R–X + LiAlH₄ or organocuprate → coupled product

Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Alcohols (R–OH):
Preparation: hydration of alkenes (acid-catalyzed, Markovnikov; hydroboration-oxidation gives anti-Markovnikov), reduction of carbonyl compounds (NaBH₄ reduces aldehydes/ketones; LiAlH₄ reduces all carbonyls including acids and esters), Grignard reaction with carbonyls (formaldehyde → primary alcohol; other aldehydes → secondary; ketones → tertiary), fermentation. Classification: primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°).

Reactions: oxidation (PCC → aldehyde from 1° alcohol without over-oxidation; K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺ → carboxylic acid; hot KMnO₄ also gives acid). Secondary alcohol → ketone; tertiary alcohols resist oxidation. Lucas test: conc. HCl + ZnCl₂; 3° alcohol reacts immediately (turidity), 2° in ~5 min, 1° on heating. Dehydration: conc. H₂SO₄ at 170°C → alkene (follows Zaitsev's rule; E1 mechanism for 2°/3° alcohols). Esterification: with carboxylic acids (acid catalyst). Reaction with active metals (Na → alkoxide + H₂).

Phenols (Ar–OH):
Preparation: Dow process (from chlorobenzene), cumene hydroperoxide method (industrial), from diazonium salts (ArN₂⁺ + H₂O → ArOH). Phenol is more acidic than alcohols because the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilized. Substituent effects: –NO₂ (especially at ortho/para) increases acidity; –OCH₃ decreases acidity. Reactions: electrophilic substitution ( bromination → 2,4,6-tribromophenol in water; in CS₂ → monobromophenol), nitration (dilute HNO₃ → o-/p-nitrophenol), Kolbe-Schmitt reaction (Na phenoxide + CO₂ → salicylic acid, ortho-carboxylation), Reimer-Tiemann reaction (CHCl₃ + NaOH → dichlorocarbene; forms o-hydroxybenzaldehyde), Gattermann-Koch formylation.

Ethers (R–O–R'):
Preparation: Williamson synthesis (R–X + R'O⁻Na⁺ → R–O–R'; primary halide best to avoid elimination). Cleavage by HI/HBr: ether bond breaks, giving alcohol and alkyl halide. With excess HI, both groups convert to alkyl iodides. The smaller alkyl group forms the iodide (SN2 on less hindered carbon).

Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

Aldehydes and Ketones (Carbonyl Compounds, RCHO / RCOR'):
Preparation: aldehydes — Rosenmund reduction (acid chloride + H₂/Pd-BaSO₄), Stephen reduction (RCN + SnCl₂/HCl → imine → H₂O → aldehyde), partial oxidation of primary alcohols (PCC). Ketones — oxidation of 2° alcohols, ozonolysis of alkenes, Friedel-Crafts acylation, hydration of alkynes (terminal → methyl ketone).

Reactivity — nucleophilic addition is the characteristic reaction. The carbonyl carbon is electrophilic (δ⁺). Order of reactivity: formaldehyde > aliphatic aldehydes > aromatic aldehydes > ketones (steric + electronic factors). Addition of HCN → cyanohydrin; NaHSO₃ → bisulfite adduct; ROH → acetal/ketal (requires acid catalyst and removal of water); NH₃ derivative → imine/oxime/hydrazone (each gives crystalline solid used for identification).

Alpha-hydrogen reactions: aldol condensation (2 molecules with α-H, dilute NaOH → β-hydroxy aldehyde/ketone → Δ → α,β-unsaturated carbonyl); crossed aldol (two different carbonyls, one must have no α-H); Cannizzaro reaction (no α-H, concentrated NaOH → alcohol + carboxylate);

Oxidation: Tollens' reagent (Ag⁺/NH₃) → aldehydes give silver mirror; Fehling's solution (Cu²⁺) → aliphatic aldehydes give red Cu₂O precipitate; ketones do not respond. Iodoform test (I₂/NaOH): methyl ketones (CH₃COR) and ethanol give yellow precipitate of CHI₃.

Reduction: Clemmensen (Zn(Hg)/HCl) and Wolff-Kishner (NH₂NH₂/KOH, glycol, Δ) reduce C=O to CH₂. NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄ reduce C=O to alcohol.

Carboxylic Acids (RCOOH):
Preparation: oxidation of primary alcohols/aldehydes (KMnO₄/H⁺ or K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺), oxidation of alkylbenzenes (KMnO₄ → benzoic acid), hydrolysis of nitriles/esters/amides, CO₂ + Grignard → carboxylic acid, haloform reaction (CH₃COX from methyl ketone), oxidation of acetylene.

Acidity: influenced by inductive and resonance effects. Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity (e.g., chloroacetic acid > acetic acid). Ortho effect: ortho-substituted benzoic acids are generally stronger regardless of the nature of the substituent. Salt formation with NaHCO₃ (effervescence of CO₂ distinguishes acids from phenols).

Reactions: esterification (Fischer — acid-catalyzed with alcohol), reduction to primary alcohols (LiAlH₄; NaBH₄ does not reduce acids), conversion to acid chlorides (SOCl₂), anhydrides, amides. Decarboxylation: soda-lime (NaOH/CaO) heating of sodium salt of carboxylic acid → alkane with one less carbon. HVZ reaction (Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky): α-halogenation of carboxylic acids using PBr₃/P + Br₂ (only occurs if α-H is present).

Chemical Tests Summary for Identification:
- 2,4-DNP test: detects aldehydes and ketones (yellow-orange precipitate)
- Tollens': distinguishes aldehydes from ketones
- Fehling's: distinguishes aliphatic from aromatic aldehydes
- Iodoform: detects CH₃CO– or CH₃CH(OH)– groups

Amines, Diazonium Salts and Nitrogen-Containing Compounds

Amines (R–NH₂, R₂NH, R₃N):
Classification: primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°). Preparation: reduction of nitro compounds (Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl or H₂/Pd), Gabriel phthalimide synthesis (for pure primary amines only — phthalimide + KOH → potassium phthalimide + RX → N-alkylphthalimide + NH₂/NH₃ → primary amine), Hofmann bromamide degradation (primary amide + Br₂ + NaOH → primary amine with one carbon less), reduction of nitriles (LiAlH₄ → primary amine), reductive amination (RCHO + NH₃ + H₂/Ni → primary amine; with primary amine → secondary).

Basicity: In gas phase, 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃ (pure inductive effect). In aqueous phase, the order is modified by solvation effect and steric hindrance. For methyl amines: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃. For ethyl amines: 2° > 3° > 1° > NH₃. Aromatic amines (aniline) are much less basic than ammonia because the lone pair is delocalized into the ring. Electron-donating groups on the ring increase basicity; electron-withdrawing groups decrease it. Order: (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃ in aqueous medium.

Reactions: alkylation (exhaustive methylation with excess CH₃I → quaternary ammonium salt), acylation (with acid chlorides/anhydrides — 1° and 2° amines react, 3° do not), carbylamine reaction (primary amine + CHCl₃ + KOH → foul-smelling isocyanide; specific test for 1° amines), reaction with HNO₂ (1° aliphatic → N₂ gas + alcohol; 1° aromatic → diazonium salt; 2° → N-nitrosoamine; 3° aliphatic → soluble nitrite salt; 3° aromatic → C-nitrosation at para position).

Diazonium Salts (ArN₂⁺X⁻):
Prepared by diazotization: aniline + NaNO₂ + HCl (0–5°C) → benzene diazonium chloride. Extremely useful synthetic intermediates. Reactions: replacement of –N₂⁺ by –OH (warm with H₂O), –H (warm with H₃PO₂), –Cl/–Br (CuCl/CuBr — Sandmeyer), –CN (CuCN — Sandmeyer), –I (with KI directly). Coupling reactions: with phenols (alkaline) → azo dyes (para coupling); with aniline (acidic) → azo dyes. Bazua-Demetriou reaction: diazonium salt + Cu powder + HBr → bromobenzene.

Other Nitrogen Compounds:
Nitro compounds (RNO₂): prepared by nitration of alkanes (vapor phase, 400–500°C), from alkyl halides (AgNO₂ → nitroalkane; NaNO₂ → nitrite ester). Nitroalkanes with α-H are acidic (pKa ≈ 10) due to resonance stabilization of nitronate anion. Reduction: Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl → amine; Zn/NH₄Cl → nitroso; Zn/alc. KOH → oxime; Na/EtOH → hydroxylamine.

Biomolecules, Polymers and Chemistry in Everyday Life

Biomolecules:
Carbohydrates: classified as monosaccharides (cannot hydrolyze further), oligosaccharides (2–10 units), polysaccharides (many units). Glucose: aldohexose; fructose: ketohexose. Mutarotation: interconversion of α and β anomers in aqueous solution via open-chain form. Glycosidic linkage: C–O–C bond formed between two monosaccharides with loss of water. Starch (α-1,4 and α-1,6 linkages; amylose + amylopectin), cellulose (β-1,4 linkages; human cannot digest), glycogen (highly branched).

Proteins: polypeptides linked by peptide bonds (–CO–NH–). Primary, secondary (α-helix, β-pleated sheet via H-bonding), tertiary, and quaternary structures. Denaturation: loss of 3D structure by heat/pH change without breaking peptide bonds. Essential amino acids: 9 amino acids that cannot be synthesized by humans (must be obtained from diet).

Vitamins: fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) stored in liver; water-soluble (B-complex, C) not stored. Vitamin C deficiency → scurvy; Vitamin D deficiency → rickets; Vitamin A deficiency → night blindness.

Nucleic Acids: DNA (deoxyribose sugar, thymine, double helix, stores genetic information); RNA (ribose sugar, uracil, usually single-stranded, involved in protein synthesis). Purine bases: adenine, guanine; Pyrimidine bases: cytosine, thymine (DNA), uracil (RNA).

Enzymes: biological catalysts (mostly proteins); lock-and-key vs induced-fit models; denatured enzymes lose catalytic activity.

Polymers:
Classification by source: natural (cellulose, proteins, rubber), synthetic (polyethylene, nylon, PVC).
By polymerization mechanism: addition polymers (monomers with C=C bonds add without loss of small molecules — e.g., polyethylene from ethene, Teflon from CF₂=CF₂, PMMA from methyl methacrylate); condensation polymers (monomers with two functional groups react with loss of small molecule — e.g., nylon-6,6 from hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid losing H₂O; terylene/DACRON from ethylene glycol + terephthalic acid; bakelite from phenol + formaldehyde).
By mode of polymerization: chain-growth (free radical, ionic) vs step-growth (condensation).
Biodegradable polymers: PHBV (poly β-hydroxybutyrate-co-β-hydroxyvalerate), nylon-2-nylon-6.
Thermoplastics (soften on heating, can be remolded: PE, PVC, PS) vs thermosetting plastics (cross-linked, cannot be remelted: bakelite, melamine).

Chemistry in Everyday Life:
Analgesics: aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), paracetamol (acetaminophen); antipyretics reduce fever.
Antibiotics: penicillin (bactericidal, inhibits cell wall synthesis), streptomycin, chloramphenicol (broad-spectrum, causes grey baby syndrome).
Tranquilizers: barbiturates (hypnotic, sedative), valium (diazepam).
Antiseptics (kill microorganisms on living tissue, e.g., phenol, benzalkonium chloride) vs disinfectants (kill microorganisms on inanimate objects, e.g., 0.2% phenol).
Antibiotics vs Antiseptics: phenol at 0.1% is antiseptic, at 1% is disinfectant.
Soaps: sodium/potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids; cleansing action due to micelle formation. Hard water ions (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) precipitate soaps as scum.
Detergents: sodium lauryl sulfate (anionic), cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (cationic), non-ionic detergents (work in hard water). Synthetic detergents are preferred over soaps for washing in hard water.

Named Reactions and Important Conversions for JEE Advanced

Mastering named reactions is essential for JEE Advanced. These are transformations that appear repeatedly in problem-solving contexts.

Carbon-Carbon Bond Forming Reactions:
- Aldol Condensation: 2 RCH₂CHO → RCH₂CH(OH)CHR(CHO) (dil. NaOH) → RCH₂CH=C(R)CHO + H₂O (heat)
- Crossed Aldol: Two different carbonyl compounds, one must lack α-H (e.g., benzaldehyde + acetaldehyde)
- Cannizzaro Reaction: 2 RCHO (no α-H) + conc. NaOH → RCH₂OH + RCOONa
- Claisen Condensation: 2 esters with α-H + alkoxide → β-keto ester
- Perkin Reaction: aromatic aldehyde + acid anhydride (sodium salt of corresponding acid) → α,β-unsaturated aromatic acid
- Knoevenagel Condensation: aldehyde/ketone + active methylene compound (malonic ester, malononitrile) + base → α,β-unsaturated product
- Wittig Reaction: R₂C=O + Ph₃P=CR₂ → R₂C=CR₂ + Ph₃P=O
- Robinson Annulation: Michael addition + intramolecular aldol → six-membered ring
- Friedel-Crafts Alkylation/Acylation: ArH + RCl/AlCl₃ → ArR; ArH + RCOCl/AlCl₃ → ArCOR
- Grignard Reaction: R'MgX + RCHO/R₂CO → alcohol after hydrolysis
- Reformatsky Reaction: α-halo ester + Zn → organozinc + aldehyde/ketone → β-hydroxy ester
- McMurry Reaction: 2 R₂C=O + TiCl₃/LiAlH₄ → R₂C=CR₂ (coupling to alkene)

Reduction Reactions:
- Clemmensen Reduction: R₂C=O + Zn(Hg)/conc. HCl → R₂CH₂
- Wolff-Kishner Reduction: R₂C=O + NH₂NH₂/KOH (glycol, Δ) → R₂CH₂
- Bouveault-Blanc Reduction: ester + Na/ethanol → primary alcohol
- Lindlar's Catalyst: alkyne + H₂/Lindlar → cis-alkene
- Na/NH₃(l): alkyne → trans-alkene

Oxidation Reactions:
- Jones Oxidation: 1°/2° alcohol + CrO₃/H₂SO₄ → carboxylic acid/ketone
- Oppenauer Oxidation: 2° alcohol + Al(OiPr)₃ + acetone → ketone
- Manganese Acetate Oxidation: allylic/benzylic C–H → alcohol/ketone
- Etard Reaction: toluene + CrO₂Cl₂/CS₂ → benzaldehyde (after hydrolysis)
-_side chain oxidation: any alkylbenzene + KMnO₄/OH⁻/Δ → benzoic acid

Special Named Reactions:
- HVZ Reaction: RCH₂COOH + Br₂/P → RCHBrCOOH (α-bromination)
- Hunsdiecker Reaction: RCOOAg + Br₂ → RBr + CO₂ (decarboxylative bromination)
- Balz-Schiemann Reaction: ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻ → ArF + N₂ + BF₃ (thermal decomposition)
- Sandmeyer/Gattermann: ArN₂⁺ + CuX → ArX (X = Cl, Br, CN)
- Gomberg-Bach反应: trityl radical formation from triphenylmethyl halide

Spectroscopy: IR, NMR and Mass Spectrometry

Spectroscopic methods are vital for structure determination in JEE Advanced.

Mass Spectrometry (MS):
Molecular ion peak (M⁺) gives molecular weight. Base peak is the most intense (100% relative abundance). Isotope patterns: Cl → M and M+2 peaks in 3:1 ratio (³⁵Cl:³⁷Cl); Br → M and M+2 in 1:1 ratio (⁷⁹Br:⁸¹Br). Nitrogen rule: compounds with odd number of nitrogen atoms have odd molecular mass.

Fragmentation patterns:
- α-cleavage (next to heteroatom): common in alcohols, amines
- McLafferty rearrangement: γ-H transfer in carbonyl compounds with γ-H → distinctive peak
- Loss of 15 (CH₃), 29 (C₂H₅ or CHO), 31 (CH₂OH), 45 (COOH), 77 (C₆H₅), 91 (C₇H₇⁺, tropylium ion from benzyl)

Infrared Spectroscopy (IR):
Absorption frequency depends on bond strength and reduced mass. Key ranges:
- O–H stretch (alcohol): 3200–3600 cm⁻¹, broad
- O–H stretch (carboxylic acid): 2500–3300 cm⁻¹, very broad
- N–H stretch: 3300–3500 cm⁻¹
- C=O stretch: 1650–1750 cm⁻¹ (amide ~1650, ester ~1735, acid ~1710, ketone ~1715, aldehyde ~1725)
- C≡N stretch: 2200–2260 cm⁻¹
- C≡C stretch: 2100–2260 cm⁻¹
- C–H stretch (sp³): 2850–3000 cm⁻¹; (sp²): 3000–3100 cm⁻¹; (sp): ~3300 cm⁻¹
- C=C stretch: 1600–1680 cm⁻¹
- Aromatic C–H: just above 3000 cm⁻¹; overtone pattern (1600–2000 cm⁻¹) indicates substitution pattern
- NO₂ symmetric/asymmetric stretches: ~1550 and ~1350 cm⁻¹

NMR Spectroscopy (¹H NMR):
Chemical shift (δ, ppm): CH₃–R ~0.9; –CH₂– ~1.3; –CH– ~1.5; allylic ~2; α to C=O ~2.1–2.5; α to electronegative atom (O, X) ~3–4; vinylic ~4.5–6.5; aromatic ~6.5–8.5; aldehyde H ~9–10; carboxylic acid H ~10–13; phenolic O–H ~4–7 (variable, broad); alcoholic O–H ~1–5 (variable, broad).

Integration: area under peak proportional to number of equivalent protons.
Splitting (n+1 rule): a proton with n neighboring equivalent protons splits into n+1 peaks. Common patterns: singlet, doublet, triplet, quartet, multiplet. Coupling constant J (Hz) gives information about dihedral angle (Karplus equation) and stereochemistry.

¹³C NMR: each chemically unique carbon gives one signal. DEPT experiments distinguish CH₃, CH₂, CH, and C (quaternary). Typical shifts: sp³ C ~0–50; C–O/C–X ~50–90; sp² (alkene/aromatic) ~100–160; C=O ~160–220.

Common Mistakes and Conceptual Confusions

Students frequently make errors in organic chemistry. Being aware of these pitfalls is essential for scoring well.

1. Markovnikov vs Anti-Markovnikov confusion: Markovnikov addition of HX to alkenes places H on the carbon with more hydrogens. Anti-Markovnikov occurs ONLY with HBr in the presence of peroxide (Kharasch effect) — NOT with HCl or HI. The radical mechanism is responsible.

2. SN1 vs SN2 conditions: Students often forget that SN1 favors tertiary substrates and polar protic solvents (water, alcohol), while SN2 favors primary substrates and polar aprotic solvents (DMF, DMSO, acetone). Polar protic solvents solvate nucleophiles, reducing their reactivity and favoring SN1.

3. Phenol vs Alcohol acidity: Phenol is about 10⁶ times more acidic than ethanol because the phenoxide ion's negative charge is delocalized over the aromatic ring. Many students incorrectly assume all –OH groups behave similarly.

4. Basicity order in gas vs aqueous phase: In gas phase: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃. In aqueous phase: solvation effects reverse this partially. For methylamines: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃. This reversal is a frequent exam question.

5. Aromaticity criteria: Not all cyclic conjugated systems are aromatic. Must satisfy ALL: (1) cyclic, (2) planar, (3) fully conjugated, (4) (4n+2) π electrons. Cyclooctatetraene (8π) is tub-shaped (non-planar) and therefore non-aromatic, not anti-aromatic.

6. Friedel-Crafts limitations: FC acylation does not suffer from rearrangement of the acylium ion (unlike alkylation). Also, FC reactions fail with strongly deactivated rings (e.g., nitrobenzene) and with aniline (the –NH₂ group complexes with AlCl₃, deactivating the ring).

7. Tollens' vs Fehling's: Both detect aldehydes, but Fehling's does NOT oxidize aromatic aldehydes. Tollens' works for both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes. Ketones (except α-hydroxy ketones) give neither test.

8. Iodoform test scope: It tests for CH₃CO– group AND CH₃CH(OH)– group. Ethanol and acetaldehyde both give positive iodoform test despite being very different compounds.

9. Carbocation rearrangement: Always check for possible 1,2-hydride or 1,2-alkyl shifts when a carbocation intermediate forms. Neopentyl systems and certain secondary carbocations adjacent to tertiary centers are classic rearrangement scenarios.

10. EAS directing effects vs activating/deactivating: o,p-directors are generally activating (except halogens, which are o,p-directing but deactivating). m-directors are always deactivating. Halogens are the exception — they are deactivating yet ortho-para directing.

Important facts

  • Hückel's rule: aromatic if cyclic, planar, conjugated, and has (4n+2) π electrons
  • Anti-Markovnikov addition of HBr occurs ONLY in presence of peroxide (radical mechanism)
  • SN1 rate depends only on substrate concentration; SN2 depends on both substrate and nucleophile
  • Phenol is more acidic than alcohol due to resonance stabilization of phenoxide ion
  • In aqueous phase, methylamine basicity order is 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃
  • Tollens' reagent oxidizes both aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes; Fehling's oxidizes only aliphatic
  • Iodoform test is positive for CH₃CO– and CH₃CH(OH)– containing compounds
  • Gabriel phthalimide synthesis gives ONLY primary amines
  • Hofmann bromamide degradation produces primary amine with one carbon less than the amide
  • Clemmensen reduction uses Zn(Hg)/HCl; Wolff-Kishner uses NH₂NH₂/KOH in glycol
  • Lindlar's catalyst gives cis-alkene from alkyne; Na/NH₃(l) gives trans-alkene
  • HVZ reaction requires P or PBr₃ as catalyst for α-halogenation of carboxylic acids
  • Sandmeyer reaction converts diazonium salt to ArCl, ArBr, or ArCN using copper salts
  • Kolbe-Schmitt reaction: Na phenoxide + CO₂ → salicylic acid (ortho-carboxylation)
  • Reimer-Tiemann reaction: phenol + CHCl₃ + NaOH → o-hydroxybenzaldehyde
  • Carbocation stability: allyl ≈ benzyl > 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺
  • Free radical stability: allyl ≈ benzyl > 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃·
  • Carbanion stability: CH₃⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3° (reverse of carbocation)
  • Conformational stability of butane: anti > gauche > eclipsed > fully eclipsed
  • Bouveault-Blanc reduction: Na/ethanol reduces esters to primary alcohols
  • Etard reaction: toluene + CrO₂Cl₂/CS₂ → benzaldehyde after hydrolysis
  • Nitrogen rule: odd molecular mass indicates odd number of nitrogen atoms
  • Chlorine isotope pattern: M and M+2 peaks in 3:1 ratio in mass spectra
  • Para substitution gives symmetrical aromatic signals in ¹H NMR
  • Carbonyl stretching frequency order: acid chloride > anhydride > ester ≈ aldehyde > ketone > amide
  • Tropylium ion (C₇H₇⁺, m/z 91) is a very stable carbocation fragment in MS

Formulas and relationships

Hückel's Rule: (4n + 2) π electrons for aromaticity (n = 0, 1, 2, ...)
Maximum stereoisomers: 2ⁿ (n = number of chiral centers, no symmetry)
SN1 rate law: Rate = k[substrate]
SN2 rate law: Rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]
E1 rate law: Rate = k[substrate]
E2 rate law: Rate = k[substrate][base]
Degree of Unsaturation (DU) = (2C + 2 + N - H - X)/2
CIP Priority Rules: higher atomic number gets higher priority
R/S Configuration: clockwise = R, anticlockwise = S (lowest priority away)
IUPAC Name construction: substituents (alphabetical) + parent chain + suffix
Zaitsev's Rule: major product is the more substituted alkene in elimination
Markovnikov's Rule: H adds to carbon with more H's in electrophilic addition
Hammett Equation: log(k/k₀) = ρσ (relates substituent effects to reactivity)
Bredt's Rule: double bond cannot be placed at bridgehead of small bicyclic systems
Anisotropic effect: ring current in benzene causes deshielding of aromatic protons (~7-8 ppm in NMR)
