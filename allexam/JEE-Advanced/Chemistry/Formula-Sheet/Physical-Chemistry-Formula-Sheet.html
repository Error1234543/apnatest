E = hν = hc/λ

Symbols: E = energy of photon, h = Planck's constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s), ν = frequency, c = speed of light (3 × 10⁸ m/s), λ = wavelength

Unit: Joules (J)

When to use: Energy of electromagnetic radiation; used in photoelectric effect, atomic spectra calculations

Conditions: Valid for photons in vacuum; in medium, replace c with v = c/n

Common mistake: Forgetting to convert nm to m when calculating E; confusing frequency ν with angular frequency ω

λ_db = h/(mv) = h/√(2mKE)

Symbols: λ_db = de Broglie wavelength, m = mass of particle, v = velocity, KE = kinetic energy

Unit: metre (m)

When to use: Calculating wavelength of any moving matter particle; crucial for electron diffraction and atomic models

Conditions: Non-relativistic case shown; for relativistic particles use λ = h/p where p = γmv

Common mistake: Using rest mass instead of relativistic mass at high velocities; forgetting that this applies to ALL matter, not just electrons

ΔE = -R_H · Z² · (1/n_f² - 1/n_i²)

Symbols: ΔE = energy change, R_H = Rydberg constant for energy (2.179 × 10⁻¹⁸ J), Z = atomic number, n_i = initial orbit, n_f = final orbit

Unit: Joules (J) per atom

When to use: Energy emitted/absorbed in hydrogen-like species during electron transitions; spectral line calculations

Conditions: Hydrogen-like (single electron) species only: H, He⁺, Li²⁺, etc.

Common mistake: Sign error — emission gives negative ΔE (energy released); confusing R_H in J vs R_∞ in m⁻¹

r_n = 0.529 · n²/Z Å, v_n = 2.18 × 10⁶ · Z/n m/s

Symbols: r_n = radius of nth orbit, n = principal quantum number, Z = atomic number, v_n = orbital velocity

Unit: Angstrom (Å) for radius, m/s for velocity

When to use: Bohr model calculations for hydrogen-like atoms; finding orbit radii and electron speeds

Conditions: Only valid for hydrogen and hydrogen-like (single electron) species under Bohr model

Common mistake: Applying Bohr model to multi-electron atoms; confusing r ∝ n²/Z with r ∝ n/Z

L = √[l(l+1)] · ℏ, L_z = m_l · ℏ

Symbols: L = magnitude of orbital angular momentum, l = azimuthal quantum number, ℏ = h/2π, L_z = z-component, m_l = magnetic quantum number

Unit: J·s (same as h)

When to use: Quantum mechanical description of electron angular momentum in orbitals

Conditions: l = 0, 1, 2, ..., n-1; m_l = -l, -(l-1), ..., 0, ..., +(l-1), +l

Common mistake: Confusing L = lℏ (wrong) with L = √[l(l+1)]ℏ; forgetting that s-orbital (l=0) has zero orbital angular momentum

μ_s = √[n(n+2)] BM

Symbols: μ_s = spin-only magnetic moment, n = number of unpaired electrons, BM = Bohr Magneton (9.274 × 10⁻²⁴ J/T)

Unit: Bohr Magneton (BM)

When to use: Calculating magnetic moment from unpaired electrons in transition metal complexes and atoms

Conditions: Spin-only formula neglects orbital contribution; good approximation for 3d series

Common mistake: Taking n wrong by miscounting d-electrons; applying to 4d/5d where orbital contribution is significant

Bond Order = (N_b - N_a)/2

Symbols: N_b = number of bonding electrons, N_a = number of antibonding electrons (in MO theory)

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Determining bond strength and stability from Molecular Orbital diagrams; predicting paramagnetism

Conditions: Bond order > 0 implies stable molecule; fractional bond orders possible (e.g., O₂⁺ has BO = 2.5)

Common mistake: Counting electrons wrong in σ/σ/π/π orbitals; forgetting that σ and π are antibonding

VSEPR steric number = σ_bonds + lone_pairs

Symbols: Steric number determines hybridization and geometry; σ_bonds = number of atoms bonded, lone_pairs = non-bonding pairs on central atom

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Predicting molecular shape, bond angles, and hybridization of central atom

Conditions: SN = 2 → sp (linear), SN = 3 → sp² (trigonal planar), SN = 4 → sp³ (tetrahedral), SN = 5 → sp³d, SN = 6 → sp³d²

Common mistake: Forgetting to count double/triple bonds as one σ bond; not accounting for lone pairs in shaping (lone pair-bond pair repulsion > bond pair-bond pair)

Fajan's Rules: Covalent character increases with → small cation, large anion, high charge on either ion, pseudo-noble gas config cation

Symbols: Qualitative rules; no single formula

Unit: N/A

When to use: Predicting degree of covalent character in ionic compounds; explaining anomalies in ionic behavior

Conditions: Polarizing power ∝ charge/radius of cation; polarizability ∝ size of anion

Common mistake: Confusing which ion causes polarization (cation polarizes, anion gets polarized); forgetting that Cu⁺ shows more covalent character than Na⁺ despite same charge due to pseudo-config

ΔH_rxn = ΣΔH_f(products) - ΣΔH_f(reactants) (Hess's Law)

Symbols: ΔH_rxn = enthalpy of reaction, ΔH_f = standard enthalpy of formation

Unit: kJ/mol

When to use: Calculating reaction enthalpy from formation enthalpies or from intermediate reaction steps

Conditions: Enthalpy is a state function; path independent

Common mistake: Sign errors when reversing reactions (reverse ΔH = -ΔH); forgetting to multiply ΔH by stoichiometric coefficient when scaling reactions

q = mcΔT, q = nCΔT

Symbols: q = heat absorbed/released, m = mass, c = specific heat capacity, n = moles, C = molar heat capacity, ΔT = temperature change

Unit: Joules (J)

When to use: Calorimetry calculations; finding heat involved in temperature changes

Conditions: C_p and C_v differ for gases; for solids/liquids C_p ≈ C_v

Common mistake: Confusing C_p and C_v for gases; sign convention — q > 0 means system absorbs heat

ΔU = q + w (First Law)

Symbols: ΔU = change in internal energy, q = heat added to system, w = work done ON the system

Unit: Joules (J)

When to use: Energy conservation in thermodynamic processes; relating heat and work to internal energy

Conditions: IUPAC sign convention: q > 0 (absorbed), w > 0 (work done on system). In older convention w = work done BY system, so ΔU = q - w

Common mistake: Mixing sign conventions; for expansion work w = -P_extΔV (IUPAC), remembering that free expansion has w = 0

w = -P_extΔV (isobaric), w = -nRT ln(V₂/V₁) (isothermal reversible ideal gas)

Symbols: w = work done BY system (older convention) or ON system (IUPAC: w = +P_extΔV), P_ext = external pressure, ΔV = volume change, n = moles, R = gas constant, T = temperature

Unit: Joules (J)

When to use: Calculating PV work in different thermodynamic processes

Conditions: Isothermal reversible: T constant, ideal gas. For irreversible: w = -P_ext(V₂ - V₁)

Common mistake: Using reversible formula for irreversible process and vice versa; forgetting that free expansion (P_ext = 0) gives w = 0

ΔH = ΔU + Δn_g · RT

Symbols: Δn_g = change in moles of gaseous species (moles gas products - moles gas reactants), R = 8.314 J/mol·K

Unit: Joules (J) or kJ

When to use: Converting between ΔU and ΔH for reactions involving gases; crucial for combustion and reaction enthalpy problems

Conditions: Assumes ideal gas behaviour; Δn_g counts only gaseous species

Common mistake: Including liquid/solid moles in Δn_g; using wrong value of R (should be 8.314 J/mol·K when ΔU in J)

ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings ≥ 0 (Second Law)

Symbols: ΔS = entropy, subscripts system/surroundings/universe

Unit: J/K

When to use: Determining spontaneity of a process; ΔS_universe > 0 means spontaneous

Conditions: At equilibrium ΔS_universe = 0; for isolated system ΔS_system ≥ 0

Common mistake: Using only ΔS_system to judge spontaneity (must consider surroundings too); confusing ΔS with ΔH

ΔS_surroundings = -ΔH_system/T

Symbols: ΔH_system = enthalpy change of system at constant pressure, T = temperature in Kelvin

Unit: J/K

When to use: Finding entropy change of surroundings from enthalpy change of system

Conditions: Constant pressure and temperature; reversible heat transfer to surroundings

Common mistake: Forgetting the negative sign; using °C instead of K for temperature

ΔG = ΔH - TΔS (Gibbs free energy)

Symbols: ΔG = Gibbs free energy change, ΔH = enthalpy change, T = absolute temperature, ΔS = entropy change

Unit: Joules (J) or kJ

When to use: Predicting spontaneity at constant T and P; ΔG < 0 spontaneous, ΔG = 0 equilibrium, ΔG > 0 non-spontaneous

Conditions: Constant temperature and pressure; standard conditions denoted by ΔG°

Common mistake: Forgetting that ΔG depends on T — a reaction can be spontaneous at one T but not another; confusing ΔG with ΔG°

ΔG° = -RT ln K ↔ ΔG° = -2.303 RT log K

Symbols: ΔG° = standard Gibbs free energy, R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T = temperature in K, K = equilibrium constant

Unit: J/mol or kJ/mol

When to use: Relating thermodynamic stability to equilibrium constant; calculating K from ΔG° or vice versa

Conditions: K is K_c for concentrations or K_p for pressures; must use consistent standard states

Common mistake: Using R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K with ΔG in Joules (must use 8.314); not converting ΔG° to J before using with R

dG = VdP - SdT (at constant T: ΔG = VΔP for condensed phases; for ideal gas: ΔG = nRT ln(P₂/P₁))

Symbols: G = Gibbs free energy, V = volume, P = pressure, S = entropy, T = temperature

Unit: Joules (J)

When to use: Pressure dependence of Gibbs free energy; chemical potential variation with pressure

Conditions: For ideal gas isothermal process; ΔG = nRT ln(P₂/P₁)

Common mistake: Applying gas formula to solids/liquids where ΔG ≈ 0 for moderate pressure changes

K_p = K_c(RT)^Δn (gas phase equilibria)

Symbols: K_p = equilibrium constant in terms of partial pressures, K_c = in terms of concentrations, Δn = moles gaseous products - moles gaseous reactants, R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K

Unit: K_p and K_c dimensionless (activity-based)

When to use: Interconverting K_p and K_c for gas-phase equilibrium reactions

Conditions: Δn = change in moles of gas only; R must match units of pressure

Common mistake: Including solid/liquid species in Δn; using R = 8.314 instead of 0.0821 when K_p uses atm

Kp = Kc(RT)^Δn

Symbols: Same as above; alternative notation

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Same as above

Conditions: Same as above

Common mistake: Same as above

Q = [C]^c[D]^d/([A]^a[B]^b) (reaction quotient)

Symbols: Q = reaction quotient, concentrations or partial pressures at any time (not necessarily equilibrium)

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Predicting direction of reaction: if Q < K forward, Q > K reverse, Q = K equilibrium

Conditions: Pure solids and liquids have activity = 1 (omitted from Q and K expressions)

Common mistake: Including pure solids/liquids in Q; confusing Q with K (Q uses current values, K uses equilibrium values)

pH = -log[H⁺], pOH = -log[OH⁻], pH + pOH = pK_w = 14 (at 25°C)

Symbols: [H⁺] = hydrogen ion concentration in mol/L, [OH⁻] = hydroxide ion concentration, K_w = ion product of water

Unit: pH and pOH are dimensionless

When to use: Acid-base calculations; finding pH of solutions, strong/weak acids and bases

Conditions: pH + pOH = 14 only at 25°C; K_w = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C, increases with temperature

Common mistake: Using pH + pOH = 14 at temperatures other than 25°C; forgetting that for very dilute strong acids (<10⁻⁶ M), autoionization of water contributes significantly

Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] ≈ x²/(C - x) (weak acid), Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B] (weak base)

Symbols: Ka = acid dissociation constant, Kb = base dissociation constant, C = initial concentration, x = [H⁺] or [OH⁻] at equilibrium

Unit: mol/L (dimensionless in thermodynamic sense)

When to use: Finding pH of weak acid/base solutions; calculating degree of dissociation

Conditions: For weak acid: if Ka << C, then [H⁺] ≈ √(Ka·C); more precisely solve quadratic

Common mistake: Using [H⁺] = √(Ka·C) when Ka is not sufficiently small compared to C (need C/Ka > 100 for approximation); forgetting that pH of weak acid is always > pH of strong acid at same concentration

pH = ½(pKa - log C) (weak acid approx.), pH = 14 - ½(pKb - log C) (weak base approx.)

Symbols: pKa = -log Ka, pKb = -log Kb, C = initial concentration of weak acid/base

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Quick calculation of pH for weak acids and bases using the approximation [H⁺] ≈ √(Ka·C)

Conditions: Valid when degree of dissociation α << 1 (i.e., C/Ka > 100)

Common mistake: Applying to relatively strong 'weak' acids where approximation fails; mixing up acid and base formulas

pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]) (Henderson-Hasselbalch for acidic buffer)

Symbols: pKa = -log Ka of weak acid, [salt] = concentration of conjugate base, [acid] = concentration of weak acid

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Calculating pH of buffer solutions; designing buffers with desired pH

Conditions: Valid when [salt] and [acid] are both >> [H⁺] and >> [OH⁻]; works best when ratio is near 1

Common mistake: Using concentrations instead of moles when volumes differ; forgetting that at half-equivalence point pH = pKa

pH = 7 + ½(pKa + log C) (salt of weak acid + strong base), pH = 7 - ½(pKb + log C) (salt of strong acid + weak base)

Symbols: pKa = -log Ka, pKb = -log Kb, C = concentration of salt

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: pH of salt solutions undergoing hydrolysis

Conditions: For WA + SB salt: anion hydrolyzes, solution basic. For SA + WB salt: cation hydrolyzes, solution acidic

Common mistake: Using wrong formula for the type of salt; forgetting that neutral salt (SA + SB) has pH = 7

K_h = Kw/Ka (for WA+SB salt), K_h = Kw/Kb (for SA+WB salt), h = √(K_h/C) (degree of hydrolysis)

Symbols: K_h = hydrolysis constant, K_w = ion product of water, Ka/Kb = dissociation constant, C = salt concentration, h = degree of hydrolysis

Unit: dimensionless (h and K_h)

When to use: Calculating extent of salt hydrolysis and resulting pH

Conditions: h << 1 approximation used; valid for dilute solutions of weak acid/base salts

Common mistake: Using K_h = Ka/Kw (wrong); confusing hydrolysis constant with dissociation constant

[H⁺] = √(Ka₁·Ka₂) (for amphoteric salt like NaHA)

Symbols: Ka₁ = first dissociation constant of diprotic acid H₂A, Ka₂ = second dissociation constant

Unit: mol/L

When to use: Finding pH of amphotericsalt solutions (e.g., NaHCO₃, NaH₂PO₄)

Conditions: Valid when Ka₁ and Ka₂ are sufficiently different and concentration is not extremely dilute

Common mistake: Applying to salts that are not truly amphoteric; forgetting that this assumes both Ka values are small enough

Ksp = [A⁺]ᵃ[B⁻]ᵇ (for AₐB_b type salt, solubility s: Ksp = aᵃ · bᵇ · s⁽ᵃ⁺ᵇ⁾)

Symbols: Ksp = solubility product, s = molar solubility, a and b = stoichiometric coefficients

Unit: (mol/L)⁽ᵃ⁺ᵇ⁾

When to use: Calculating solubility from Ksp, predicting precipitation, common ion effect

Conditions: At equilibrium between solid and saturated solution; temperature dependent

Common mistake: Forgetting stoichiometric coefficients when writing Ksp (e.g., for Ag₂CrO₄, Ksp = 4s³ not s²); comparing Ksp directly only for same-type salts

IP = [A⁺]ᵃ[B⁻]ᵇ (ionic product); Precipitation if IP > Ksp

Symbols: IP = ionic product (current product of ion concentrations), Ksp = solubility product

Unit: Same as Ksp

When to use: Predicting whether a precipitate will form when two solutions are mixed

Conditions: Must account for dilution when mixing solutions; use concentrations AFTER mixing

Common mistake: Using initial concentrations before mixing instead of after dilution; confusing IP with Ksp

s' = s · Ksp/(Ksp + [Common Ion]) (approximate); More precisely solve: Ksp = (s + C)(s) or similar

Symbols: s' = solubility in presence of common ion, s = solubility in pure water, C = concentration of common ion

Unit: mol/L

When to use: Common ion effect — calculating reduced solubility in presence of shared ion

Conditions: If C >> s, then s' ≈ Ksp/C

Common mistake: Adding common ion concentration wrong (forgetting it comes from BOTH sources); applying to salts where common ion causes complex formation

E_cell = E°_cell - (RT/nF) ln Q (Nernst Equation)

Symbols: E_cell = cell potential, E°_cell = standard cell potential, R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T = temperature, n = number of electrons transferred, F = Faraday constant (96500 C/mol), Q = reaction quotient

Unit: Volts (V)

When to use: Finding cell potential at non-standard conditions; concentration cells; relating E° to K

Conditions: At 298 K: E_cell = E°_cell - (0.0591/n) log Q

Common mistake: Using wrong n (must balance the full redox equation); sign error in Q (products/reactants for the cell reaction as written); E°_cell = E°_cathode - E°_anode (reduction potentials)

E°_cell = (RT/nF) ln K ↔ log K = nE°_cell/0.0591 (at 298K)

Symbols: K = equilibrium constant for the cell reaction, n = electrons transferred, E°_cell = standard cell potential

Unit: K is dimensionless

When to use: Finding equilibrium constant from standard electrode potentials; predicting completeness of redox reactions

Conditions: At equilibrium E_cell = 0

Common mistake: Forgetting that large positive E°_cell means large K (reaction goes to completion); sign errors

Λ_m = κ/C (molar conductivity), Λ°_m = λ⁺_° + λ⁻_° (Kohlrausch's law)

Symbols: Λ_m = molar conductivity (S·m²/mol or S·cm²/mol), κ = conductivity (S/m or S/cm), C = molar concentration, λ⁺_°, λ⁻_° = limiting ionic conductivities

Unit: S·m²/mol or S·cm²/mol

When to use: Determining degree of dissociation of weak electrolytes; finding Λ°_m for weak electrolytes

Conditions: Λ°_m is molar conductivity at infinite dilution; Λ_m decreases with increasing concentration for weak electrolytes

Common mistake: Using wrong units for C (must be in mol/m³ for S·m²/mol, or mol/cm³ for S·cm²/mol); Λ_m = κ×1000/C when C in mol/L and Λ_m in S·cm²/mol

α = Λ_m/Λ°_m (degree of dissociation from conductivity)

Symbols: α = degree of dissociation, Λ_m = molar conductivity at concentration C, Λ°_m = molar conductivity at infinite dilution

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Finding degree of dissociation of weak electrolytes; then Ka = Cα²/(1-α)

Conditions: Valid for weak electrolytes where α is small; for strong electrolytes, interionic effects dominate

Common mistake: Applying to strong electrolytes without correction; for weak electrolytes with significant α, use Ka = Cα²/(1-α) not just Cα²

Q = It (charge), n = Q/F (moles of electrons)

Symbols: Q = charge in Coulombs, I = current in Amperes, t = time in seconds, F = Faraday constant = 96500 C/mol

Unit: Coulombs (C)

When to use: First law of electrolysis; relating charge passed to amount of substance deposited/dissolved

Conditions: 100% current efficiency assumed unless stated otherwise

Common mistake: Not converting time to seconds; forgetting valency factor (n in Faraday's law is electrons per mole of reaction)

W = nM·(I·t)/zF (mass deposited), where z = valency

Symbols: W = mass deposited, M = molar mass, I = current, t = time, z = number of electrons per ion (valency), F = 96500 C/mol

Unit: grams (g)

When to use: Calculating mass of substance deposited at electrode during electrolysis

Conditions: Assumes 100% current efficiency

Common mistake: Using wrong valency (z); e.g., for Al³⁺, z = 3 not 1

t⁺ + t⁻ = 1, t⁺ = λ⁺_°/(λ⁺_° + λ⁻_°), t⁻ = λ⁻_°/(λ⁺_° + λ⁻_°)

Symbols: t⁺, t⁻ = transport numbers of cation and anion, λ⁺_°, λ⁻_° = limiting ionic conductivities

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Finding how much current is carried by each ion; useful in moving boundary experiments

Conditions: At infinite dilution; transport numbers can vary with concentration

Common mistake: Confusing transport number with degree of dissociation

k = A·e^(-Ea/RT) (Arrhenius equation)

Symbols: k = rate constant, A = pre-exponential factor (frequency factor), Ea = activation energy, R = 8.314 J/mol·K, T = temperature in K

Unit: Depends on reaction order

When to use: Temperature dependence of reaction rates; finding Ea from rate constants at two temperatures

Conditions: A and Ea assumed temperature-independent over moderate ranges

Common mistake: Using Ea in kJ with R in J (must match units); ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ - 1/T₂) — note the order of T terms

ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)·(1/T₁ - 1/T₂)

Symbols: k₁, k₂ = rate constants at temperatures T₁ and T₂ respectively

Unit: dimensionless ratio

When to use: Finding activation energy from rate constants at two different temperatures

Conditions: Ea assumed constant over temperature range

Common mistake: Reversing T₁ and T₂ leading to sign error; using °C instead of K

t_½ = 0.693/k (first order), t_½ = 1/(k·a) (second order, a = initial conc.), t_½ = a/(2k) (zero order)

Symbols: t_½ = half-life, k = rate constant, a = initial concentration

Unit: time (s, min, hr depending on k units)

When to use: Half-life calculations for different reaction orders

Conditions: First order: t_½ is independent of initial concentration. Second order: inversely proportional to a. Zero order: directly proportional to a

Common mistake: Using first-order half-life formula for other orders; forgetting that t_½ for first order = ln2/k always

N = N₀·e^(-kt) (first order integrated), 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt (second order), [A] = [A]₀ - kt (zero order)

Symbols: N or [A] = concentration/amount at time t, N₀ or [A]₀ = initial concentration/amount, k = rate constant

Unit: Varies by order

When to use: Finding remaining amount/concentration at any time t

Conditions: Each equation is specific to its reaction order

Common mistake: Using wrong integrated rate law for the given order; not checking order before applying

n half-lives: fraction remaining = (1/2)ⁿ, fraction completed = 1 - (1/2)ⁿ

Symbols: n = number of half-lives elapsed = t/t_½

Unit: dimensionless fraction

When to use: Quick calculation for first-order reactions after integer number of half-lives

Conditions: Only for first-order reactions (since t_½ is constant)

Common mistake: Applying to non-first-order reactions where half-life changes with concentration

k = (2.303/t)·log([A]₀/[A]) (first order, log form)

Symbols: Same as above but using log₁₀ instead of ln

Unit: time⁻¹

When to use: First-order rate constant from experimental concentration-time data using common logarithm

Conditions: Equivalent to N = N₀e^(-kt)

Common mistake: Using ln when the formula has 2.303 (or vice versa); 2.303 = ln 10

ΔT_b = i·K_b·m (ebullioscopy), ΔT_f = i·K_f·m (cryoscopy)

Symbols: ΔT_b = boiling point elevation, ΔT_f = freezing point depression, i = Van't Hoff factor, K_b = ebullioscopic constant, K_f = cryoscopic constant, m = molality

Unit: Kelvin (K) for temperature change, mol/kg for molality

When to use: Colligative property calculations; finding molar mass of solute

Conditions: i = 1 for non-electrolytes; i > 1 for dissociation, i < 1 for association; m = moles solute/kg solvent

Common mistake: Forgetting the Van't Hoff factor for electrolytes; confusing molality (m) with molarity (M)

π = i·M·R·T (van't Hoff equation for osmotic pressure)

Symbols: π = osmotic pressure, i = Van't Hoff factor, M = molarity (mol/L), R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K, T = temperature in K

Unit: atm (if R = 0.0821) or Pa (if R = 8.314)

When to use: Finding molar mass of polymers/biomolecules; most accurate colligative property for large molecules

Conditions: Ideal dilute solution; M in mol/L with R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K

Common mistake: Using molarity instead of molality (osmotic pressure uses M, others use m); forgetting i for electrolytes

Relative lowering of vapour pressure: (P⁰ - P_s)/P⁰ = i·n/(n + N) ≈ i·n/N (dilute)

Symbols: P⁰ = vapour pressure of pure solvent, P_s = vapour pressure of solution, n = moles solute, N = moles solvent

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Raoult's law for non-volatile solutes; finding molar mass

Conditions: Non-volatile, non-electrolyte solute for simple form; add i for electrolytes

Common mistake: Forgetting i for electrolytic solutes; using mass instead of moles for n and N

Raoult's Law: P_total = P_A⁰·χ_A + P_B⁰·χ_B (ideal solution)

Symbols: P_A⁰, P_B⁰ = vapour pressures of pure components, χ_A, χ_B = mole fractions in liquid phase

Unit: same as P⁰ (atm, Pa, mmHg)

When to use: Vapour pressure of ideal binary liquid solutions; distillation calculations

Conditions: Ideal solution: ΔH_mix = 0, ΔV_mix = 0; follows Raoult's law over entire composition range

Common mistake: Using mole fraction in vapour phase instead of liquid phase for P_i = P_i⁰·χ_i(liquid); for vapour phase: y_i = P_i/P_total

χ_i(vapour) = P_i/P_total = P_i⁰·χ_i(liquid)/P_total

Symbols: χ_i(vapour) or y_i = mole fraction in vapour phase, P_i = partial pressure of component i

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Finding vapour phase composition in distillation; vapour is richer in more volatile component

Conditions: Ideal solution behaviour

Common mistake: Confusing liquid and vapour phase compositions; the more volatile component has higher y than x

ΔH_mix = 0, ΔV_mix = 0 (ideal solution); ΔH_mix > 0 for positive deviation; ΔH_mix < 0 for negative deviation

Symbols: ΔH_mix = enthalpy of mixing, ΔV_mix = volume change on mixing

Unit: J/mol

When to use: Understanding non-ideal solutions and azeotropes

Conditions: Positive deviation: P_obs > P_ideal (weaker A-B interactions); negative deviation: P_obs < P_ideal (stronger A-B interactions)

Common mistake: Confusing which deviation gives which azeotrope type; positive deviation → minimum boiling azeotrope, negative → maximum boiling azeotrope

i = 1 + α(EX - 1) (Van't Hoff factor for dissociation), i = 1 + α(1/X - 1) (for association)

Symbols: i = Van't Hoff factor, α = degree of dissociation/association, X = number of particles formed per molecule (dissociation) or number of molecules associating (association)

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Correcting colligative properties for electrolytic dissociation or molecular association

Conditions: For dissociation: AB → A⁺ + B⁻ gives X = 2; For association: 2A → A₂ gives X = 1/2

Common mistake: Using wrong sign for association vs dissociation; for complete dissociation α = 1, i = X

ρ = Z·M/(N_A·a³) (crystal density)

Symbols: ρ = density, Z = number of atoms per unit cell, M = molar mass, N_A = Avogadro's number (6.022 × 10²³), a = edge length of cubic unit cell

Unit: kg/m³ or g/cm³

When to use: Finding density of crystal from unit cell parameters; finding atomic radius from density

Conditions: Z values: SC = 1, BCC = 2, FCC = 4, diamond = 8

Common mistake: Using wrong Z for the crystal structure; unit mismatch between M (g/mol) and a (pm vs cm)

r = (√3 - √2)·a/2 (for octahedral void), r = (√2 - 1)·a (for tetrahedral void)

Symbols: r = radius of void, a = edge length of unit cell

Unit: same as a

When to use: Finding size of interstitial voids in close-packed structures

Conditions: For FCC (cubic close packed): tetrahedral void radius ratio = 0.225, octahedral = 0.414

Common mistake: Memorizing wrong ratios; confusing tetrahedral and octahedral void sizes

For FCC: 4r = √2·a (atoms touch along face diagonal); For BCC: 4r = √3·a (atoms touch along body diagonal); For SC: 2r = a

Symbols: r = atomic radius, a = edge length

Unit: same units

When to use: Relating atomic radius to unit cell edge length in cubic crystals

Conditions: Assumes hard sphere model with atoms touching along specified direction

Common mistake: Mixing up which diagonal atoms touch along; FCC uses face diagonal, BCC uses body diagonal

Defect-related: Schottky defect → density decreases (equal cation+anion vacancies); Frenkel defect → density unchanged (cation displaced to interstitial)

Symbols: Qualitative; no numerical formula

Unit: N/A

When to use: Understanding point defects in crystals and their effect on density and properties

Conditions: Schottky: typical in NaCl, KCl (similar sized ions). Frenkel: typical in AgBr, ZnS (large size difference)

Common mistake: Thinking Frenkel defect changes density (it doesn't — just displaces ion); confusing which defect type occurs in which compound

θ = k·t^n (empirical adsorption isotherm, Freundlich), or θ = (K·P)/(1 + K·P) (Langmuir)

Symbols: θ = fraction of surface covered, k, K = constants, P = pressure (gas) or C = concentration (solution), n = exponent (0 < 1/n < 1)

Unit: dimensionless θ

When to use: Adsorption isotherms; describing gas adsorption on solid surface

Conditions: Langmuir: monolayer adsorption, identical sites, no interaction between adsorbed molecules. Freundlich: empirical, works at intermediate pressures

Common mistake: Applying Langmuir at very high pressure where multilayer forms; confusing the high-pressure limit (θ → 1) with the low-pressure limit (θ ∝ P)

At high P: θ ≈ 1 (saturation); at low P: θ ≈ K·P (linear); at intermediate: Langmuir equation

Symbols: Same as above

Unit: dimensionless

When to use: Limits of Langmuir adsorption isotherm; understanding behaviour at different pressure ranges

Conditions: Derived from Langmuir equation by taking limits

Common mistake: Forgetting that all three regions follow Langmuir; just memorizing the middle region formula

μ = q·d (electric dipole moment), % ionic character = (μ_obs/μ_calc)×100

Symbols: μ = dipole moment, q = charge, d = distance between charges, μ_obs = observed dipole moment, μ_calc = calculated for 100% ionic (q = e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C)

Unit: Debye (D); 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m

When to use: Finding percent ionic character of a bond from dipole moment measurements

Conditions: Assumes bond length is known; ideal ionic would have full charge separation

Common mistake: Using wrong conversion between D and C·m; confusing dipole moment with polarizability
