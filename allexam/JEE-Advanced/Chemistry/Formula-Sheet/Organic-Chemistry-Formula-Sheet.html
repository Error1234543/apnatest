DoU = C + 1 - H/2 - X/2 + N/2

Symbols: C = number of carbon atoms, H = number of hydrogen atoms, X = number of halogen atoms, N = number of nitrogen atoms. Oxygen and sulfur are ignored.

Unit: Dimensionless (number of rings + pi bonds)

When to use: Determines the degree of unsaturation to find total rings and multiple bonds in a molecular formula.

Conditions: Valid for neutral molecules. For ions, add one H for each negative charge or subtract one H for each positive charge before applying.

Common mistake: Forgetting to adjust H for ionic charge; treating oxygen as contributing to unsaturation.

Index of Hydrogen Deficiency (IHD) = DoU

Symbols: Same as above; IHD and DoU are interchangeable terms.

Unit: Dimensionless

When to use: Alternate name; used interchangeably in problems asking for rings plus double bonds.

Conditions: Same as DoU formula above.

Common mistake: Confusing IHD with oxidation state calculations.

Graham's Law of Diffusion/Effusion (gas-phase organic vapors): r₁/r₂ = √(M₂/M₁)

Symbols: r = rate of diffusion/effusion; M = molar mass.

Unit: Rate in mol/s or volume/time; M in g/mol.

When to use: Comparing rates of vapor diffusion of two organic compounds.

Conditions: Applies to gaseous/vapor state at same temperature and pressure.

Common mistake: Inverting the ratio of molar masses.

Markovnikov's Rule: In addition of HX to an asymmetric alkene, H adds to the carbon with more H atoms, X adds to the carbon with fewer H atoms.

Symbols: HX = hydrogen halide (HCl, HBr, HI); alkene = C=C double bond.

Unit: Qualitative rule; no numerical unit.

When to use: Predicts the major product of electrophilic addition of HX to unsymmetrical alkenes via the more stable carbocation intermediate.

Conditions: Follows carbocation stability: 3° > 2° > 1°. Rearrangements can occur if a more stable carbocation is accessible.

Common mistake: Forgetting carbocation rearrangement (hydride/methyl shift) can change the predicted major product.

Anti-Markovnikov Addition (Peroxide Effect/Kharasch): R-CH=CH₂ + HBr + ROOR → R-CH₂-CH₂-Br

Symbols: ROOR = peroxide initiator (e.g., benzoyl peroxide); HBr only shows peroxide effect; HCl and HI do not.

Unit: Qualitative regioselectivity rule.

When to use: Gives the opposite regiochemistry to Markovnikov addition; proceeds via free-radical mechanism.

Conditions: Only operative with HBr, not HCl or HI. Peroxide must be present. Alkene must be terminal or unsymmetrical.

Common mistake: Applying peroxide effect to HCl or HI; forgetting it does not work with symmetrical alkenes.

Hammond's Postulate (qualitative): The structure of a transition state resembles the species (reactant or product) closest to it in energy.

Symbols: Used to rationalize kinetic vs thermodynamic control in reactions.

Unit: Conceptual; no numerical form.

When to use: Explains why exothermic reactions have early (reactant-like) TS and endothermic reactions have late (product-like) TS, affecting selectivity.

Conditions: Applies to elementary steps; especially useful in EAS, carbocation reactions, and radical reactions.

Common mistake: Confusing kinetic control (lower activation energy) with thermodynamic control (more stable product).

Carbocation Stability Order: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃⁺; Stabilized by +I, +R groups; destabilized by +I withdrawers and adjacent electron-withdrawing groups.

Symbols: +I = inductive electron donation (alkyl groups); +R = resonance donation (lone pair or π bonds).

Unit: Qualitative stability order.

When to use: Predicts regioselectivity in addition, elimination, and substitution reactions; explains rearrangement propensity.

Conditions: Resonance-stabilized carbocations (allylic, benzylic) can exceed simple 3° in stability.

Common mistake: Ranking primary benzylic above tertiary alkyl without considering the specific context; forgetting hyperconjugation contributes to 3° > 2° > 1°.

Carbanion Stability Order: CH₃⁻ < 1° < 2° < 3° (opposite of carbocation); Stabilized by -I and -R groups (e.g., NO₂, CN, C=O adjacent).

Symbols: -I = inductive withdrawal; -R = resonance withdrawal.

Unit: Qualitative stability order.

When to use: Predicts acidity of C-H bonds and reactivity in aldol, Claisen, and Michael reactions.

Conditions: sp-hybridized carbanions (alkynides) are more stable than sp² which are more stable than sp³ due to s-character.

Common mistake: Applying carbocation stability logic to carbanions; confusing acidity with carbanion stability.

Free Radical Stability Order: 3° > 2° > 1° > CH₃· ; Allylic and benzylic radicals are exceptionally stable due to resonance.

Symbols: Radical centered on carbon; stabilized by hyperconjugation and resonance.

Unit: Qualitative order.

When to use: Predicts site of halogenation in radical reactions (e.g., NBS bromination, high-temp chlorination).

Conditions: Selectivity follows stability; bromination is more selective than chlorination due to higher activation energy.

Common mistake: Forgetting that NBS provides low, constant concentration of Br₂ for allylic/benzylic bromination.

SN2 Rate = k[Substrate][Nucleophile]; SN1 Rate = k[Substrate]

Symbols: k = rate constant; Substrate = alkyl halide or similar; Nucleophile = attacking species.

Unit: SN2: second order overall; SN1: first order overall.

When to use: Distinguishes kinetics of bimolecular vs unimolecular substitution; predicts rate dependence on nucleophile concentration.

Conditions: SN2 favored by strong nucleophiles, polar aprotic solvents, primary substrates. SN1 favored by weak nucleophiles, polar protic solvents, tertiary substrates.

Common mistake: Assuming SN1 always gives racemization — partial inversion occurs; confusing solvent effects.

SN2 Stereochemistry: Walden inversion (100% inversion of configuration at the reacting center).

Symbols: Inversion means R→S or S→R if the center is the only stereocenter.

Unit: Qualitative stereochemical outcome.

When to use: Predicts the stereochemical result of bimolecular nucleophilic substitution.

Conditions: Requires backside attack; blocked by steric hindrance (tertiary substrates do not undergo SN2).

Common mistake: Applying inversion to SN1 (which gives racemization, not clean inversion).

E2 Rate = k[Substrate][Base]; E2 requires anti-periplanar geometry between H and leaving group.

Symbols: Base = strong base (e.g., OH⁻, OR⁻, NH₂⁻); anti-periplanar = dihedral angle ≈ 180°.

Unit: Second-order kinetics.

When to use: Predicts regioselectivity (Zaitsev vs Hofmann) and stereochemical requirements of elimination.

Conditions: Zaitsev product (more substituted alkene) favored with small bases; Hofmann product (less substituted) favored with bulky bases (t-BuOK). Requires anti-periplanar H and LG.

Common mistake: Forcing Zaitsev when a bulky base is used; ignoring that cyclic systems require trans-diaxial arrangement for E2.

E1 Rate = k[Substrate]; E1 competes with SN1; involves carbocation intermediate followed by loss of a proton.

Symbols: Same substrate notation as above.

Unit: First-order kinetics.

When to use: Predicts elimination product under conditions favoring carbocation formation (weak base, protic solvent, heat favors E1 over SN1).

Conditions: Favored by heat; follows Zaitsev rule; carbocation rearrangements possible.

Common mistake: Not recognizing E1/SN1 competition; forgetting heat favors elimination over substitution.

Zaitsev's Rule: In elimination, the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product.

Symbols: More substituted = alkene with more alkyl groups attached to C=C carbons.

Unit: Qualitative regioselectivity rule.

When to use: Predicts major alkene product in E1 and E2 (with small bases) eliminations.

Conditions: Overridden by bulky bases (Hofmann rule) or when anti-periplanar geometry restricts available β-hydrogens.

Common mistake: Applying Zaitsev universally without checking base size or stereochemical constraints.

Hofmann's Rule: With bulky bases, the less substituted alkene is the major product.

Symbols: Bulky bases: t-BuOK, LDA; less substituted = fewer alkyl groups on C=C.

Unit: Qualitative regioselectivity rule.

When to use: Predicts major alkene when steric hindrance directs base to abstract the more accessible (less hindered) β-hydrogen.

Conditions: Operates in E2 with sterically hindered bases; also in thermal decomposition of amine oxides (Hofmann elimination).

Common mistake: Confusing Hofmann elimination (amine oxide pyrolysis) with Hofmann rule in E2.

Bond Dissociation Energy (BDE) for C-H: 3° < 2° < 1° < CH₃; Lower BDE means easier homolytic cleavage.

Symbols: BDE = energy required to break C-H bond homolytically to give radical + H·.

Unit: kJ/mol or kcal/mol.

When to use: Rationalizes radical halogenation selectivity; lower BDE at 3° C-H makes it preferentially abstracted.

Conditions: Allylic and benzylic C-H bonds have particularly low BDE due to radical resonance stabilization.

Common mistake: Confusing BDE trends with carbocation stability trends (same order but different reasoning).

Acidity Order (general): RC≡CH > PhOH > RCOOH > H₂O > R-OH > NH₃ > alkane; pKa ≈ -log[Ka], lower pKa = stronger acid.

Symbols: pKa = -log₁₀(Ka); smaller pKa means stronger acid.

Unit: pKa is dimensionless.

When to use: Predicts direction of acid-base equilibria; determines which protons are abstractable by a given base.

Conditions: Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity; resonance stabilization of conjugate base increases acidity.

Common mistake: Thinking phenol is more acidic than carboxylic acid; confusing pKa with pH.

Basicity Order (general): Alkylamines (gas phase): 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃; Aqueous phase: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃ (due to solvation effects). Arylamines << alkylamines.

Symbols: Basicity measured by pKb or conjugate acid pKa; higher pKa of BH⁺ = stronger base.

Unit: Dimensionless (pKb/pKa).

When to use: Predicts amine reactivity in nucleophilic substitution, acylation, and salt formation.

Conditions: Solvation stabilizes smaller ions better (1° > 2° > 3°), competing with inductive effect.

Common mistake: Using gas-phase order for aqueous solutions; forgetting aniline is much weaker due to resonance delocalization of lone pair.

Hammett Equation: log(k/k₀) = ρσ; log(K/K₀) = ρσ

Symbols: ρ = reaction constant (positive = electron-withdrawing accelerates; negative = electron-donating accelerates); σ = substituent constant (σ > 0 for EWG, σ < 0 for EDG).

Unit: Dimensionless.

When to use: Quantifies electronic effects of substituents on reaction rates and equilibria in aromatic systems.

Conditions: Applicable to meta- and para-substituted benzenes; ortho effects are complicated by steric factors.

Common mistake: Ignoring that ρ sign tells you whether the transition state is electron-rich or electron-poor.

Hückel's Rule: A planar, cyclic, fully conjugated system is aromatic if it has (4n + 2) π electrons (n = 0, 1, 2, ...). Anti-aromatic if it has 4n π electrons and is planar.

Symbols: n = integer ≥ 0; π electrons = electrons in the conjugated p-orbital system.

Unit: Count of π electrons.

When to use: Determines aromaticity of rings: benzene (6π), cyclopentadienyl anion (6π, aromatic), cyclopropenyl cation (2π, aromatic), cyclobutadiene (4π, anti-aromatic).

Conditions: Must be cyclic, planar, fully conjugated (every atom in ring has a p orbital). Non-planar systems (e.g., cyclooctatetraene) avoid anti-aromaticity by adopting non-planar conformations.

Common mistake: Counting π electrons incorrectly for ions; forgetting that heteroatoms contribute lone pairs only if needed for conjugation.

Aromaticity of Heterocycles: Pyridine (6π, aromatic, N lone pair orthogonal); Pyrrole (6π, aromatic, N lone pair in π system); Furan/Thiophene (6π, aromatic, one lone pair from O/S in π system).

Symbols: π electrons include those from heteroatom lone pairs participating in conjugation.

Unit: Qualitative classification.

When to use: Determines reactivity patterns: pyridine undergoes EAS slowly (ring is electron-poor); pyrrole/furan undergo EAS readily (ring is electron-rich).

Conditions: The lone pair on N in pyridine is in sp² orbital (not in π system); in pyrrole it is in a p orbital (participates in aromaticity).

Common mistake: Counting pyridine's N lone pair toward aromaticity; confusing basicity of pyridine N vs pyrrole N.

Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) Rate Order: Activators (-OH, -NH₂, -OR, -R) > H > Deactivators (-NO₂, -CN, -COR, -COOH, -halogens). Ortho/para directors: all activators + halogens. Meta directors: all deactivators except halogens.

Symbols: Activators increase ring electron density; deactivators decrease it. Halogens are unique: deactivating but o/p directing.

Unit: Qualitative directing/activating classification.

When to use: Predicts product distribution in nitration, sulfonation, halogenation, Friedel-Crafts acylation/alkylation, and other EAS reactions.

Conditions: Strong activators (-NH₂, -OH) can be protonated in strongly acidic media, converting them to meta-directing deactivators.

Common mistake: Forgetting Friedel-Crafts fails on strongly deactivated rings (e.g., nitrobenzene) and with -NH₂ groups (complexation with AlCl₃).

Inductive Effect Decay: The -I effect diminishes rapidly with distance; significant up to 3-4 bonds, negligible beyond ~5 bonds.

Symbols: -I strength: -NO₂ > -CN > -COOH > -F > -Cl > -Br > -I > -OH > -OR > -NH₂ > -CH₃ (-I weakest among common groups). +I: 3° alkyl > 2° > 1° > H.

Unit: Qualitative, distance-dependent.

When to use: Predicts acidity/basicity changes with substituent position; explains why remote substituents have diminishing effects.

Conditions: Operates through σ bonds; permanent effect independent of reaction conditions.

Common mistake: Assuming inductive effect operates through π systems (that is resonance, not induction).

Resonance (Mesomeric) Effect: +R/E (electron donation): -OH, -OR, -NH₂, -NHR, -SH, -X (lone pair donation). -R/E (electron withdrawal): -NO₂, -CN, -CHO, -COR, -COOH, -COOR, -SO₃H, -NR₃⁺.

Symbols: +R donates electron density into the π system; -R withdraws electron density from the π system.

Unit: Qualitative classification.

When to use: Predicts regioselectivity in EAS, stability of intermediates, and reactivity patterns in conjugated systems.

Conditions: Only operative when the substituent is conjugated with the π system (coplanar arrangement required). Stronger than inductive effect at appropriate distances.

Common mistake: Listing - halogens as +R only; they are -I dominant but +R directing in EAS.

Hyperconjugation: Number of α-hydrogens determines stability contribution. More α-H → greater hyperconjugative stabilization. Order: 3° carbocation/radical/alkene > 2° > 1°.

Symbols: α-hydrogen = hydrogen on a carbon adjacent to the electron-deficient center or double bond.

Unit: Qualitative stabilization energy (~2-3 kcal/mol per α-H).

When to use: Explains carbocation/radical stability, alkene stability (more substituted = more stable), and ortho effect in some cases.

Conditions: Requires proper orbital overlap (C-H σ bond parallel to adjacent p orbital or π system).

Common mistake: Counting all hydrogens instead of only α-hydrogens.

Booster Rule (for counting resonating structures): A resonating structure is a valid booster if it (1) has maximum covalent bonds, (2) places negative charge on the more electronegative atom, (3) places positive charge on the less electronegative atom, (4) does not separate opposite charges unnecessarily.

Symbols: Booster = significant contributor to resonance hybrid.

Unit: Qualitative assessment.

When to use: Determines major resonance contributors and predicts reactive sites in molecules.

Conditions: All valid resonance structures must have same atomic positions (only electrons move); same total charge.

Common mistake: Counting canonical forms with broken sigma bonds or changed atomic positions as resonance structures.

Snell's Law / Refraction (for optical isomers, specific rotation): [α]_D^T = α / (l × c), where α = observed rotation (degrees), l = path length (dm), c = concentration (g/mL), T = temperature (°C), D = sodium D-line (589 nm).

Symbols: [α] = specific rotation; α = observed rotation; l = tube length in decimeters; c = concentration in g/mL.

Unit: [α] in degrees·mL·dm⁻¹·g⁻¹ (commonly reported as just degrees).

When to use: Calculates specific rotation for chiral compounds; compares optical purity.

Conditions: Pure substance or solution; monochromatic light (Na D-line standard). Temperature and wavelength must be specified.

Common mistake: Using cm instead of dm for path length; confusing specific rotation with optical purity calculation.

Optical Purity = {[α]observed / [α]pure} × 100% = % excess of one enantiomer

Symbols: [α]observed = measured specific rotation of sample; [α]pure = specific rotation of pure enantiomer.

Unit: Percentage (%)

When to use: Determines enantiomeric excess (ee) from polarimetry data.

Conditions: Assumes no impurities affecting rotation; linear relationship between composition and observed rotation.

Common mistake: Confusing optical purity with diastereomeric excess.

Fischer Projection Rules: Horizontal bonds come OUT of plane (toward viewer); vertical bonds go INTO plane (away from viewer). Interchanging any two groups twice restores original; once inverts configuration.

Symbols: Standard convention for representing stereochemistry in 2D.

Unit: Qualitative representation.

When to use: Assigns R/S configuration; converts between Fischer, wedge-dash, and Newman projections.

Conditions: Carbon chain drawn vertically with most oxidized carbon at top.

Common mistake: Rotating Fischer projection by 90° in plane (changes stereochemistry); only 180° rotation preserves configuration.

R/S Configuration (Cahn-Ingold-Prelog): Assign priorities 1-4 to substituents on chiral center by atomic number. Orient molecule so #4 points away. Trace 1→2→3: clockwise = R, counter-clockwise = S. If #4 points toward viewer, reverse the result.

Symbols: Priority based on atomic number of directly attached atom; tie-breaking proceeds outward atom by atom.

Unit: Absolute configuration descriptor (R or S).

When to use: Unambiguously names stereoisomers; essential for naming chiral molecules in JEE Advanced.

Conditions: Isotopes: higher mass number gets higher priority (D > H). Double bonds count as duplicated atoms.

Common mistake: Forgetting to reverse when lowest priority is toward viewer; incorrect tie-breaking at double bonds.

Newman Projection: View along a C-C bond; front carbon shown as a point with three bonds at 120°; back carbon shown as a circle with three bonds. Conformations: staggered (anti, gauche) and eclipsed (higher energy).

Symbols: Anti = largest groups 180° apart; Gauche = largest groups 60° apart; Eclipse = groups aligned (0° dihedral).

Unit: Qualitative conformational descriptor.

When to use: Analyzes steric strain, predicts most stable conformation, calculates energy differences between conformers.

Conditions: Rotation around single bond; energy barriers ~3-5 kcal/mol for ethane, ~10-12 kcal/mol for butane (gauche interaction ~0.9 kcal/mol).

Common mistake: Drawing eclipsed as staggered; miscounting gauche interactions in larger molecules.

Conformational Energy (butane): Anti (most stable) < Gauche (< 0.9 kcal/mol higher) < Eclipsed (H/H ~1.0, CH₃/H ~1.4, CH₃/CH₃ ~2.5-2.8 kcal/mol higher than anti).

Symbols: Energy values approximate; depend on measurement conditions.

Unit: kcal/mol or kJ/mol.

When to use: Predicts relative stability of butane conformers; basis for analyzing substituted alkane conformations.

Conditions: Values are additive for multiple interactions in larger molecules (approximate).

Common mistake: Adding all interactions without considering which pairs actually interact in a given conformation.

Werner's Coordination Theory (for organometallic relevance): Primary valency = oxidation state (ionizable); Secondary valency = coordination number (non-ionizable, defines geometry).

Symbols: Common coordination numbers: 4 (tetrahedral or square planar); 6 (octahedral).

Unit: Dimensionless counts.

When to use: Determines geometry of coordination compounds; relevant for inorganic-organic interface topics.

Conditions: Geometry determined by coordination number and ligand field; d⁸ metals (Ni²⁺, Pd²⁺, Pt²⁺) often form square planar complexes.

Common mistake: Assuming all 4-coordinate complexes are tetrahedral; forgetting square planar for d⁸.

IUPAC Naming - Principal Group Priority (highest to lowest): -COOH > -SO₃H > -COOR > -COCl > -CONH₂ > -CN > -CHO > -C=O > -OH > -NH₂ > -C≡CH > -C=C- > -OR > -Halogen.

Symbols: Highest priority group becomes suffix; all others become prefixes.

Unit: Nomenclature ordering.

When to use: Determines parent name and numbering direction in IUPAC nomenclature.

Conditions: Numbering gives the principal group the lowest possible locant; tie broken by next substituent.

Common mistake: Forgetting that -COOH overrides all other groups including -CHO; misnumbering when multiple相同 priority groups exist.

IUPAC Name Construction: [Prefix(es)] + [Parent chain] + [Suffix for principal group]. Prefixes in alphabetical order; locants placed immediately before the part they modify.

Symbols: Example: 3-bromo-2-methylpentanoic acid (bromo before methyl alphabetically).

Unit: Systematic nomenclature.

When to use: Generates correct IUPAC names for exam questions requiring naming or structure derivation.

Conditions: Longest carbon chain containing the principal group is the parent; multiple chains of equal length: choose the one with maximum substituents.

Common mistake: Choosing wrong parent chain; forgetting alphabetical order for prefixes (di-, tri- ignored in alphabetizing).

Lucas Test: Rochelle's salt / ZnCl₂ + conc. HCl. Tertiary alcohols react immediately (cloudiness), secondary in 5 min, primary no reaction at room temperature.

Symbols: Test distinguishes 1°, 2°, 3° alcohols by rate of SN1 substitution to form alkyl chloride.

Unit: Qualitative test; time-based observation.

When to use: Distinguishes alcohol classes in qualitative analysis.

Conditions: Works for water-soluble low molecular weight alcohols; allylic/benzylic alcohols react rapidly.

Common mistake: Applying Lucas test to phenols (they do not react); confusing with Victor Meyer test.

Victor Meyer Test: Alcohol → RI → AgNO₂ (sn2) → nitroalkane → HNO₂ → color. Primary = red/blood color; Secondary = blue; Tertiary = no color (immiscible layer).

Symbols: Steps: (1) P + I₂/red P → RI; (2) RI + AgNO₂ → RNO₂; (3) RNO₂ + HNO₂ → nitrous acid test.

Unit: Qualitative color test.

When to use: Distinguishes 1°, 2°, 3° alcohols based on color of nitroso compound formed.

Conditions: Only for aliphatic alcohols; phenols and aryl halides do not follow this pathway.

Common mistake: Reversing the color assignments; confusing with Lucas test results.

Carius Method (halogen estimation): Mass of AgX formed = (Atomic mass of AgX / Molecular mass of compound) × (Number of halogen atoms) × Mass of compound. % Halogen = (Mass of AgX × 35.5 / 143.5) / Mass of compound × 100 (for Cl).

Symbols: AgX = silver halide precipitate; 35.5 = atomic mass of Cl; 143.5 = molecular mass of AgCl.

Unit: % by mass.

When to use: Calculates percentage of halogen in an organic compound from precipitate mass.

Conditions: Compound must be completely decomposed by fuming HNO₃ + AgNO₃ in Carius tube.

Common mistake: Forgetting to multiply by number of halogen atoms; using wrong molecular mass of AgX.

Dumas Method (Nitrogen estimation): Volume of N₂ collected (V) at temperature T and pressure P. Moles of N₂ = PV/RT. Mass of N = 28 × PV/RT. % N = (Mass of N / Mass of compound) × 100.

Symbols: P = atmospheric pressure minus water vapor pressure (if collected over water); V in liters; T in Kelvin; R = 0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹.

Unit: % by mass.

When to use: Calculates nitrogen content from volume of N₂ gas evolved.

Conditions: Organic compound heated with CuO; N₂ released is collected over KOH solution (CO₂ absorbed).

Common mistake: Forgetting to subtract vapor pressure of water when gas is collected over water.

Kjeldahl's Method (Nitrogen estimation): N% = (14 × V × M × 100) / (m × 1000) for titration with standard acid, where V = volume of acid (mL), M = molarity, m = mass of compound (g). Simplified: N% = (1.4 × N_factor × V_mL) / m_g.

Symbols: N_factor = normality of H₂SO₄ used; V = volume of acid neutralized by NH₃; m = mass of compound.

Unit: % by mass.

When to use: Determines nitrogen in organic compounds (except nitro, azo, and heterocyclic N which are not converted to NH₃).

Conditions: Compound is digested with conc. H₂SO₄ + catalyst; NH₃ distilled into standard acid; excess acid back-titrated.

Common mistake: Applying Kjeldahl to nitro/azo/heterocyclic compounds (N is not reduced to NH₄⁺); wrong normality factor usage.

Wurtz Reaction: 2R-X + 2Na + dry ether → R-R + 2NaX. Limited to symmetrical alkanes; mixed Wurtz gives mixture of 3 products.

Symbols: R = alkyl group; X = Cl, Br, I (reactivity: I > Br > Cl). Na = sodium metal; dry ether = solvent.

Unit: Reaction equation.

When to use: Prepares symmetrical alkanes with even number of carbons; couple of identical alkyl halides.

Conditions: Dry ether mandatory (moisture destroys Na); works best with primary halides; tertiary halides give elimination.

Common mistake: Using mixed halides expecting one product (gets 3 products: R-R, R'-R', R-R'); using tertiary halide.

Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm (surface chemistry interface): w/m = k × P^(1/n) where 0 < 1/n < 1. At high pressure: w/m = k (saturation); at low pressure: w/m ∝ P.

Symbols: w = mass adsorbed; m = mass of adsorbent; P = pressure; k, n = constants dependent on adsorbate-adsorbent pair.

Unit: k has units depending on pressure units used.

When to use: Describes physisorption of organic vapors on solids; relates to chromatography principles.

Conditions: Physical adsorption (van der Waals); reversible; multilayer possible at high pressure.

Common mistake: Applying to chemisorption (which has different behavior); forgetting the isotherm breaks down at extreme pressures.

Gibbs Free Energy and Equilibrium: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q; at equilibrium ΔG = 0, so ΔG° = -RT ln K.

Symbols: ΔG = Gibbs free energy change; ΔG° = standard Gibbs free energy; R = gas constant; T = temperature (K); Q = reaction quotient; K = equilibrium constant.

Unit: ΔG in J/mol or kJ/mol.

When to use: Predicts spontaneity and equilibrium position of organic reactions (e.g., esterification, tautomerism).

Conditions: Constant temperature and pressure; K must be dimensionless (activities).

Common mistake: Using ΔG° to predict rate (it predicts equilibrium, not kinetics); confusing Q with K.

Arrhenius Equation: k = A × e^(-Ea/RT); ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R) × (1/T₁ - 1/T₂)

Symbols: k = rate constant; A = frequency/pre-exponential factor; Ea = activation energy; R = 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹; T = temperature (K).

Unit: k depends on reaction order; Ea in J/mol or kJ/mol.

When to use: Calculates rate constant at different temperatures; determines activation energy from rate data.

Conditions: A and Ea assumed temperature-independent over modest ranges.

Common mistake: Using °C instead of K; forgetting to convert Ea to J/mol when R is in J.

van't Hoff Equation (temperature dependence of K): ln(K₂/K₁) = (ΔH°/R) × (1/T₁ - 1/T₂)

Symbols: ΔH° = standard enthalpy change; K₁, K₂ = equilibrium constants at T₁, T₂.

Unit: ΔH° in J/mol.

When to use: Predicts how equilibrium constant changes with temperature; exothermic reactions (ΔH < 0) have K decreasing with T.

Conditions: ΔH° assumed constant over temperature range considered.

Common mistake: Sign error in ΔH; applying to kinetic rate constants instead of equilibrium constants.

Le Chatelier's Principle for Organic Equilibria: Increase in pressure favors side with fewer moles of gas. Increase in temperature favors endothermic direction. Adding reactant shifts right; adding product shifts left.

Symbols: Applied to reactions like esterification (equimolar, pressure irrelevant), Haber process (not organic), etc.

Unit: Qualitative prediction.

When to use: Predicts shift in equilibrium position for reversible organic reactions (esterification, hydration, etc.).

Conditions: System must be at equilibrium; changes must be applied to closed system.

Common mistake: Applying pressure effect to reactions with equal moles on both sides (no effect); confusing with kinetic effects.

Osazone Formation: One mole of reducing sugar + 3 moles of phenylhydrazine → osazone + NH₃ + 2 H₂O. Glucose, fructose, and mannose give same osazone.

Symbols: Phenylhydrazine (C₆H₅NHNH₂) reacts with C1 and C2 of aldose/ketose.

Unit: Stoichiometric: 1 sugar : 3 PhNHNH₂.

When to use: Identifies reducing sugars; distinguishes sugars that form different osazone crystals (different shapes/timing).

Conditions: Only reducing sugars (free or potential aldehyde/ketone group) form osazones; non-reducing sugars (sucrose) do not.

Common mistake: Thinking all sugars form osazones equally; not knowing glucose/fructose/mannose share the same osazone.

Molar Mass from Colligative Properties ( ebullioscopic): M₂ = (K_b × w₂ × 1000) / (w₁ × ΔT_b); where K_b = ebullioscopic constant, w₂ = mass of solute, w₁ = mass of solvent, ΔT_b = boiling point elevation.

Symbols: K_b = ebullioscopic constant of solvent (for water: 0.512 K·kg/mol); ΔT_b = T_solution - T_solvent.

Unit: M₂ in g/mol.

When to use: Determines molecular mass of organic compounds (especially non-volatile, non-electrolyte) from boiling point elevation.

Conditions: Solute must be non-volatile, non-electrolyte (or account for van't Hoff factor i); dilute solution.

Common mistake: Forgetting van't Hoff factor for electrolytes; using wrong units for w₁ and w₂.

van't Hoff Factor (i): i = (Experimental colligative property) / (Normal colligative property) = (Normal M) / (Observed M) = 1 + (n-1)α for dissociation; i = 1 + (1/n-1)α for association.

Symbols: n = number of particles produced per formula unit; α = degree of dissociation/association.

Unit: Dimensionless.

When to use: Corrects colligative properties for electrolytes (acids, salts) and associating species (carboxylic acids in benzene dimerize, i = 0.5).

Conditions: Complete dissociation assumed for strong electrolytes; partial for weak.

Common mistake: Using i = 1 for all organic compounds (some associate/dissociate in solution); forgetting dimerization of carboxylic acids in non-polar solvents.

Raoult's Law for Volatile Solutions: P_total = P_A°×χ_A + P_B°×χ_B; P_i = Partial pressure of component i; χ_i = mole fraction in liquid; P_i° = vapor pressure of pure component.

Symbols: χ_A + χ_B = 1; P_total = sum of partial pressures.

Unit: Pressure (atm, mmHg, Pa).

When to use: Calculates vapor pressure of ideal binary mixtures (e.g., benzene-toluene); basis for distillation theory.

Conditions: Ideal solution: ΔH_mix = 0, ΔV_mix = 0; molecules A-A, B-B, A-B interactions similar.

Common mistake: Using mole fraction in vapor instead of liquid; assuming ideality for highly non-ideal mixtures.

Azeotrope Types: Minimum-boiling azeotrope (positive deviation, e.g., ethanol-water, bp 78.2°C for 95% EtOH); Maximum-boiling azeotrope (negative deviation, e.g., HNO₃-water). Cannot be separated by fractional distillation.

Symbols: Positive deviation: P_total > predicted (weaker A-B interactions). Negative deviation: P_total < predicted (stronger A-B interactions).

Unit: Qualitative classification.

When to use: Explains why certain mixtures cannot be purified by simple fractional distillation; relevant to solvent preparation.

Conditions: Azeotropic composition is fixed at given pressure; changes with pressure.

Common mistake: Thinking all mixtures can be separated by distillation; confusing azeotrope with ideal solution.

Nernst Distribution Law: C₁/C₂ = K_D (distribution coefficient) at constant temperature, where C₁ = concentration in solvent 1, C₂ = concentration in solvent 2. Both phases must be immiscible.

Symbols: K_D = constant at given T; applies when solute is same molecular form in both solvents.

Unit: Dimensionless ratio (if concentrations in same units).

When to use: Calculates extraction efficiency in liquid-liquid extraction (e.g., extracting organic product from aqueous layer).

Conditions: Solute must not associate/dissociate differently in the two solvents; same temperature.

Common mistake: Applying when solute associates in one solvent (e.g., acetic acid dimerizes in benzene); wrong number of extraction stages.

Extraction Efficiency: Fraction remaining after n extractions = [V_aq / (V_aq + K_D × V_org)]^n. Multiple small extractions are more efficient than one large extraction.

Symbols: V_aq = aqueous phase volume; V_org = organic phase volume per extraction; K_D = distribution coefficient.

Unit: Fraction (dimensionless).

When to use: Optimizes lab extraction procedure; determines number/volume of extracts needed.

Conditions: Each extraction reaches equilibrium; volumes remain constant.

Common mistake: Using one large volume instead of multiple small ones; reversing K_D in the formula.

Gibbs Phase Rule: F = C - P + 2, where F = degrees of freedom, C = number of components, P = number of phases.

Symbols: For condensed systems (neglecting vapor pressure): F = C - P + 1.

Unit: F = number of intensive variables (T, P, composition) that can be changed independently.

When to use: Analyzes phase diagrams for organic mixtures (binary systems like phenol-water, partially miscible liquids).

Conditions: System at equilibrium; no chemical reactions among components (or account for them).

Common mistake: Forgetting to subtract for chemical reactions reducing independent components; miscounting phases.

Sol-Gel Transition (critical micelle concentration, CMC): Above CMC, surfactant molecules aggregate into micelles. CMC decreases with increasing hydrocarbon chain length and with added electrolyte.

Symbols: CMC = concentration above which micelles form; depends on tail length, head group, temperature, salt.

Unit: mol/L or g/L.

When to use: Explains detergent action, micellar catalysis in organic reactions, and solubility enhancement of organic compounds.

Conditions: Only for amphiphilic molecules (surfactants); temperature affects CMC (Krafft temperature below which no micelles form).

Common mistake: Thinking all organic molecules form micelles; confusing CMC with solubility limit.

Michaelis-Menten Kinetics (enzyme-catalyzed organic reactions): v = (V_max × [S]) / (K_M + [S]), where K_M = (k₋₁ + k₂)/k₁.

Symbols: v = reaction rate; V_max = maximum rate at saturating substrate; [S] = substrate concentration; K_M = Michaelis constant.

Unit: v and V_max in mol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹; K_M in mol/L.

When to use: Describes enzyme-catalyzed organic transformations (relevant to biomolecules section); K_M inversely related to substrate affinity.

Conditions: Steady-state approximation; [S] >> [E]_total; initial rate conditions.

Common mistake: Confusing K_M with dissociation constant K_d (they are equal only if k₂ << k₋₁).

Turnover Number (k_cat): k_cat = V_max / [E]_total; represents maximum number of substrate molecules converted to product per enzyme active site per unit time.

Symbols: [E]_total = total enzyme concentration.

Unit: s⁻¹ or min⁻¹.

When to use: Measures catalytic efficiency of enzymes; combined with K_M gives specificity constant k_cat/K_M.

Conditions: At saturating substrate concentration.

Common mistake: Using initial enzyme concentration instead of total; confusing with K_M.

Inhibition Types (enzyme kinetics): Competitive: increases apparent K_M, V_max unchanged. Non-competitive: decreases V_max, K_M unchanged. Uncompetitive: decreases both V_max and K_M proportionally.

Symbols: Competitive inhibitor binds active site (similar to substrate); non-competitive binds elsewhere; uncompetitive binds ES complex only.

Unit: Qualitative kinetic effects.

When to use: Analyzes enzyme inhibition patterns; relevant to drug design and biochemistry in JEE.

Conditions: Measured via Lineweaver-Burk plot (1/v vs 1/[S]); different inhibitor types give distinct line intersections.

Common mistake: Mixing up competitive and non-competitive effects on K_M and V_max.

Lineweaver-Burk Plot: 1/v = (K_M/V_max)(1/[S]) + 1/V_max. Y-intercept = 1/V_max; X-intercept = -1/K_M; Slope = K_M/V_max.

Symbols: Linear transformation of Michaelis-Menten equation for graphical determination of K_M and V_max.

Unit: 1/v in L·mol⁻¹·s; 1/[S] in L·mol⁻¹.

When to use: Extracts K_M and V_max from experimental data; identifies inhibition type from plot patterns.

Conditions: Valid only over the measured concentration range; double-reciprocal plots overweight errors at low [S].

Common mistake: Reading intercepts incorrectly; not recognizing different inhibitor patterns on the plot.

Half-life (first-order): t₁/₂ = 0.693/k = ln(2)/k. For first-order reactions, t₁/₂ is independent of initial concentration.

Symbols: k = rate constant (units: time⁻¹); t₁/₂ = half-life.

Unit: Time (s, min, hr, yr depending on k).

When to use: Radioactive decay (C-14 dating), first-order kinetic problems in organic chemistry (decays, decompositions).

Conditions: Only for first-order reactions; zero-order: t₁/₂ = [A]₀/(2k); second-order: t₁/₂ = 1/(k[A]₀).

Common mistake: Using t₁/₂ = 0.693/k for wrong order; confusing half-life with mean lifetime (τ = 1/k).

Radioactive Dating (C-14): t = (1/λ) × ln(N₀/N) = (t₁/₂/0.693) × ln(N₀/N), where λ = decay constant = 0.693/t₁/₂.

Symbols: N₀ = original activity/mass of C-14; N = current activity/mass; t₁/₂ of C-14 = 5730 years.

Unit: Time in years (matching t₁/₂ units).

When to use: Determines age of organic materials (archaeological samples, fossils) using C-14 decay.

Conditions: Assumes constant atmospheric C-14/C-12 ratio; valid up to ~50,000 years.

Common mistake: Using wrong half-life value; confusing decay constant calculation.

Nuclear Reaction Notation: ^A_Z X (bombardment) → ^A'_Z' Y + particles. Conservation: sum of A (mass numbers) and Z (atomic numbers) must be equal on both sides.

Symbols: A = mass number (protons + neutrons); Z = atomic number (protons). Common particles: α (=⁴₂He), β⁻ (=⁰₋₁e), β⁺ (=⁰₊₁e), n (=¹₀n), p (=¹₁H).

Unit: Balanced equation requirement.

When to use: Balancing nuclear equations in radiochemistry; identifying unknown nuclides or particles.

Conditions: Applies to all nuclear reactions including those relevant to tracer studies in organic mechanism elucidation.

Common mistake: Forgetting to balance both A and Z simultaneously; misidentifying particle notation.
